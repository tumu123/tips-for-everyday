How to Buy Used Furniture Without Bringing Home Problems

Save Money Shopping Published October 15, 2026 10 min read
Advertisement

Used furniture is one of the largest genuinely available discounts in household spending. A solid wood dresser that costs $900 new typically sells for $150–300 secondhand — and it will likely outlast a $400 new particleboard version.

But the discount comes with a short list of things that can go badly wrong. Three categories of problems matter: pests, safety standards, and hidden structural damage. All three are checkable on site if you know where to look.

Never Buy These Used

This list is short, and it’s not negotiable.

ItemWhy not
Car seats / booster seatsUnknown crash history, plastic degrades, expiration dates
CribsSafety standards have changed substantially; drop-side and spacing issues
Items under active recallCheck before buying, not after
Corded window coveringsStrangulation hazard; modern versions are cordless
Mattresses (generally)Bed bugs, allergens, hygiene — see below
Upholstered items with any sign of pestsTreatment is difficult and expensive
Anything you can’t inspect properlyDark photos, “sold as seen” with no viewing

On mattresses specifically: the standard advice is to buy new, and it’s sound — mattresses are the single most common vector for bringing bed bugs home, and you cannot adequately inspect the interior. If budget forces a used mattress, the minimum conditions are: you can inspect it in daylight in the owner’s home, there is no sign of pests anywhere in that home, it has a removable washable cover, and it’s from a household you know. Even then, encasement and thorough vacuuming are required. For most people, a cheaper new mattress is the better purchase.

Check recalls before buying: look up the manufacturer and model. This takes a minute and applies to baby equipment, dressers with tip-over risk, and many other categories.

What’s Genuinely Worth Buying Used

High-value secondhand categories share one trait: the material or construction holds up.

CategoryWhy it works used
Solid wood dressers, tables, bookcasesOften decades old and still structurally sound
Dining chairs and tablesEasy to inspect; joints are visible
Metal shelving and filing cabinetsDurable; minimal failure modes
Office furnitureHigh new cost, heavy depreciation
Outdoor furniture (metal, teak)Built to weather
Vintage pieces with real joineryOften better built than current equivalents

What to be cautious about:

CategoryThe concern
Particleboard / laminate furnitureWeakens with moisture; cannot be repaired; often fails on moving
Upholstered seatingPest risk; foam degrades invisibly
Beds and mattressesPest and hygiene risk
Large sectionalsHard to clean, hard to move, hard to resell
Anything with a persistent odorSmoke and pet odors are extremely difficult to remove

The On-Site Inspection

Spend five minutes. Do it in good light, and do it before you commit.

Structural checks:

  1. The wobble test. Push the piece from each corner. Movement means loose joints. Some looseness is fixable; widespread joint failure is not.
  2. Lift it, don’t just look. Weight is a rough proxy for material quality. Solid wood is heavy; hollow-core laminate is not.
  3. Open every drawer. They should slide without catching. Look at the corner joints — dovetail joints indicate quality; stapled or glued butt joints indicate particleboard.
  4. Check the back panel. Solid wood furniture has a substantial back. If the back is thin cardboard-like sheet, the whole piece is low-grade.
  5. Look underneath. Water damage, swelling, and pest evidence collect there.

Surface checks:

  1. Smell it. Open the drawers and smell the interior. Musty means mildew; heavy fragrance may be masking something.
  2. Check for water rings, swelling, and veneer lifting — moisture damage that will not repair.
  3. Run your hand along edges for splintering, chips, and lifting veneer.

Telling Solid Wood From Particleboard

This single skill determines whether a used piece is worth buying.

TestSolid wood / qualityParticleboard / veneer
WeightHeavy for its sizeSurprisingly light
Drawer jointsDovetail or doweledStapled, glued, or notched
Edge feelContinuous grain wraps aroundThin banding; seam visible
Back panelSolid or thick plywoodThin fiberboard, often stapled
UndersideUnfinished but solid woodCoated pressed board
Water damage effectCan be sanded and refinishedSwells permanently
Screw holdingScrews grip and can be re-tightenedStrips out; won’t hold again

The drawer test is the fastest. Pull a drawer out and look at the front corner. Dovetailed interlocking joints mean the piece was built to last. Staples mean it wasn’t.

Check the underside of the top surface. Veneer over particleboard has a visible seam at the edge and often a slight lip you can feel.

Checking for Bed Bugs

This is the check people skip, and the one with the largest downside.

Bring a flashlight and a phone. You’re looking for very small evidence.

Where to look:

  • Seams and piping on upholstery — especially along the underside and back
  • Under cushions and in the crevices where cushions meet the frame
  • Screw holes and joints in wooden furniture
  • Any crack or gap in wooden frames
  • Under labels, tags, and dust covers
  • Inside drawers, particularly the corners and under the bottom panel

What to look for:

SignAppearance
Live insectsFlat, oval, reddish-brown, about the size of an apple seed
Dark spotsSmall black or rust-colored stains — digested blood
Eggs and shellsTiny pale, translucent, about 1 mm
Shed skinsLight brown, translucent, insect-shaped
Sweet musty odorNoticeable in heavy infestations

If you find any of these: walk away. Do not negotiate, do not try to treat it yourself.

Additional precautions: inspect the piece outdoors or in a garage before bringing it inside. Ask where it came from — a piece from a home with a known issue is an automatic no.

How to Evaluate the Price

Research the new equivalent first. Look up a comparable new piece and use it as the anchor.

Typical depreciation:

ConditionTypical share of new price
Like new, current model50–70%
Good condition, normal wear30–50%
Solid wood, older, cosmetic wear20–40%
Needs refinishing or repair10–25%
Antique, good conditionHighly variable

What reduces value: water damage, structural looseness, missing hardware, persistent odors, pest evidence, particleboard construction.

What adds value: solid wood, dovetail joinery, original hardware, recognizable maker, metal construction.

Negotiation that works: point out specific defects rather than asking for a vague discount. “The drawer runners are bent and the veneer is lifting here — would you take $X?” is far more effective than “Can you do better?”

Before It Comes Inside

Plan the transport first. Measure the piece, the doorways, the stairwells, and your vehicle. Most furniture damage happens during moving, not during use.

Then treat it:

  1. Vacuum thoroughly — every surface, every crevice, every drawer interior. Use a crevice tool.
  2. Wipe all surfaces with an appropriate cleaner for the material.
  3. Clean inside drawers and cabinets — these are rarely cleaned by previous owners.
  4. Leave it isolated in a garage or spare room for a few days if you have any doubt at all.
  5. Check again after a week for any signs of pests.

For wooden pieces, a paste wax or appropriate wood conditioner after cleaning protects the finish and reduces moisture absorption.

For upholstered items, steam cleaning helps but does not reliably eliminate a pest problem. If you’re unsure about an upholstered piece, don’t buy it.

Why This Works

Older furniture was frequently built better. Dovetail joinery, solid wood, and replaceable hardware were standard at price points where laminated particleboard is standard today. The used market therefore offers construction quality that is genuinely hard to buy new at the same price.

The discount is largest on items with long service lives. Furniture depreciates quickly but functions for decades, so a ten-year-old solid wood piece often has most of its useful life remaining at a fraction of the cost.

The risks are concentrated and identifiable. Pests concentrate in predictable locations — seams, joints, undersides. Safety-standard changes affect a small, specific set of products. Structural damage is visible under load. All three can be checked in minutes, which is what makes the used market workable rather than a gamble.

A short exclusion list removes most of the downside. The catastrophic outcomes are concentrated in a handful of categories — car seats, cribs, recalled items, mattresses, and pest-affected upholstery. Avoid those and the remaining risk is manageable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to buy a used couch?

It's the highest-risk upholstered category. Inspect it thoroughly with a flashlight, check the seller's home environment, and be willing to walk away.

Can bed bugs survive in wooden furniture?

Yes — in joints, screw holes, and cracks. Wood gets a reputation for safety because it lacks fabric seams, but it is not risk-free.

How do I know if furniture is solid wood?

Check the drawer joints for dovetails, examine the edges for continuous grain, and look at the back panel. All three together are reliable.

What should I never buy used?

Car seats, cribs, recalled products, corded window coverings, and — except under narrow conditions — mattresses.

How much should I offer?

Start from the new equivalent price, then apply depreciation for condition and subtract for specific defects you can point to.

How do I clean used furniture before using it?

Vacuum thoroughly with a crevice tool, wipe all surfaces with a material-appropriate cleaner, clean drawer and cabinet interiors, and isolate it for a few days.

Is used office furniture a good deal?

Usually yes. Commercial-grade pieces are built for heavy use and depreciate steeply.

Do smoke odors come out of used furniture?

Rarely, and almost never from upholstery or particleboard. Treat a persistent odor as a permanent defect.
