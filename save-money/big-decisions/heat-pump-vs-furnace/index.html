Heat Pump vs Furnace: The Choice That Actually Matters

Save Money Big Decisions Published October 24, 2026 12 min read

Replacing a heating system is a $6,000 to $15,000 decision that locks in your operating cost for fifteen years, and most of the public argument about it is conducted in the wrong units. Heat pumps are described as efficient (they are), furnaces as reliable (they are), and the discussion stops there. None of that tells you which one is cheaper to run in your house, which is the only question that matters once the check is written.

The right comparison is cost per million BTU of delivered heat, computed from your actual fuel prices. It is a five-minute calculation, it produces a different answer in different parts of the country, and it is the argument that survives contact with a real heating season rather than a manufacturer’s brochure.

Efficiency Ratings Are Not Comparable Across Fuels

A gas furnace is rated by AFUE — annual fuel utilization efficiency — as a percentage. A 96% AFUE furnace turns 96 cents of every dollar of gas into heat in the house, and loses 4 cents up the flue. That is close to the practical ceiling, because you cannot recover much more from combustion.

A heat pump is rated by HSPF2 (heating seasonal performance factor, the 2023 replacement for HSPF) or by COP. Both are ratios of heat delivered to energy consumed, and they can exceed 1 because a heat pump moves heat rather than creating it. An HSPF2 of 9 corresponds to a seasonal COP of roughly 2.6; a good cold-climate model reaches HSPF2 10 to 11.

Comparing 96% to “300%” is the source of a great deal of confusion, because the two numbers are on different scales and neither converts directly to money. The conversion is through fuel price.

The Only Comparison That Counts: Dollars per Million BTU

One million BTU is roughly the heat content of 10 therms of natural gas, 1.1 gallons of heating oil, 11 gallons of propane, or 293 kWh of electricity — before accounting for how efficiently each is turned into delivered heat.

Formula: cost per million BTU = fuel price ÷ (efficiency × energy content).

Here is where that lands at recent US average prices:

FuelPriceEfficiencyCost per million BTU delivered
Natural gas$1.20 per therm95% AFUE furnace$12.60
Natural gas$1.20 per therm80% AFUE furnace$15.00
Propane$2.50 per gallon90%$30.40
Heating oil$4.00 per gallon85%$34.10
Electricity, resistance$0.17 per kWh100%$49.80

Electricity, heat pump$0.17 per kWhCOP 2.0 (cold)$24.90
Electricity, heat pump$0.12 per kWhCOP 3.0$11.70

Read that table carefully, because it contains the entire argument:

  • Against natural gas at $1.20 a therm, a heat pump at COP 3 and $0.17 electricity loses — $16.60 against $12.60. About 30% more to run.
  • Against propane and oil, the heat pump wins by roughly half. This is not close, and it is why heat pumps dominate in rural areas with no gas main.
  • At $0.12 per kWh, the heat pump beats natural gas. The crossover for a COP 3 heat pump against $1.20 gas is about $0.13 per kWh.
  • Resistance electric heat is the worst option on the list by a wide margin, and it is what a heat pump falls back to when it cannot keep up. That fallback is the thing to understand before you buy.

The crossover formula, if you want to run it with your own numbers: break-even electricity price = gas price per therm × COP × 0.03412. At $1.20 gas and COP 3, that is $0.123/kWh.

Where the Heat Pump Loses Its Advantage: Cold

COP is not a constant. It falls as outdoor temperature falls, because there is less heat to move. A typical system holds a COP near 3 at 47°F, around 2 at 17°F, and closer to 1.5 to 1.8 at 5°F. Cold-climate models do better, holding 2 or above at 5°F, but none of them hold 3.

Two consequences:

The seasonal average is not the rated number. An HSPF2 rating is a whole-season average for a climate region. If you live where January averages 20°F, your real seasonal COP may be 2.2 rather than 3, which moves the cost from $16.60 to $22.60 per million BTU — enough to flip the answer against cheap gas.

The balance point decides whether you ever see the bad numbers. The balance point is the outdoor temperature at which the heat pump’s capacity exactly matches the house’s heat loss. Below it, something has to make up the difference. On a standard system, that something is electric resistance auxiliary heat — and at a COP of 1, it costs $49.80 per million BTU. A house that spends 15% of its heating hours on aux heat pays a real penalty.

How to keep that under control:

  • Size the heat pump for the load, and set the balance point properly. Many installers leave the aux heat lockout at the factory default, which brings on the strips earlier than necessary. Ask for the lockout temperature in the commissioning report.
  • Choose a cold-climate model if you are in a cold zone. Look for a rated capacity at 5°F that is 70% or more of rated capacity at 47°F, and ask to see the spec sheet.
  • Fix the envelope first. Air sealing and attic insulation reduce the load, which lowers the balance point and shrinks the system you need. This is almost always cheaper per BTU saved than another half ton of capacity.

Install Cost, Which Is Where the Decision Usually Gets Made

Installed prices vary by region and by how much ductwork is involved, but the ranges are stable enough to plan around:

OptionTypical installed cost
Gas furnace, 80% AFUE$3,000 to $5,500
Gas furnace, 95%+ AFUE$4,500 to $8,000
Air-source heat pump, standard$5,500 to $10,000
Air-source heat pump, cold-climate$8,000 to $14,000
Ductless mini-split, single zone$3,000 to $6,000
Dual-fuel (heat pump + furnace)$9,000 to $15,000

Two costs that surprise people:

Electrical work. A heat pump needs a 240V circuit, and a cold-climate unit with 10 to 15 kW of backup strips can need 60 to 100 amps of capacity. If the panel is full, add $1,500 to $4,000 for a service upgrade. Ask for a load calculation before signing, not after.

Ductwork. A heat pump moves less air at a higher temperature differential than a furnace in heating mode, and marginal ducts that worked with a furnace can leave rooms cold. Duct sealing and a couple of new returns can add $1,000 to $3,000.

Subtract incentives from the gross number, not the other way around, and get the incentive amounts in writing with the program name. The federal tax credit that used to cover qualified heat pumps has expired for homeowner installations placed in service after 2025; state and utility rebates still exist in many areas and vary enormously. Confirm what is currently available before comparing quotes, because a quote built around an expired credit is not a quote.

Hybrid Systems: The Option Most People Should Seriously Consider

A dual-fuel system pairs a heat pump with a gas furnace and switches between them based on outdoor temperature. Above the switchover point it runs the heat pump; below it, the furnace. You get the heat pump’s efficiency in mild weather and the furnace’s capacity and low running cost in a cold snap.

Where it wins: cold climates with cheap natural gas, where a heat pump alone would spend too many hours on aux heat. Where it is pointless: mild climates (the furnace never runs and you paid for it), and houses with no gas service (the whole point is the cheap fuel).

The switchover temperature should be set from your actual fuel prices, not left at a default. With the numbers above — $1.20 gas, $0.17 electricity — the economic switchover is where heat pump COP falls below about 1.9, which is somewhere around 20°F to 25°F for a standard unit. Many installers set it at 35°F to 40°F out of caution, which means the furnace runs far more than it needs to.

Operating Cost Over a Real Winter

A house needing 60 million BTU of delivered heat over a season:

  • 95% gas furnace at $1.20/therm: 60 × $12.60 = $756
  • Heat pump, seasonal COP 2.8 at $0.17/kWh: 60 × $17.80 = $1,068
  • Heat pump, seasonal COP 2.8 at $0.12/kWh: 60 × $12.50 = $750
  • Propane at 90% and $2.50/gal: 60 × $30.40 = $1,824
  • Oil at 85% and $4.00/gal: 60 × $34.10 = $2,046

The pattern is consistent with the per-unit table (the method is the same one behind saving on heating costs this winter): gas versus heat pump is close and price-dependent; heat pump versus propane or oil is a rout. If you are currently heating with propane or oil, the heat pump usually pays back its extra install cost in five to eight years on fuel alone. If you have cheap natural gas and expensive electricity, the heat pump is a comfort and cooling decision rather than a savings one — and that is fine, as long as you decide it knowing which one it is.

Lifespan, Maintenance, and the Things That Fail

Both systems last 15 to 20 years with maintenance. The failure modes differ:

  • Furnaces need annual cleaning and a combustion check, and the heat exchanger is the expensive failure. A cracked heat exchanger is also a carbon monoxide risk, which is why the annual inspection matters.
  • Heat pumps need the outdoor coil kept clear, the filter changed, and refrigerant circuits left alone. The common failures are the reversing valve, the capacitor, and the compressor — and the compressor is a $1,500 to $3,000 repair.
  • Refrigerant transition. New residential equipment has been moving to A2L refrigerants (R-454B and R-32) following the phase-down of R-410A. This is a good thing environmentally and a small complication practically: A2L systems require slightly different handling, and prices for early-transition equipment were higher. Ask what refrigerant the unit uses and whether your contractor is equipped for it.
  • Heat pumps also cool. If you are comparing against a furnace plus a separate air conditioner, remember that the heat pump replaces the AC too. A furnace at $6,000 plus a $4,000 AC is $10,000, which is the same ballpark as a heat pump alone. This comparison is omitted from most furnace quotes and it is often the deciding fact.

How to Decide in Ten Minutes

Work down this list:

  1. What do you heat with now? Propane or oil: heat pump almost certainly wins. Natural gas: continue.
  2. What is your electricity price, all-in, per kWh? Below $0.13: heat pump likely wins. Above $0.18: gas likely wins unless gas is expensive.
  3. What is your winter design temperature? Above 25°F: standard heat pump is fine. Below 15°F: cold-climate model or dual fuel.
  4. Are you also replacing an air conditioner? If yes, the heat pump’s cost advantage improves by the price of the AC you no longer buy.
  5. Is the electrical panel adequate? If not, add the upgrade cost.
  6. How long will you stay? Under five years, the operating savings may not repay the install premium; comfort and cooling may still justify it.
  7. What incentives are actually available today? Get them in writing, per program.

What NOT to Do

  • Do not compare AFUE to HSPF2. Compare dollars per million BTU.
  • Do not size a heat pump by the old furnace’s output. Get a manual J load calculation; oversized heat pumps short-cycle, dehumidify poorly, and cost more to run.
  • Do not skip the envelope work. Air sealing and insulation reduce the load, which makes whatever you install cheaper to run and possibly smaller.
  • Do not leave the aux heat lockout at the default. It is the difference between a good winter bill and a shocking one.
  • Do not accept a quote without a model number and a commissioning report. “A 3-ton heat pump” is not a specification.
  • Do not install a heat pump with a failing roof over the outdoor unit, or in a location where snow burial is likely without a stand.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a heat pump keep my house warm at 0°F?

A cold-climate model will, at reduced capacity, with aux heat making up the difference. A standard model will too, but it may spend significant time on resistance heat, which is expensive. The honest answer depends on your design temperature and the model's rated capacity at 5°F.

Why is my heat pump bill higher than my old gas bill?

Usually one of three things: electricity is expensive relative to gas, the aux heat is running more than it should, or the system is oversized or the ducts leak. Check the aux heat runtime first — smart thermostats report it. If the bill moved suddenly rather than being consistently higher, [diagnose the jump first](/save-money/bills/why-electric-bill-jumped/), because the cause may be somewhere else entirely.

Is a heat pump cheaper to run than a 96% furnace?

At $0.17/kWh and $1.20/therm, no — it is roughly 30% more. At $0.12/kWh, yes. The answer is entirely in the two prices.

How long does a heat pump last?

15 to 20 years with annual maintenance, similar to a furnace, though compressor failures shorten the tail.

Do I need a backup furnace?

Only in cold climates where the heat pump's capacity at design temperature is marginal. Below 20°F design temperature, dual fuel is worth pricing.

What about ductless mini-splits?

Excellent for houses without ducts, additions, and single rooms. A multi-zone mini-split can heat a whole house, but the per-zone cost adds up and the aesthetics are a real consideration.

Are heat pumps worse for the environment?

They use less primary energy than any combustion system at typical grid carbon intensity, and considerably less on a clean grid. That is a real advantage, but it is a separate question from your bill.
