What It Actually Costs to Hire Out vs Do It Yourself

Save Money Big Decisions Published October 24, 2026 12 min read

“Do it yourself and save money” is true often enough to be a cliché and wrong often enough to be expensive. The reason is that both sides of the comparison are usually calculated badly: the DIY cost is counted as materials only, ignoring tools, waste, permits, and the value of a weekend, while the contractor price is treated as pure profit rather than labor, overhead, insurance, warranty, and risk transfer.

The honest comparison is one division: money saved ÷ hours spent = your effective hourly rate. If that number is above what your time is worth to you, do it yourself. If it is below, pay someone. Everything else — pride, learning, control — is a legitimate consideration, but it belongs outside the arithmetic, not disguised as one.

The DIY Side, Priced Properly

Most DIY cost estimates consist of the materials, which is the smallest part of the real number.

Tools. A job that needs a $180 wet saw, a $90 oscillating multi-tool, or a $250 rotary hammer is not a $30 job. The honest treatment depends on reuse: a drill you will use for a decade is a capital purchase; a tile saw for one bathroom is a $60 rental or a $180 tool you will sell for $90. Count the rental, or count half the purchase price if you expect to keep it and use it twice.

Waste and error. Materials estimates assume no mistakes. Reality: 10% overage on tile and flooring, one extra sheet of drywall, a second length of pipe because the first was cut short, a $60 cartridge you bought twice because you got the model wrong. Add 10% to 20% to any materials estimate for a first attempt at a job.

Permits and inspection. Most jurisdictions require permits for electrical work beyond replacing a fixture, plumbing beyond a fixture swap, structural changes, gas work, and anything involving a new circuit. A permit is $50 to $500, and the inspection is a hard stop: work that fails inspection has to be redone. Skipping the permit saves money until you sell the house and the buyer’s inspector finds unpermitted work — which is a price negotiation at best and a demolition order at worst.

Time, including the time you did not plan for. The trip to the hardware store is part of the job. So is the second trip, which happens on almost every first-time job. So is the research, the drying time between coats, the curing time for mortar, and the cleanup. Multiply your estimate by 1.5 for a job you have not done before.

Disposal. Old carpet, a water heater, demolition debris, an old toilet. A dump run is an hour and $40 to $80; a rented dumpster is $300 to $600.

Risk. This is the hidden line item that decides most close calls. If you do the job wrong, the cost is not the job — it is the water damage from a badly soldered joint, the fire from an undersized wire, the roof leak from a badly flashed chimney. A 5% chance of a $6,000 failure adds $300 of expected cost to a DIY job, which wipes out the savings on plenty of mid-size projects.

The Pro Side, Priced Honestly

Contractors are not charging you the tradesperson’s hourly wage. The bill covers:

Labor at a loaded rate. A plumber billing $120 an hour is paying the plumber $35 to $45, plus payroll taxes, insurance, a truck, tools, dispatch, and the office. The loaded rate is not a markup on greed; it is what the business costs.

Material markup. Commonly 10% to 30% over what you would pay at a home center, sometimes more on specialty items. This is partly profit and partly the cost of stocking, warrantying, and returning materials. You can often buy the fixtures yourself and have the contractor install them — ask first, because some will not warranty owner-supplied materials.

Trip and minimum charges. Many trades have a minimum call-out of $95 to $200 that covers the first hour, and a trip charge on top. This is why a 15-minute job costs $180 and why bundling several small jobs into one visit is the single best way to reduce contractor costs.

Disposal, permits, and inspection coordination. Usually itemized, sometimes bundled into the price.

Warranty and liability. A licensed contractor carries insurance and warranties the work, which means a failure is their problem rather than yours. That transfer is a real product you are buying, and it is worth more on some jobs than others.

Change orders. The quoted price assumes a defined scope. What is behind the wall is unknown until the wall is open, and the price changes when it is. Budget a 10% to 20% contingency on any job that opens a wall, a floor, or a ceiling.

Read the pattern rather than the specific numbers:

  • Small fixture swaps produce the highest effective rates — $100 to $200 an hour for an hour of work, because the contractor’s price has to cover a trip and a minimum. These are the clearest DIY wins.
  • Large projects produce lower effective rates — $30 to $80 an hour — because the pro’s price is dominated by labor hours, not by fixed charges. These are the ones where DIY is a job rather than a task.
  • Anything requiring a permit and inspection narrows the gap further once the permit, the inspection, and the risk of failing it are counted.
  • A few jobs are never DIY regardless of the rate: gas lines, torsion springs, anything involving a service panel upgrade, and structural work. Not because of the arithmetic, but because failure is fatal or catastrophic. The full breakdown by consequence rather than difficulty is in DIY or call a pro.

Compare the rate against your own after-tax hourly earnings. If you take a day of unpaid leave to save $250, you have not saved $250 — you have traded a day of income for a smaller amount. If you use a Saturday you would otherwise spend on something you do not value much, the comparison is different and the DIY job may well be worth it.

When DIY Genuinely Wins

  • You have done it before. The second bathroom you tile takes half the time and produces no waste. Skill is the largest variable in the entire calculation.
  • The tools are reusable. If you will use the saw, the nail gun, or the pressure washer for years, the tool cost amortizes to near zero.
  • The job is small and the call-out is large. Any task under two hours against a $150 minimum is usually a DIY win.
  • You enjoy it. A real consideration, and one that does not need to be justified. Just do not book it as a financial saving.
  • The work is invisible and forgiving. Painting a closet, patching a wall in a basement, replacing a toilet seat, caulking a tub: errors are cheap and correctable.

When the Pro Is Cheaper in Real Terms

  • Permits and code apply. Electrical circuits, gas, structural, plumbing alterations, and anything a future buyer’s inspector will ask about.
  • Warranty matters. A roof, a water heater, a furnace: the warranty on the work is often worth more than the labor difference — and for machines, the repair decision has its own arithmetic, covered in repair or replace an appliance.
  • The job requires specialty equipment. Sewer camera, concrete saw, refrigerant recovery, scaffolding.
  • The failure mode is expensive. Water, gas, electricity, and anything above a living space.
  • You are on a deadline. A pro finishes in two days what takes you three weekends, and the carrying cost of an unfinished project is real — a kitchen out of action for a month is a month of takeaway.
  • The job must be invisible. Drywall finishing, tile setting, and trim work are judged by the eye, and a first attempt looks like a first attempt.

The Costs Both Sides Forget

On the DIY side: the tools you buy and never use again, the materials you over-order, the permit you skipped that surfaces at resale, the injury (a ladder fall is the most common home injury and the most expensive), and the opportunity cost of the weekends.

On the pro side: the deposit. Never pay more than 10% to 30% up front, never pay in full before the work is complete, and never pay cash without a receipt. A large deposit before materials arrive is the signature of a contractor with a cash flow problem, and it is how homeowners lose money. Ask for a lien waiver from any subcontractor or supplier on a large job, because in many states an unpaid supplier can place a lien on your house.

On both sides: the change order. On a DIY job it is your own scope creep; on a pro job it is an invoice you did not expect. Get changes in writing, with a price, before the work happens.

A Five-Question Test

Before you decide, answer these:

  1. Does it need a permit? If yes, the pro usually wins on total cost once inspection risk is counted.
  2. What happens if I get it wrong? Water, fire, structural, or gas: hire it out.
  3. How many hours, honestly? Take your estimate and multiply by 1.5.
  4. What is the effective hourly rate? If it is below your after-tax wage, you are buying a hobby, not saving money.
  5. Would I rather have the weekend? A legitimate answer. Just make it consciously.

What NOT to Do

  • Do not count materials as the whole DIY cost. Tools, waste, permits, disposal, and time are the rest of it.
  • Do not compare a pro’s price to your materials cost and conclude the pro is a rip-off. You are comparing two different things.
  • Do not attempt gas, panel upgrades, or garage door springs. The arithmetic does not matter.
  • Do not pay a large deposit up front, and do not pay the balance before you have inspected the work.
  • Do not skip the permit to save $200 on work that will be inspected at resale.
  • Do not start a job that leaves the house uninhabitable if you run out of time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is DIY always cheaper?

For small, repeatable, low-risk jobs, almost always. For jobs with permits, specialty tools, or expensive failure modes, frequently not.

How much does a contractor mark up materials?

Commonly 10% to 30% over retail. On a $3,000 materials package that is $300 to $900, which is a legitimate part of the comparison but rarely the deciding factor.

What is a fair hourly rate for a handyman?

$50 to $100 an hour in most US markets, with a one-hour or two-hour minimum. Specialists — electricians, plumbers, HVAC — bill $90 to $175 an hour in most metros.

Should I buy tools or rent them?

Rent anything you will use once or twice, and buy anything you will use every year. A $180 tool used three times is a $60 rental; a $180 tool used for a decade is a bargain.

Can I supply my own materials to a contractor?

Often yes, but ask first, and expect the warranty to exclude them. The saving is usually the markup, which is 10% to 30% — not nothing, but not the main cost.

How much should I budget for surprises?

10% to 20% on any job that opens a wall, floor, or ceiling. On older houses, budget the higher end.

Is it worth hiring out cleaning and lawn care?

That is a different comparison — it is priced per visit rather than per job, and the effective hourly rate of doing it yourself is genuinely low.
