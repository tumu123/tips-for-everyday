Solar Panels: What the Payback Actually Looks Like

Save Money Big Decisions Published October 24, 2026 12 min read

Solar sales presentations have a habit of quoting a 25-year savings number that runs to six figures. It is not a lie exactly, but it is a marketing construction: it assumes retail electricity prices rise every year for a quarter century, assumes the system produces at its rated output forever, and ignores the cost of the inverter replacement in year 12 to 15 and the possibility that you sell the house in year 7.

The number that matters is simpler and much less impressive: the year the cumulative bill savings exceed what you paid. Everything else — the 25-year figure, the “cash flow positive in month one,” the environmental argument — is downstream of that number, and if the payback is longer than you plan to stay in the house, the deal is not a deal no matter what the brochure says.

The Calculation, Reduced to Four Numbers

Every honest solar payback calculation is these four numbers and one division:

  1. Net cost — what you pay after incentives and before financing.
  2. Annual production in kilowatt-hours.
  3. The value of each kilowatt-hour to you, which is not the same as the retail rate.
  4. Annual bill savings = production × value, minus charges that remain on the bill anyway.

Then: payback in years = net cost ÷ annual savings.

Each of those four has a way of being quietly overstated. Look at them in order.

Number 1: Net Cost, Not Sticker Price

US residential solar runs roughly $2.50 to $3.40 per installed watt before incentives, all-in, for a typical rooftop system. An 8 kW system therefore lands around $20,000 to $27,000 before any incentive.

Where the incentive picture stands matters a great deal right now. The federal residential clean energy credit (Section 25D) that paid 30% of the cost of a homeowner-owned system has closed to systems placed in service after 2025, subject to transition rules for projects that had already started. That changes the arithmetic substantially: the same $24,000 system that used to net down to about $16,800 is now a $24,000 system unless a state, utility, or local program contributes. Confirm your own eligibility with the installer in writing and with your state energy office, because this is exactly the kind of rule that changes and exactly the kind that salespeople describe optimistically.

What still exists in many states: rebates from state energy programs, Solar Renewable Energy Certificates (SRECs) in a handful of states, property tax exemptions, and utility-specific programs. And what still exists nationally: the federal credit for systems installed on a business or investment property, which is a different code section with different rules — do not assume it applies to your roof because your installer mentions it.

Ask for the incentive line items individually, by name and amount, with the source. “Up to $9,000 in incentives” is not a number; it is a headline.

Number 2: Production, Which Depends on Where You Are

Production is quoted in kilowatt-hours per year and is usually estimated with a tool like PVWatts, which is publicly available and worth running yourself.

A rough national range is 1,200 to 1,600 kWh per installed kilowatt per year, with the higher end in the Southwest and the lower end in the cloudy Northeast and Pacific Northwest. An 8 kW system in a middling location produces around 11,000 to 12,000 kWh a year.

Three things erode that number, and the quote usually does not mention them:

Shade. A single tree branch across a panel’s cell string can cut that panel’s output disproportionately, and in older string-inverter systems, drag down the panels wired with it. Ask for the shade report — most installers run one — and look at the specific hours it projects losses.

Orientation and pitch. A south-facing roof at 30 to 40 degrees is the reference case. West-facing loses a few percent a year but often produces at better times of day; north-facing is a bad idea in most of the country.

Age. Panels degrade around 0.5% a year, and most warranties guarantee 80% to 87% of rated output at year 25. That is a mild effect, but it does mean year 25 production is not year 1 production.

Number 3: The Value of a Kilowatt-Hour Is Where Quotes Break

This is the number that decides whether solar works, and the one most affected by policy changes in the last few years.

Behind-the-meter self-consumption. A kilowatt-hour you produce and use in your own house avoids the full retail rate — generation, transmission, distribution, and taxes. At a $0.17/kWh all-in rate, that is $0.17 saved. This is the most valuable solar kWh there is, and it is why the ratio of production to daytime consumption matters so much.

Net metering at full retail. Historically, utilities credited exports at the full retail rate, so a kWh exported at noon was worth the same as one used at 7 pm. Several states have moved away from this. Where full retail net metering survives, a kWh is worth the full rate however it is used.

Export at avoided cost. Under newer tariffs, exports are compensated at a wholesale or avoided-cost rate that can be a fraction of retail — often $0.03 to $0.08/kWh. In that structure, only the electricity you consume on site is worth full retail, and exports are worth little.

Time-of-use valuation. Some newer tariffs pay more for exports during evening peaks and almost nothing at midday, which is exactly when solar produces. This is where a battery starts to have an economic case, and also where a solar-only system’s payback lengthens.

Fixed charges that never go away. Even with solar covering 100% of usage, you still pay the monthly service charge, and increasingly a separate grid access or minimum bill charge. Those are $10 to $25 a month that no amount of production removes.

The practical consequence: the same system can pay back in 8 years or 20 depending entirely on the export rules in your state and how much of your production you use at home. A quote that values every kWh at the retail rate is assuming full retail net metering. Check whether that is still true where you live, and get the tariff name in writing.

Still not the 5-year payback the brochure implied, and now assume the 30% credit had applied: net cost $16,800, payback 8.8 years. That is a good deal in most of the country — which is exactly why the credit’s expiry matters and why the sales pitch built around it may not survive its removal.

Two refinements worth making: electricity rates rise, which shortens payback in nominal terms, and you should model a rise of 2% to 3% a year rather than the 5% to 6% quotes often use. And the inverter replacement — $1,500 to $3,000 in year 12 to 15 for a string inverter — belongs in the model as a cost, not an afterthought.

Lease and PPA: Lower Upfront, Different Math

A solar lease or power purchase agreement puts a third party’s system on your roof. You pay a monthly amount, usually with an escalator of 1% to 3% a year, and you do not own the system.

What the marketing says: no money down, pay less than the utility from day one.

What is actually true:

  • You do not get the tax credit. The third party does. That is the entire economic basis of the lease.
  • The escalator can outrun the utility rate. A 2.9% annual escalator against a utility that raises rates 2% a year ends up costing more, typically in year 10 to 15.
  • Selling the house becomes harder. The buyer must qualify for and accept the lease, or you must buy it out. This has killed deals, and it is the single biggest practical objection.
  • Removing the system at the end of the term is sometimes your cost, depending on the contract.
  • Roof work is complicated. If the roof needs replacing, the system has to come off — who pays depends on the contract, and leases are usually worse on this than ownership.

A lease can make sense for a household that cannot use the tax credit, plans to stay 20 years, and has a new roof. It is a financing product, not a discount, and it should be compared against a home equity loan on an owned system, which usually wins on total cost.

What Changes the Math More Than the Panel Brand

Ranked by how much they move the payback:

  1. The export tariff. Full retail net metering versus avoided cost is a larger factor than the price per watt.
  2. How much production you consume on site. Shifting laundry, dishwashing, and EV charging into daylight hours is free and can move several percentage points.
  3. Roof age. A roof with 8 years left means paying to remove and reinstall the array. Replace the roof first, or do both at once.
  4. Your electricity rate. Solar is worth more in Hawaii and California than in states with $0.10/kWh power — and if the rate has moved recently, work out why the bill changed before modelling 25 years of it.
  5. Financing cost. A 7% loan over 12 years adds several thousand dollars of interest. Cash beats a loan beats a lease, in that order, if you have the cash.
  6. The inverter type. Microinverters and DC optimizers cost more up front and handle shade better; string inverters are cheaper and simpler. Neither changes payback as much as the tariff does.

The Questions That Expose a Weak Quote

Ask these and watch how they are answered:

  • “What is the exact tariff name and export rate you modeled?” If they cannot name it, the savings are theoretical.
  • “What percentage of production did you assume I use on site, and why?” A 100% assumption is a red flag.
  • “What electricity price escalation did you use?” Above 3% is optimistic.
  • “Does the proposal include an inverter replacement?” If not, the 25-year number is incomplete.
  • “What is my production estimate from PVWatts, and can I see the shade report?”
  • “Is the federal credit included, and under which code section?” This is the question that separates a current quote from a copied one.
  • “If I sell in year 7, what happens?” For a lease, get the answer in writing.

What NOT to Do

  • Do not sign at the kitchen table on the first visit. Solar is not urgent, and the “price expires Friday” line is a sales tactic.
  • Do not accept a 25-year savings number as the decision metric. Payback, or the internal rate of return, or nothing.
  • Do not put panels on a roof you will replace within 10 years.
  • Do not lease if you plan to sell within 10 years. The transfer problem is real.
  • Do not finance solar on a 20-year loan and describe it as “no cost.” You are paying interest for two decades to save a bill.
  • Do not ignore efficiency first. Insulation and air sealing are usually cheaper per kWh saved than solar is per kWh generated, and they reduce the system size you need — a home energy audit is the cheapest way to decide which measures pay, and if you are also replacing heating, heat pump versus furnace changes both the load and the bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does solar take to pay for itself?

Under full retail net metering with a 30% incentive, typically 7 to 10 years. Without the federal credit and with avoided-cost export, 15 to 20 years or more. The range is that wide because policy, not hardware, drives it.

Is solar still worth it without the federal credit?

In high-rate states with strong net metering, often yes. In low-rate states with poor export compensation, often not. Run the four-number calculation above with your own tariff before you decide.

Does solar increase home value?

Studies generally find a premium for owned systems, smaller than the sales industry claims and dependent on the local market. Leased systems can reduce value because of the transfer obligation.

What happens when net metering changes?

Your payback changes. Existing systems are usually grandfathered for a period, but new systems get the new tariff. This is a reason to check the current docket, not a reason to rush.

Do I need a battery?

For backup, it is a separate decision and rarely pays back on arbitrage alone. For economics under time-of-use export rates, it can improve the return by letting you store cheap midday production for evening use — but the battery is $8,000 to $15,000, so run its own payback separately.

How long do panels last?

Most carry 25-year performance warranties and degrade about 0.5% a year. The inverter is the component that fails first, typically at 10 to 15 years.

Can I install solar myself?

Permitting, interconnection approval, and roof work usually make this impractical, and it can void the manufacturer warranty. Some states allow owner-builder installation with an electrician's sign-off; check before assuming.
