Why This Month's Electric Bill Jumped

Save Money Bills Published October 24, 2026 10 min read

A bill that jumps by 40% in one month is almost never caused by forty small things. It is usually one of five: the weather, the number of days in the billing cycle, a rate or fee change, a new appliance, or a billing error. The task is to work out which one before you start changing behaviour, because unplugging phone chargers in response to a rate increase is a waste of a weekend — and the no-cost changes that do work are a different list from the ones that sound good.

The method is arithmetic, not investigation. Compare the right two numbers in the right order and the cause narrows to one or two candidates.

Step 1: Compare Kilowatt-Hours First, Dollars Second

The dollar figure on the bill is the product of usage, rate, and fees. Comparing dollars month to month confounds all three. Compare kWh, which is the one thing that usually explains the change.

Find both bills — this month and the same month last year, plus last month — and write down:

  • kWh used for each period
  • Days in the billing cycle for each period
  • Total charged, and the split between supply (generation) and delivery (transmission, distribution, fees)
  • The rate per kWh in each of those periods

Two things to notice immediately:

If kWh is flat and the total went up, the change is rate or fees. Look for a new line item, a rider, a rate case increase, an expiring promotional rate, or a change in a time-of-use window. There is nothing to change at home; the response is a rate comparison or a call to the utility.

If kWh went up, the cause is usage or billing mechanics. Continue to Step 2.

If the number of days in the cycle changed, that alone can move a bill by 10%. Utilities read meters on routes, not on calendar months, so a 34-day cycle against a 28-day cycle is a 21% swing with no change in behaviour. Divide total kWh by days in the cycle to get kWh per day, and compare that number instead of the raw total. This is the most commonly missed explanation for a “sudden” jump, and it requires no fix at all.

Step 2: Normalize for Weather

Heating and cooling are the largest variable load in almost every house, and they are driven by weather rather than by anything you did. Degree days are the standard way to account for it.

Heating degree days (HDD) count how far the average daily temperature fell below 65°F. Cooling degree days (CDD) count how far it rose above. Your utility’s website often publishes them, and they are available from weather services for your zip code.

The comparison to make: kWh per heating degree day for this January against last January. If your kWh rose 25% and the HDD rose 25%, the house behaved exactly as it did last year and the weather did it — winter heating measures are the response, not a hunt for a faulty appliance. If kWh rose 25% and HDD rose 5%, something in the house changed.

This matters because it prevents two bad decisions: blaming yourself for a cold snap, and ignoring a real problem because the bill “looks normal for winter.”

Step 3: Identify the Load That Changed

If usage per day is up and the weather does not explain it, the cause is a load. Loads that change suddenly, ranked by how often they are the answer:

1. Electric resistance heating that was not running before. A space heater is the single most common culprit. A 1,500-watt heater running 8 hours a day for 30 days uses 360 kWh — at $0.17 that is $61 a month, from one appliance, in one room. Two of them, and the bill is explained. Heat pump auxiliary heat behaves the same way: when the strips come on, the house is heating at a COP of 1, which is the most expensive heat there is.

2. Air conditioning running longer or harder. A failing capacitor, a dirty condenser coil, a refrigerant leak, or a clogged filter all make the system run longer for the same comfort. A system that lost 15% of its capacity runs 15% longer, and it shows up as a mysterious 10% bill increase with no comfort complaint yet.

3. A water heater working harder. A failing lower element, a leaking hot water line, a dripping faucet on the hot side, or a houseguest taking long showers. Electric water heating is 15% to 20% of a typical bill, and a leak on the hot side can add a surprising amount. Check the meter with everything off — if it is still turning, something is drawing.

4. A new appliance or an old one that changed behaviour. A chest freezer in the garage, a dehumidifier (500 watts running continuously is 360 kWh a month), a pool pump, an EV charger, a hot tub, a second refrigerator. An EV at 12,000 miles a year is roughly 3,000 to 4,000 kWh — a genuine 25% to 30% increase if you were not expecting it.

5. Seasonal lighting and heating pads. Holiday lighting is real but usually small — a few hundred kWh for an elaborate display. Grow lights and space heaters in a garage or shed are not small.

6. Estimated reads and catch-up bills. A bill based on an estimated reading, followed by an actual reading, produces a jump that reflects two months of usage. Check whether the bill says “estimated.” If it does, the next one will correct it.

7. A rate change or a new rider. Look at the supply and delivery lines separately. A distribution rate increase, a storm cost recovery rider, or a change to a time-of-use window all raise the bill without touching usage.

How to Find the Load, Concretely

Method 1: The meter test. Turn everything off at the breaker panel except one circuit at a time, and read the meter’s kWh-per-hour, or watch the instantaneous kW if you have a smart meter display. The circuit that draws when you expected nothing is your answer. This takes an hour and costs nothing.

Method 2: The utility’s hourly data. Most utilities with smart meters now provide 15-minute or hourly usage in the online portal, sometimes with a Green Button download. Look for the shape: a new block of consumption at the same time every evening is behavioural; a constant elevated baseline is a device running continuously; spikes are cycling loads like a heat pump or a well pump.

Method 3: A plug-in meter. $20 to $30, borrowed from a library in some areas. Put it on the suspect appliance for a week. This is the only way to get a real number for a space heater, a dehumidifier, or an old refrigerator.

Method 4: Compare the same hour across days. A refrigerator that used to cycle twice an hour and now runs constantly is failing, and its consumption shows up as a flat 24-hour band.

When the Answer Is the Rate

If kWh is flat and the total rose, you are looking at a price change. Do three things:

  1. Check the rate schedule on the bill against last year’s. Utilities publish tariffs; a rate case increase is public information and usually gives notice.
  2. Check the riders. Storm recovery, energy efficiency program costs, and fuel adjustment clauses are separate line items that move independently of the base rate.
  3. Re-run the plan comparison. If a time-of-use plan was marginal for you last year, a rate change can flip it — in either direction.

If the increase is a base-rate change, there is no behavioural fix. The options are a different rate plan, a switch of supplier where the market is deregulated, or investment in reducing the load that the rate applies to.

The Things That Are Not the Answer

  • Phone chargers, cable boxes, and standby draw. Real, but small: standby is typically 5% to 10% of usage, and the biggest contributors are set-top boxes and game consoles, not chargers. See the vampire draw guide for which ones actually matter.
  • “The fridge door was left open.” A refrigerator recovers. It does not add 30% to a monthly bill.
  • Older appliances in general. A 20-year-old fridge matters; a 20-year-old microwave does not.
  • Neighbours stealing power, unless you are in a shared-metering situation, in which case it is worth checking whether the meter serves another unit.

What NOT to Do

  • Do not change behaviour before you know the cause. You will spend effort on the wrong thing and conclude that saving energy does not work.
  • Do not compare a 34-day cycle to a 28-day cycle and call it an increase.
  • Do not ignore an estimated reading. Call and request an actual read if two consecutive estimates appear.
  • Do not assume a smart meter is wrong. They fail, but rarely, and usually by reading zero rather than high.
  • Do not buy a whole-house monitor before checking the free hourly data your utility may already provide.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did my bill double in one month with no changes?

In order of likelihood: a cold or hot snap, a space heater or dehumidifier someone started using, a billing cycle with extra days, an estimated read catching up, or aux heat running because the heat pump lost capacity. Work the three steps in order and one of them will fit.

Can a bad meter cause a high bill?

It can, but it is rare. The test is to shut off the main breaker and confirm the meter stops. If it does not, call the utility and request a meter test, which is usually free.

What is a normal electric bill in the US?

Roughly $120 to $160 a month for an average household, but the range by state and by heating fuel is enormous — a household heating with resistance electricity in a cold state can be four times that in January.

Does the billing period length really matter?

Yes. A 34-day cycle against a 28-day cycle is a 21% difference in usage for identical behaviour, and some utilities routinely have cycles that vary by several days.

How do I know if my heat pump is using aux heat?

Most smart thermostats report auxiliary or emergency heat runtime separately. If more than about 10% to 15% of heating runtime is aux heat in a moderate climate, the system is either undersized, wrongly configured, or losing capacity.

Is it worth switching to a time-of-use plan after a rate increase?

Only after you know your hourly load shape. The question is not whether the peak rate is higher, but whether you can move enough usage off it.

What if the utility says the bill is correct and I disagree?

Request the meter data in writing, ask for a meter test, and escalate to your state's utility commission if the account remains disputed. Keep paying to avoid disconnection while the dispute is open.
