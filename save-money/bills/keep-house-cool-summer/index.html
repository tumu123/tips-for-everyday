How to Keep a House Cool in Summer Without Running the AC More

Save Money Bills Published October 15, 2026 10 min read
Advertisement

The principle that makes everything else work: keeping heat out is dramatically cheaper than removing heat that’s already inside. Once sunlight has passed through glass and warmed your sofa, floor, and walls, you have to move that energy back out with a machine — and machines cost money to run.

So the order of operations is: block first, ventilate second, cool third. Most advice reverses this, which is why it underdelivers.

Where the Heat Actually Comes From

Before spending anything, know what you’re fighting.

SourceTypical share of summer heat gain
Sun through windows25–40%
Roof and ceiling15–25%
Air infiltration (leaks)15–25%
Walls10–20%
Internal sources (appliances, cooking, people)10–20%

Windows dominate — and that’s convenient, because windows are the cheapest thing to fix.

Which windows matter: east-facing glass peaks in the morning, west-facing glass in the afternoon, and south-facing glass gets consistent exposure all day. West-facing windows are usually the worst offender, because they peak when outdoor temperature is already at its highest and the house has been absorbing heat for hours. North-facing glass contributes very little.

Priority 1: Block the Sun Before It Enters

This is the highest-return item on the list, and the distinction inside it matters more than people expect.

Outside shading beats inside shading by a wide margin. A curtain or blind on the inside stops light, but the radiant energy has already passed through the glass and been absorbed by the room’s surfaces. By then it’s heat, and you’re stuck with it. An exterior barrier — awning, shade sail, exterior shutter, or even a well-placed tree — rejects that energy before it ever enters.

The cheap high-leverage move: close curtains and blinds on sun-facing windows before the sun hits them. Closing them after the room has heated up is far less effective. If you’re out during the day, closing them in the morning is what actually helps.

Foil or cardboard in a window works in a genuine pinch and costs nothing, but it also blocks all light and looks like an emergency. Fine for a west-facing window you don’t need; not a permanent solution.

Priority 2: Manage Windows on a Schedule

Natural ventilation can do real work, but only when the outdoor air is actually cooler than indoors.

Daytime, when it’s hotter outside than in: keep windows closed, keep sun-facing coverings closed. Opening windows just lets hot air in and forces your AC to fight it.

Nighttime, when outdoor temperature drops: open windows on opposite sides of the house to create cross-ventilation. This genuinely flushes accumulated heat out of the building’s thermal mass — and it’s free.

Where it works: climates with a large day-night temperature swing — dry, high-elevation, and inland areas. Where it doesn’t: humid coastal climates, where night air is nearly as warm as day air and brings moisture with it.

Thermal mass matters here. If your home has significant mass — concrete, brick, tile, stone — night flushing is especially effective, because you’re cooling a large heat reservoir that then absorbs heat slowly through the following day.

Priority 3: Use Fans Correctly (Most People Don’t)

The key fact: a fan cools people, not rooms. A fan moves air across your skin, which accelerates evaporation and makes you feel several degrees cooler. It does not lower the air temperature — and the motor actually adds a small amount of heat to the room.

This has one very direct consequence:

Turn fans off when you leave the room. A fan running in an empty room is pure waste. It is not cooling anything.

Ceiling fan direction. In summer, blades should push air downward — you want to feel a breeze. Running a ceiling fan in reverse during summer is a common mistake. (Reverse is for winter, to gently circulate warm air trapped at the ceiling without creating a draft.)

Where fans genuinely help:

SituationDoes a fan help?
You’re in the room, sedentaryYes — significant comfort gain
Room is emptyNo — turn it off
You want to raise the thermostat 2–3°FYes — this is the real savings mechanism
Outdoor air is cooler than indoorYes — use a window fan to pull it in
Humidity is very highPartially — evaporation is less effective
Temperature is dangerously highNo — this needs real cooling, see below

The actual money-saving move with fans is not “use fans instead of AC.” It’s “use a fan so you can set the AC 2–4°F higher and still be comfortable.” That produces a real, measurable reduction in cooling cost, because each degree of reduced cooling load matters.

Window fans and whole-house fans can be genuinely effective at night in suitable climates. Pull air in from the coolest side and exhaust from the warmest.

Priority 4: Get More From the AC

If you’re running air conditioning, these are the things that actually change the bill.

Raise the setpoint. Every degree you raise it reduces cooling load meaningfully. 78°F (26°C) when home, higher when away, is the common recommendation.

Change the filter. A clogged filter restricts airflow, which makes the system run longer for the same cooling. It’s the cheapest efficiency improvement available, and the most skipped.

Clear and shade the outdoor unit. The condenser needs airflow. Trim vegetation back, and keep at least a couple of feet clear. Shading the condenser helps slightly — but never enclose it or block the discharge.

Seal the ducts if any run through an unconditioned attic or crawlspace. Leaky ducts are one of the largest hidden losses in a cooling system.

Use a programmable or smart thermostat to raise the temperature while you’re out. Note that a very large setback with AC is less effective than with heating — the system has to work hard to recover. A few degrees is right; setting it to 85 and crashing it to 72 when you get home does not save much.

Keep interior doors open and return air paths clear. Closing doors to unused rooms can create pressure imbalances that reduce overall system efficiency.

Priority 5: Cut the Heat You Generate Indoors

Everything that uses electricity indoors eventually becomes heat. In summer that’s a double loss — you pay for the energy, then you pay again to remove it.

Cooking dinner with the oven on a hot afternoon can raise the temperature of a small kitchen noticeably, and the AC then pays to remove it. Shifting cooking to a cooler part of the day is free.

The Humidity Lever

This is the most underrated item in the whole list.

High humidity makes you feel hotter than the temperature suggests, because sweat evaporates more slowly. It also makes the air feel heavy and sticky at temperatures that would otherwise be fine.

Dehumidifying is cheaper than cooling. Running a dehumidifier in a damp basement or ground floor can allow you to set the thermostat several degrees higher at the same comfort level — and it addresses the moisture that drives mold and mildew problems in the first place.

Sources of indoor humidity: showers, cooking, drying laundry indoors, damp basements and crawlspaces, and humid outdoor air. Ventilate the bathroom and kitchen to the outdoors, run a dehumidifier where needed, and dry laundry outside when you can.

For a household in a humid climate, addressing humidity often produces a bigger comfort improvement than any change to the air conditioner.

Notice how many of these cost nothing. The free items — covering windows, managing fans, scheduling the thermostat, shifting appliance use — together typically produce most of the achievable savings. Spend on the paid items only after the free ones are habits.

One honest limit: these methods reduce heat gain and improve comfort. They do not replace air conditioning in genuinely extreme heat. During dangerous heat events, prioritize actual cooling — the guidance about not relying on fans applies above roughly 90°F (32°C), and especially for older adults, young children, and anyone with a health condition.

Why This Works

Radiant heat has to be stopped before it becomes sensible heat. Sunlight passing through glass is absorbed by interior surfaces and re-radiated as heat. An exterior barrier rejects that energy at the boundary; an interior one only reduces glare and direct radiation after the energy is already inside. Same goal, very different physics, very different results.

Fans work on people through evaporation, not on air through refrigeration. Moving air strips the humid boundary layer off your skin so sweat evaporates faster. That requires your body to be there — hence the rule about turning them off in empty rooms.

Cooling load scales with the temperature difference. Heat flows into your house in proportion to how much hotter it is outside. Raise the setpoint, and you reduce both the temperature difference and the runtime — a double effect on the bill.

Humidity blocks your body’s own cooling system. Evaporation is how you shed heat. When the air is already saturated, that mechanism stalls, and you feel hot at temperatures that would otherwise be comfortable. Removing moisture restores it at a fraction of the cost of lowering the temperature.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a fan actually cool a room? No. It cools you by moving air across your skin. The room temperature stays the same, and the motor adds a small amount of heat.

Should I leave a ceiling fan on all day? Only when someone is in the room. Otherwise it’s wasted electricity.

Which windows should I cover first? West-facing, then south, then east. North-facing windows contribute very little summer heat gain.

Is it cheaper to leave the AC on all day or turn it up when away? Raising the setpoint when away saves money. Just avoid an extreme setback — a few degrees is the sweet spot.

Does closing curtains really help much? Yes, especially on west-facing glass, and especially if you close them before the sun arrives rather than after.

Why does my house feel hot even at 74°F? Usually humidity. High moisture prevents sweat from evaporating, which is your body’s cooling mechanism.

Do window fans work? In climates with cool nights, yes — particularly with cross-ventilation. In humid climates, the benefit is much smaller.

What’s the single cheapest thing to do? Close coverings on sun-facing windows before the sun hits them. It costs nothing and addresses the largest source of heat gain.
