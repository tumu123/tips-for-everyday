What Temperature Should Your Water Heater Be Set To?

Save Money Bills Published October 17, 2026 9 min read
Advertisement

You will read in a lot of places that your water heater should be set to 120°F. That is reasonable advice, but it is presented as an answer when it is actually a compromise — and understanding what is being traded away matters more than the number itself.

Three things pull in different directions:

GoalFavoursTension
Preventing scaldsLower temperatureHigher temperatures burn faster and deeper
Controlling bacterial growth in the tankHigher temperatureWarm water is where bacteria multiply
Reducing standby heat lossLower temperatureLoss scales with the gap to room temperature

120°F sits near the point where all three are tolerable rather than optimal. There is a way to do better than “tolerable,” and it involves storing water hotter than you deliver it.

1. What the Temperature Actually Controls

Standby loss. A tank of hot water loses heat to the surrounding air continuously, and the rate of loss is roughly proportional to the difference between the water temperature and the room temperature. Raise the setpoint and you widen that gap, so the heater works harder simply to hold temperature — even when nobody uses any water.

This is why water heating is usually among the largest electricity or gas expenses in a home, and why the setpoint is one of the few adjustments that changes the bill without changing behaviour. Every degree you drop is not just less energy to heat new water; it is less energy lost all day, every day.

Delivered volume. A hotter tank means each gallon goes further, because you blend more cold water at the tap. Lowering the setpoint slightly reduces the effective capacity of the tank. In practice this rarely matters, because most tanks are oversized for daily use — but it is the reason a household that regularly runs out of hot water should be cautious about a large reduction.

Scale and sediment. In hard water areas, higher temperatures accelerate mineral scale formation on heating elements and at the bottom of the tank. Scale insulates the element from the water, which makes the heater less efficient and shortens its life. This is an argument for the lower end that is independent of the energy bill.

2. The Scald Side of the Tradeoff

Scald risk is not linear with temperature — it changes dramatically over a narrow range, which is the single most important fact in this decision.

Approximate time to a serious burn in adults:

Water temperatureTime to second-degree burn
120°F (49°C)Around 5–10 minutes
130°F (54°C)Around 30 seconds to 2 minutes
140°F (60°C)Around 3–5 seconds
150°F (66°C)Around 1–2 seconds

The difference between 130°F and 140°F is the difference between “uncomfortable surprise” and “serious injury before you can react.” Children and older adults burn faster than these adult averages.

Where this matters most: the tap or shower is where exposure happens, and the tank temperature is not always the delivered temperature. In a house with a long uninsulated pipe run, the tap may be cooler than the tank. In a house with a recirculation loop, it may be very close to tank temperature.

If anyone in the household has reduced mobility, reduced sensation, or is very young, the delivered temperature matters more than the energy saving. This is not a place to optimise aggressively.

3. The Bacterial Side, Stated Honestly

The other direction of the trade: warm stored water is an environment where bacteria can multiply, and the organism of concern in water systems is Legionella.

The honest summary is that the risk profile depends heavily on the situation:

  • In a well-maintained household tank that is regularly heated and used, routine testing rarely finds problematic levels, and the risk to healthy household members is low.
  • In systems with long pipe runs, infrequently used outlets, storage tanks held at moderate temperatures, or large buildings, the risk is materially higher, and higher storage temperatures are a recognised control measure.
  • In homes with immunocompromised residents, the calculation changes and is worth discussing with a clinician rather than resolving from an article.

The practical implication: do not set a household tank below about 120°F, and be more careful if you have storage volume that sits stagnant. If you have a large tank, a recirculation loop, or guest bathrooms used a few times a year, the higher-temperature-plus-mixing-valve approach described below is a better answer than simply turning the tank down.

4. The Better Answer: Store Hot, Deliver Warm

There is a device that resolves the conflict rather than balancing it. A thermostatic mixing valve (sometimes called a tempering valve) is installed at the water heater outlet. It blends hot water from the tank with cold water to deliver a controlled maximum temperature to the house.

This lets you:

  • Store water at a higher temperature (often 140°F), which suppresses bacterial growth and effectively increases usable capacity, because each stored gallon is blended with more cold water downstream.
  • Deliver water at a safe temperature (typically 120°F or lower), which protects anyone at the tap regardless of how hot the tank is.

The energy picture is more nuanced than it first appears. A hotter tank loses more heat, so standby loss rises. But the higher storage temperature means you draw less hot water from the tank for the same shower, so the total energy can be comparable. The reason to install a mixing valve is primarily safety and capacity, with energy roughly neutral — not as a way to cut the bill.

A mixing valve is a plumbing job involving the supply line, and in many jurisdictions it is inspected work. It is most worth considering when:

  • There are young children, older adults, or anyone with reduced sensation in the home
  • You want the extra capacity of a hotter tank without the scald risk
  • You have a recirculation loop or a large storage volume
  • You are replacing the water heater anyway, when the installation cost is largely absorbed

5. The Adjustments That Are Pure Saving

Whether or not you install a mixing valve, these reduce cost without touching the safety tradeoff.

Insulate the tank. An insulation blanket on an older tank reduces standby loss directly. Many modern tanks are already well insulated — if yours feels warm to the touch, it is losing heat and a blanket will help; if it feels cool, it is already insulated and a blanket adds little. Do not cover the thermostat, the burner compartment on a gas heater, or any label you need to read.

Insulate the hot water pipes. The first few feet from the tank, and any long run to a distant bathroom, are worth insulating. This reduces both heat loss and the wait for hot water at the tap, which also saves water.

Lower the setpoint only if it is high. Many tanks ship at 140°F. Moving to 120°F is the single quickest water-heater saving available and takes minutes. If your tank is already at 120°F, going lower trades away the bacterial margin for very little additional saving.

Use vacation mode properly. If you are away for more than a few days, most heaters have a vacation or low setting. Do not turn a gas heater off entirely unless you know how to relight it safely, and be aware that an electric heater left off for a week still loses all its stored heat, so the reheat cost is real but one-time.

Flush sediment periodically. Sediment at the bottom of the tank insulates the water from the heat source, forcing longer run times. A periodic partial flush is standard maintenance and keeps the heater efficient. In hard water areas this matters more.

Fix the drips. A hot water leak is the most expensive kind of leak, because you have already paid to heat the water. A slow hot tap drip costs more than most people assume — our water bill checklist covers how to find the ones you cannot hear.

6. How to Make the Change Safely

If you decide to adjust the setpoint:

  1. Find the actual setpoint first. Electric heaters often have two thermostats behind access panels, with a reset button on the upper one. Turn off the breaker before removing panels. Gas heaters have a dial on the gas control valve, usually marked with approximate temperatures rather than degrees.
  2. Change it in small increments and give the tank several hours to reach the new temperature.
  3. Test the delivered temperature at the tap, not the tank. Run the hot water for two minutes at a frequently used fixture and measure with a thermometer. Kitchen thermometers and candy thermometers work.
  4. Adjust based on the tap reading. What matters is what reaches skin, not what the dial says.
  5. Record what you changed and note the date, so a future adjustment is not a guess.

Why This Works

The setpoint is unusual among energy adjustments because it does not require behaviour change. You change it once and the effect applies continuously, including during the many hours when nobody is using water at all.

That last part is why it outperforms measures that sound more impressive. A water heater spends most of its life doing nothing but holding temperature against losses to the surrounding air. Loss rate scales with the temperature gap, so the savings from a lower setpoint accrue during idle hours — the hours when you get no benefit from the hotter water anyway.

The scald data is the reason not to treat this as a pure optimisation. The time-to-burn curve collapses over roughly 20°F, meaning the difference between a safe and a dangerous delivered temperature is smaller than intuition suggests. That is also why measuring at the tap matters: the dial setting and the delivered temperature can differ, and only the latter touches skin.

The mixing valve is the logically clean answer because it separates two requirements that a single-temperature tank has to merge. Storage temperature and delivery temperature do not need to be the same number. Once you have a device that decouples them, you can set each for its own purpose rather than splitting the difference.

Finally, insulation and leak repair work because they attack the same loss from the other side: reduce the rate of loss rather than the temperature driving it. That is a saving that does not touch the safety or bacterial tradeoff at all, which makes it strictly better than lowering the setpoint further.

Turning the temperature up to get more hot waterIncreases scald risk substantially. A mixing valve or a larger tank solves capacity without that tradeoff.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best water heater temperature setting?

Around 120°F at the tap is the usual recommendation for a household tank: it keeps scald risk manageable while retaining a margin against bacterial growth. Confirm by measuring at the tap, since the dial and the delivered temperature can differ.

Does lowering the water heater actually save money?

Yes, because standby heat loss scales with the difference between tank and room temperature, and the heater holds temperature all day whether or not you use water. Moving from a typical 140°F factory setting to 120°F is one of the quicker available savings.

Is 140°F dangerous?

At the tap, yes — it can cause a serious burn in seconds. In the tank, it is a recognised way to control bacterial growth, which is exactly why it should be paired with a mixing valve so that what reaches the tap is cooler.

What is a thermostatic mixing valve and do I need one?

It blends hot tank water with cold to cap the delivered temperature. It lets you store hot for safety and deliver warm for safety. Most valuable in homes with young children, older adults, recirculation loops, or large storage volumes.

Will a hotter tank give me more hot water?

Effectively yes, because each stored gallon is blended with more cold water at the tap. That is a genuine benefit of higher storage temperature, but it should be paired with a mixing valve rather than delivered as-is.

How do I measure my actual water temperature?

Run the hot tap at a frequently used fixture for about two minutes, then measure the water with a kitchen or candy thermometer. Test more than one fixture, since distant outlets can differ.

Does an insulation blanket help?

If the tank feels warm to the touch, yes — it is losing heat and a blanket reduces standby loss. If it feels cool, the tank is already well insulated and a blanket adds little. Never cover controls, labels, or a gas burner compartment.

Should I turn the water heater off when I go on holiday?

For trips longer than a few days, a vacation or low setting is sensible. Avoid fully shutting down a gas heater unless you are confident about the relighting procedure.

How often should I flush the tank?

Typically once a year, and more often in hard water areas. Sediment insulates the heat source from the water, so removing it keeps the heater efficient and extends its life.

Why does my hot water run out faster since I lowered the temperature?

Lower tank temperature means less cold-water blending at the tap, so each stored gallon delivers less usable hot water. If this is a persistent problem, a mixing valve with a hotter tank setting resolves it without raising delivered temperature.
