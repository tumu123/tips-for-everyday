Vampire Power: What Standby Draw Actually Costs (And What Doesn't)

Save Money Bills Published October 17, 2026 9 min read

“Unplug your phone charger to save money” is the most repeated energy tip on the internet, and it is close to worthless. A modern USB charger left in the wall with nothing attached draws a fraction of a watt — pennies per year.

That does not mean standby power is a myth. It means the advice has been aimed at the wrong devices. Standby draw is real, and it is extremely unevenly distributed: a handful of devices account for most of it, and they are usually not the ones people unplug.

The short version:

1. Understand Why a Device Draws Power When It Is Off

Two mechanisms produce standby draw, and they behave differently.

Switched-mode power supplies — the small lightweight adapters used by nearly all modern electronics — convert incoming AC to DC at high frequency. They draw a small continuous current to keep the control circuit alive so the device can respond to a remote or a button press. Modern designs draw very little when idle; older ones draw more.

Devices that are never really off. This is the bigger category, and it is mostly a design choice rather than an inefficiency. A cable box stays powered to download guide data and be ready to record. A game console in instant-on mode stays powered to download updates and resume quickly. A smart speaker stays powered because it must always listen. These are not leaks — they are features being paid for.

That distinction matters because it tells you where the savings are. You cannot eliminate the draw of a device whose function requires it to be awake. But you can often choose a lower-power mode, and for devices that do not need to be awake, you can actually turn them off.

The tell: a device that warms up slightly when “off” is drawing meaningful power. A device that is cold to the touch is not.

2. Measure Your Own Without Buying a Meter

You can find your real standby load using the electricity meter on the side of your house. It takes about ten minutes and requires no equipment.

On a digital meter:

  1. Note the reading, or take a photo.
  2. Turn off the main breakers to the house, except leave one circuit on if you need a light. Better: do this during daylight. Better still: have someone watch the meter while you operate the panel.
  3. With everything off, watch the meter for two minutes. If it still advances, you have either missed a circuit or there is standby draw on a circuit that is still live.

A cleaner method that does not require shutting anything off:

  1. Pick a quiet time when nothing major will run (no laundry, no oven, no AC cycle).
  2. Read the meter, wait exactly ten minutes, read it again. Note the baseline consumption.
  3. Now switch off at the wall a specific group of devices — the entertainment centre, say — and repeat the ten-minute measurement.
  4. The difference is that group’s contribution.

Traditional spinning-disc meters are actually easier for this: count disc revolutions per minute with a group on, then off. The change is directly readable. Digital meters with a pulsing indicator work similarly — count pulses per minute.

If you want to check individual devices, a plug-in power meter is inexpensive and gives a watts reading directly. It pays for itself mostly by ending arguments rather than by the electricity saved — but it also reliably identifies the two or three worst offenders in a house, which is the useful output.

3. Rank Your Devices by What They Actually Cost

A rough translation from watts to money: a device drawing 1 watt continuously for a full year consumes about 8.8 kWh. At a typical residential rate, that is roughly one to two dollars per year, per watt.

That number is the whole argument. A charger drawing half a watt costs well under a dollar a year. A cable box drawing 25 watts continuously costs tens of dollars a year. The first is noise; the second is worth a plug.

Where to look, in order:

  • Cable or satellite boxes and DVRs. Frequently the largest single standby item in a home. Some models draw almost as much “off” as on.
  • Game consoles. Instant-on and background-download modes keep the machine substantially powered. A full shutdown mode typically draws far less, at the cost of faster startup.
  • Desktop computers and monitors. A desktop left in a light sleep state still draws power; a monitor left on standby less so. Configure sleep timings and, if the machine is genuinely unused overnight, shut it down.
  • Printers and scanners. Periodic self-wake cycles for maintenance consume more than people expect for a device used a few times a month.
  • Streaming boxes and smart speakers. Low individually, but always on. Worth checking rather than assuming.
  • Anything with a large external power brick. Older, heavier bricks are less efficient than modern ones. If a brick feels warm while the device is idle, it is converting power to heat continuously.
  • Microwaves, ovens, coffee makers. The clock display and control board draw a small continuous amount. Real, but small; the annual cost is typically a few dollars.

Once the standby load is measured, the next question is what it costs by the hour — peak and off-peak pricing explains when the hours are priced differently and whether that helps.

4. Turn Off the Ones That Do Not Need to Be Awake

Once you know which devices matter, the fixes are mostly about configuration rather than discipline.

Switch off at the wall, or use a switched strip, for:

  • Entertainment equipment that you use daily but that does not need to be ready instantly
  • Printers and scanners
  • Desktop peripherals — speakers, secondary monitors, docking stations
  • Power tools and chargers for batteries
  • Anything seasonal

A switched strip is better than unplugging, because unplugging does not survive the first week. One switch for a group of devices converts a daily decision into a single motion.

Keep plugged in and awake:

  • Anything that records scheduled content
  • Anything you control remotely
  • Network equipment you depend on
  • Devices where a full restart is genuinely annoying

Use the device’s own settings first. Most consoles, TVs, and computers have a power mode setting with a meaningful difference between options. Enabling the efficient mode usually costs a few seconds of startup time and is the least effort per watt saved.

Smart plugs are a partial answer. They let you schedule or remotely cut standby draw, and they work well for things used on a predictable schedule. Be aware that the plug itself draws a small amount of power, so on a device that already draws very little, a smart plug can increase total consumption. Use them on the high-draw devices, not the low ones.

5. Do Not Confuse Standby With the Thing That Actually Costs Money

Standby draw in a typical home is a modest share of the total bill. The large items are heating and cooling, water heating, laundry, refrigeration, and anything that makes heat or moves heat.

This is worth stating plainly, because the “vampire power” framing leads people to unplug chargers while ignoring a water heater set higher than it needs to be, or an air filter that has not been changed in a year. Both of those are worth more than every charger in the house combined.

Do the standby pass once, set it up so it stays fixed, and then move your attention to the larger loads.

Standby draw is the invisible cost on the electricity side; recurring charges are the invisible cost on the banking side — finding recurring charges you forgot finds them in one sitting.

Why This Works

The physics gives you a simple conversion: continuous draw is the only kind that matters, because a device that runs for ten minutes a day cannot use much energy regardless of its wattage. Watts times hours is the whole equation, and standby devices win on hours, not watts.

That is why the distribution matters so much. Across a house, standby draw spans roughly two orders of magnitude — from a fraction of a watt to tens of watts — and the total is dominated by the top few entries. Treating all standby draw as equivalent spreads effort evenly across a distribution that is anything but even.

Measuring changes the behaviour in a way that advice does not. Once you have watched your own meter slow down when you switch off one specific group of devices, the decision stops being ideological and becomes arithmetic. You stop unplugging chargers, and you start switching off the cable box, because you have seen which one moves the needle.

The reason configuration beats discipline is that discipline decays. A setting persists; a nightly unplugging ritual lasts about a week. A switched strip is a compromise between the two: one physical action, done once, that holds indefinitely.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does standby power actually cost a typical household?

Meaningfully, but less than is often claimed — commonly a low single-digit to low double-digit percentage of total electricity use, and concentrated in a few devices. The range is wide because it depends almost entirely on whether you have high-draw always-on equipment.

Is it worth unplugging my phone charger?

Not for the money. A modern charger left in the wall with nothing attached draws a fraction of a watt, worth pennies a year. There is no safety or cost argument that makes this worth doing daily.

Which single device usually draws the most standby power?

In many homes, a cable or satellite box with DVR functionality. Some models draw nearly as much when "off" as when in use, which is why it is the first thing to measure.

Can I measure this without buying a meter?

Yes. Read your electricity meter over a fixed interval with a group of devices on, then repeat with them switched off at the wall. The difference is that group's contribution. Ten-minute intervals are usually enough to see a clear change.

Does turning my game console fully off actually save much?

Usually yes, if it is currently in an instant-on or background-download mode. The trade-off is slower startup and losing automatic updates, which is a reasonable trade for a device that is idle most of the day.

Are smart plugs worth it for standby savings?

For high-draw devices with predictable schedules, yes. For low-draw devices, no — the plug's own consumption can exceed what you save. Check the draw first, then decide.

Does a device with a clock display use much power?

Not much. Microwave, oven and coffee maker clocks and control boards draw a small continuous amount, typically a few dollars a year. Real, but low priority.

Will switching things off at the wall damage anything?

Generally no for consumer electronics, though some devices lose settings or need to re-acquire a signal. Avoid doing it to equipment that performs scheduled tasks or that you update remotely.

Is standby draw getting better or worse?

Both. Individual devices have become far more efficient, but the number of always-on connected devices in a home has grown substantially, so total standby draw has not fallen as much as the per-device improvements suggest.

Where should I focus instead if I want a real reduction?

Heating and cooling, water heating, air sealing, refrigeration maintenance, and laundry habits. See our guide to [cutting your electricity bill without spending money](/save-money/bills/cut-electricity-bill-without-spending-money/) for those, which are larger by an order of magnitude.
