How to Handle Common Household Pests Without a Pest Control Contract

Save Money Bills Published October 17, 2026 10 min read

A recurring pest control visit is one of the more expensive recurring household services relative to the time it actually takes. What you are usually paying for is not specialised knowledge — it is regularity, and the fact that someone else has to drive to your house.

Most household pest problems fall into three categories, and each one has a completely different correct response. Applying the wrong one is the main reason DIY attempts fail, and it is also why spraying can make some problems measurably worse.

The three categories:

CategoryTypical examplesCorrect primary response
Occasional invadersSpiders, crickets, silverfish, sowbugs, cluster fliesExclusion plus habitat reduction. Killing individuals achieves little.
Foraging pestsAnts, cockroaches, pantry mothsBait and source removal. Spray usually backfires.
Structural intrudersMice, rats, squirrels, batsExclusion, decisively. No product substitutes for closing the hole.

Identify which you have before buying anything. The rest follows from that.

Step 1: Identify What You Actually Have

Catch one and look at it properly, or photograph it. “Small brown bug” is not enough information to choose a treatment.

Things worth noting:

  • Ants: count the segments between the thorax and abdomen (nodes), and whether they have a single or double waist. More useful: watch where they go. A visible trail means foragers following a pheromone route to a food source, which is a bait situation.
  • Cockroaches: size, whether they fly, and time of day seen. Seeing them during daylight usually indicates a larger population than seeing one at night.
  • Spiders: almost always a symptom. Spiders live where prey lives. Killing spiders without addressing prey and entry points changes nothing.
  • Rodents: droppings size and shape, gnaw marks, greasy rub marks along walls, and noise timing. Rodents follow walls, so marks concentrate along edges.
  • Pantry pests: check the food itself. Moths, small beetles, or webbing in dry goods means the source is in your cupboard, not outside.

Set sticky monitors before treating. Place a few traps along walls and in corners, then check them after a few days. This gives you a baseline: where activity concentrates, and whether what you do next works.

The first step of the general method matters more here than anywhere else. For the evidence that settles an ambiguous bite, see identifying a pest before acting on it.

Step 2: Find Where They Are Getting In

This is the step that determines whether anything else matters. Insects and rodents do not materialise indoors; they arrive through openings, and those openings are findable.

Walk the exterior in daylight:

  1. Utility penetrations. Where pipes, cables, vents, and hose bibs pass through the wall. These are almost always the worst gaps, because they are cut oversized and often never sealed.
  2. The foundation-to-siding junction, and any gap where different materials meet.
  3. Around windows and doors, including the corners where trim meets siding. Check whether old caulk has cracked and pulled away.
  4. Door bottoms. Hold a flashlight inside at night and look from outside — light visible under a door means the gap needs a sweep.
  5. Roof and soffit intersections, ridge vents, and gaps around chimney or flue penetrations.
  6. Crawl space and basement vents, plus any damaged screens.
  7. Garage door perimeter, which is often a much larger gap than it looks.

Then walk the interior, looking at:

  • Under sinks, where supply and drain pipes pass through the cabinet back
  • Gaps behind and under appliances
  • Recessed light fittings and attic hatches
  • Floor-wall junctions in basements and utility rooms
  • Anywhere you have seen activity

Use the light test. Stand inside a dark room and look toward the door or window frames from outside after dark, or have someone shine a light from outside while you look from inside. Light through a gap is a gap.

Photograph everything and make a list. This becomes your work order.

An entry point proves it got in; it does not prove it is still there. For the powder test and the trace reading that settle it, see how to confirm mice are in the walls.

Every gap closed in September is a gap that does not deliver insects in January. For the insect that overwinters in a wall, and how to catch the ones already inside, see sealing the autumn entry points.

Step 3: Seal the Entry Points

Do this before, or at least alongside, any treatment. Killing pests without closing entry points guarantees they return.

Match the material to the gap:

  • Hairline cracks and trim joints: exterior-grade caulk. Clean and dry the surface first; caulk over dirt or old failed sealant will not hold.
  • Gaps wider than about a quarter inch: pack with backer rod first, then caulk over it. Caulk alone in a wide gap sags and fails.
  • Gaps around pipes: caulk or a purpose-made escutcheon. Where rodents are a possibility, pack copper mesh or steel wool behind the sealant, since they will chew through caulk and expanding foam.
  • Larger structural openings: hardware cloth or sheet metal, mechanically fastened. Sealant alone is not structural.
  • Door bottoms: a sweep or threshold insert. Adjustable thresholds work well on uneven floors.
  • Vents and weep holes: screen them or use purpose-made covers. Do not seal weep holes in masonry — they exist to let moisture out, and blocking them causes bigger problems than the pests.
  • Chimneys and large voids: these need a cap or professional work, not sealant.

Also look at the things that invite them in:

  • Firewood, mulch, or stored materials stacked against the foundation
  • Vegetation touching siding
  • Standing water and clogged gutters
  • Open compost or unsealed garbage
  • Pet food left out overnight
  • Unscreened vents

Moving these away from the structure is free and reduces pressure on every seal you install.

Step 4: Remove What Is Attracting Them

Pests come inside because something inside is worth the trip.

Food and water:

  • Store dry goods in sealed containers. Pantry pests arrive in packaging and spread from there.
  • Do not leave dirty dishes overnight; a sink with residue is a resource.
  • Wipe counters and stovetops, including the grease film that builds near cooking surfaces.
  • Fix drips. Many pests need water more than food, and a slow drip under a sink sustains a population indefinitely.
  • Empty the trash regularly and use a lid.
  • Do not leave pet food down overnight.

Shelter and clutter:

  • Reduce cardboard storage in basements and garages; it holds moisture and provides harbourage.
  • Clear the clutter directly against walls where you have seen activity.
  • Keep stored items off the floor in garages and basements.

Removing the attractant is the step most kitchens stop at. For the version built around the breeding site — and the trap that tells you when it is gone — see why fruit flies keep coming back.

Step 5: Treat Only Where It Is Actually Needed

Now — and only now — consider a product.

For ants: use bait, not spray.

This is the most important single piece of advice here, and it is counterintuitive. Ant colonies are structured around a queen, and the workers you see are a small fraction of the colony.

  • Baits work by transfer. A forager takes slow-acting bait back and shares it, which reaches the queen and the brood you will never see. The bait must be slow-acting — if the forager dies before returning, the colony learns nothing and you have killed a few workers.
  • Sprays work by contact, killing what you hit. On a foraging ant trail, a contact spray can trigger colony budding: the disturbance causes the colony to split and relocate, converting one nest into several. This is why a sprayed ant problem often reappears in more places.
  • Place bait on the trail, not across it. Put stations where you see ants walking, and do not spray or clean near them — a competing scent or a dead forager on the trail will stop others from taking it.
  • Be patient. Baits take days to a couple of weeks. If activity at the bait stops, it is usually working; if it moves, follow it and reposition.
  • Identify the species if you can, because sugar-feeding and protein- or fat-feeding ants need different bait formulations. If one bait is ignored, try the other type before concluding it failed.

For cockroaches: bait and gel in cracks, not broadcast spray.

Gel baits placed in small dabs where they travel — hinge areas, cracks, behind appliances, under sinks — outperform surface sprays. Combine with the sanitation and sealing above.

For spiders: skip the insecticide.

Spiders are predators. Removing their prey and their entry points does more than spraying, and spraying removes the predators that eat other pests. Vacuum webs and egg sacs, reduce the insects they feed on, and seal entry points.

For rodents: exclusion is the treatment.

Traps address the individuals present; only sealing prevents reinfestation. Use snap traps placed perpendicular to walls with the trigger toward the wall, since rodents travel along edges. Seal every gap you found, including the small ones — a mouse needs a surprisingly small opening. Avoid poison baits indoors, where rodents can die in inaccessible places and create an odour problem that is worse than the original issue.

For pantry pests: discard, clean, and store sealed.

Find the infested package, discard it, vacuum the shelf and its cracks thoroughly, and move remaining dry goods into sealed containers. Freezing suspect items for several days kills eggs if you want to salvage rather than discard.

A contract is not automatically more expensive than the work you could do yourself — when a contract beats doing it yourself shows where the effective hourly rate makes DIY irrational and where it pays well.

Step 6: Use Sprays Only as a Perimeter, If At All

If you do use a liquid insecticide, the defensible use is a perimeter treatment: applied to the exterior foundation band, around entry points, and under the first course of siding — not as an indoor broadcast and not across an active ant trail.

Precautions that matter:

  • Read and follow the label. The label is the legal instruction, and “more” is not better.
  • Keep it away from drains, waterways, and any surface that touches water.
  • Keep children and pets away until dry.
  • Do not spray near bait stations, near food-preparation surfaces, or on plants you intend to eat.
  • Do not apply indoors as a general treatment. Indoor broadcast spraying is the least effective and highest-exposure use.

Step 7: Verify and Maintain

Two weeks later, check your monitors again.

  • Activity down and centralised: the approach is working; continue.
  • Activity moved: follow it. New trails after baiting often mean a satellite nest.
  • Activity unchanged: re-check entry points. Something is still open.
  • Activity up: reconsider whether you used a contact spray on a social insect, or whether a food source remains.

Then make it seasonal: re-check seals and door sweeps in autumn, before cold weather drives pests indoors, and again in spring — the same audit that keeps bugs out of the house through summer. Re-caulk where the bead has failed. A short audit twice a year is far cheaper than a recurring contract.

Why This Works

Pest pressure is a function of access and attraction, not of the number of pests living in your walls. An unsealed gap is an open door that will be found again no matter how many individuals you kill, which is why treatment without exclusion has a predictable failure mode: it works for a few weeks and then the population is back.

The ant example is the clearest illustration of why mechanism matters more than product strength. A contact insecticide is highly effective at killing individual insects and almost useless against a colony, because the reproductive core is hidden and the workers you see are replaceable. Worse, the disturbance of a contact kill can split the colony, so the treatment increases the number of nests. A slow-acting bait has the opposite property: low toxicity per individual, but it travels to the part of the colony that actually matters.

Monitoring before and after is what makes this a process rather than a hope. Without a baseline, you cannot distinguish “it worked” from “they moved,” and those require different responses.

Seasonal timing works because pest pressure is cyclical. Most indoor invasions happen at predictable transitions — cold weather in autumn, rain or heat in summer — so sealing before the transition prevents the wave instead of reacting to it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do ants come back worse after I spray them?

Contact insecticides kill the foragers you see but not the colony. The disturbance can cause budding, where the colony splits and relocates, turning one nest into several. Slow-acting bait is the correct tool for social insects.

How long does ant bait take to work?

Typically several days to a couple of weeks. The delay is the mechanism — foragers need time to carry it back and share it before the queen is affected.

Do I need a pest control service at all?

For most occasional and foraging problems, no. Structural issues — termites, bats in an attic, a large established rodent population, or anything involving wiring or structural damage — are worth professional help.

What size gap can a mouse get through?

Smaller than most people expect — an opening around the diameter of a pencil is enough for a young mouse. This is why sealing requires attention to small penetrations, not just obvious holes.

Should I seal gaps with expanding foam?

For insulation, yes; as a rodent barrier, no. Rodents chew through it. Pack copper mesh or steel wool behind the sealant, or use hardware cloth for larger openings.

Are natural or essential-oil repellents effective?

Some have mild repellent effects, but they do not remove a colony or an entry point. Treat them as marginal, not as a substitute for exclusion and sanitation.

How do I know if the problem is coming from inside or outside?

Set monitors and watch where activity concentrates. Pantry pests originate in food packaging indoors; ants and occasional invaders typically come from outside; the trail direction usually tells you which.

Is it safe to use insecticides around pets?

Follow the label exactly and keep animals away until treated surfaces are fully dry. Prefer baits in enclosed stations and targeted crack treatments over any broadcast application.

Will sealing entry points alone solve the problem?

For occasional invaders and rodents, largely yes. For foraging pests with an established nest inside or adjacent, sealing should be combined with baiting and source removal.

How often should I re-check?

Twice a year is reasonable — autumn before cold weather drives pests indoors, and spring after winter has stressed exterior sealant.
