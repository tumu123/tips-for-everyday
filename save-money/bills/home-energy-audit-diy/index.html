How to Do a Home Energy Audit Yourself

Save Money Bills Published October 15, 2026 11 min read
Advertisement

Most “DIY energy audit” checklists give you the same generic list: seal drafts, add insulation, change filters. The problem is that they’re not ranked, and they’re not derived from your actual data. A household with a huge baseload gets nothing from sealing windows, while a household with a leaky envelope gets very little from unplugging chargers.

The method here starts with your bills, because the bills will tell you which category of problem you actually have. The walkthrough comes second, and only after you know where to look.

Time: about 30 minutes on bills, about two hours walking the house.

Step 0: Read Your Bills First (This Determines Everything)

Before you touch the house, spend half an hour with twelve months of utility data.

Separate your usage into two buckets.

Your bill contains a baseload — usage that stays roughly constant all year: refrigerator, lights, electronics, standby power, water heating — and a weather-sensitive load — heating and cooling, which swings with the seasons.

Here is how to split them without any special equipment:

Find your shoulder months. Look at your usage across twelve months. Pick the months with the mildest weather — typically spring and autumn — when you were running neither heat nor air conditioning. In most climates these are the two lowest-usage months.

That low number is approximately your baseload. Everything above it is seasonal heating and cooling.

Your shoulder-month usageWhat it meansWhere to focus
Low (well under half of peak)Envelope-driven billsAir sealing, insulation, HVAC
High (most of your usage is constant)Baseload-driven billsWater heater, fridge, standby, lighting
Roughly flat all yearAlmost all baseloadAppliances, not insulation

This one comparison prevents the most common mistake: spending a weekend sealing windows when the real problem is a fifteen-year-old water heater running constantly.

Also grab your actual rate. Find the price per kWh on your bill — not the total. It’s often buried in the line items, and it’s usually higher than the advertised rate once delivery charges and fees are included. You need this number for every calculation that follows.

And check whether you’re on a tiered or time-of-use plan. Under tiered pricing, the marginal kWh costs more than the average, which changes which savings are worth chasing. Under time-of-use, shifting when you run things matters as much as using less.

Step 1: Calculate What Each Major Load Actually Costs

You do not need a meter on every device. The formula is simple:

Watts × hours used per day ÷ 1000 = kWh per day
kWh per day × 365 × your rate = annual cost

Worked example — a 60-watt-equivalent incandescent bulb:

60 W × 4 h/day = 240 Wh/day = 0.24 kWh/day
0.24 × 365 = 87.6 kWh/year
87.6 × $0.16 = about $14/year per bulb

One bulb is $14. Twenty bulbs is $280 — which is why lighting is genuinely worth fixing even though each individual bulb is trivial.

For anything with a motor or a compressor (fridge, freezer, AC, dehumidifier), do not use the nameplate wattage. Those units cycle on and off, so the rated wattage overstates actual use dramatically. Measure with a plug-in power meter over a full week, or look up the unit’s EnergyGuide label, which gives estimated annual kWh directly.

Where household energy typically goes:

End useTypical share of home energy
Space heating and cooling40–50%
Water heating15–25%
Appliances and electronics10–20%
Lighting5–10%
Refrigeration4–8%

Your own bill may differ substantially. A household in a mild climate with an old water heater and a big aquarium could easily invert this ranking — which is exactly why Step 0 comes first.

Step 2: Check the Water Heater

Usually the second-largest energy use in a home, and almost never inspected.

Feel the tank. With a storage-tank unit, place your hand near the top and bottom. Warmth on the outside means heat escaping through insufficient insulation. A tank that is cool to the touch is well insulated.

Check the temperature setting. Many units ship at 140°F (60°C), which is hotter than needed and increases both standby losses and scald risk. 120°F (49°C) is the usual recommendation — adequate for household needs and meaningfully cheaper to maintain.

Feel the pipes. Hot pipes running long distances lose heat continuously. Insulating the first several feet of pipe leaving the tank is inexpensive and quick.

Look at the anode rod. On a tank past five years, this is the single most valuable maintenance item — it is what prevents the tank from corroding through. We cover the full procedure in our water heater maintenance guide.

Check the age. Storage tanks typically last 8–12 years. If yours is at the end of that range and showing any symptoms, the honest calculation is a replacement decision — and heat-pump water heaters use dramatically less electricity than conventional electric resistance units, with rebates commonly available.

Step 3: Hunt the Standby Load

Standby consumption — power drawn by devices that are “off” or idle — typically accounts for 5–10% of household electricity use. It’s a real but modest number. It is not the 20%+ that some sources claim.

The high-value targets, in order:

Device typeWhy it draws
Cable/satellite boxes and DVRsOften draw near full power while “off”
Game consolesStandby modes keep network and USB alive
Desktop computers and monitorsSleep states vary enormously
Anything with an external power brickTransformer losses continue when idle
Devices with always-on displaysClocks, indicator panels, smart displays
Printers and multi-function devicesWake-on-network keeps them partly live

How to find yours. Feel the power bricks. Warm when the device is off means it is drawing. Better: put a plug-in meter on a power strip and measure the whole entertainment centre at once.

Be honest about the savings. Cutting 5–10% of a typical electricity bill is real money, but the individual numbers are small — a few dollars a year per device. The reason to bother is that it’s cheap and takes one afternoon, not because it transforms your bill. Prioritize the entertainment centre; skip unplugging phone chargers, which draw almost nothing.

Step 4: Find Air Leaks

Air leakage is typically one of the largest single sources of heating and cooling loss in older homes. It’s also the cheapest thing to fix.

Do this first: check the big holes before the small ones. The largest leaks are almost never around windows. They are:

  • Where the house frame meets the foundation
  • Gaps around plumbing, wiring, and duct penetrations
  • The attic hatch or pull-down stairs
  • Recessed light fixtures in ceilings below unconditioned space
  • Chimneys and flues
  • Unsealed ductwork in unconditioned areas

Incense test. On a windy day, or with an exhaust fan running to depressurize the house, hold a lit incense stick near suspect spots. Watch the smoke: it will be pulled inward or blown sideways at a leak. Move slowly — a few inches per second.

Wet-hand test. On a cold day, run a wet hand around frames and edges. Cold spots are obvious.

Paper test. Close a door or window on a sheet of paper. If it pulls out with no resistance, the seal isn’t sealing.

We cover the sealing sequence and the priority order in detail in Cheap Ways to Insulate Windows and Doors.

Step 5: Check Insulation — Attic First

Heat rises, so the attic is where insulation pays back fastest.

How to check depth. Look across the attic floor. If the insulation is at or below the level of the joists, you have less than you want. In most US climates, the recommended level is around R-38 to R-60, which generally means roughly 12–17 inches depending on material.

Look for the warning signs:

What you seeWhat it means
Joists clearly visible above insulationInsufficient depth
Dark patches in the insulationAir leaking and filtering dust
Insulation compressed or mattedLost effectiveness
Uneven coverage, bare spotsGaps doing nothing
Frost or dampness on the underside of the roof deckMoisture and ventilation problem

Do not skip the air sealing. Adding insulation on top of significant air leaks underperforms, because air moves through the insulation rather than being stopped by it. Seal first, then insulate.

Check the attic hatch. It is frequently the single worst-insulated square foot in the house, and it takes twenty minutes to fix.

Step 6: Check the HVAC System

Change the filter. A clogged filter makes the system work harder and reduces airflow. This is genuinely the highest-value item in this whole section, and it costs a few dollars.

Check supply vents. Make sure furniture, rugs, and curtains aren’t blocking them. Blocked vents force the system to run longer.

Check the outdoor unit. Clear vegetation and debris. There should be clearance around the condenser. Gently rinsing dirty coils from the inside out can help — but do not use high pressure, which bends the fins.

Check the thermostat. Programmable setback genuinely works. A few degrees of setback for eight hours a day produces measurable savings, because heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference between inside and outside.

Look at the ducts. In unconditioned spaces — attics, crawlspaces, garages — leaky ducts waste a surprising amount. Sealing accessible joints is a cheap, high-return afternoon.

Note the bottom of that table. Replacing an appliance that still works has the longest payback by a wide margin, even when it’s inefficient. Do the cheap things first and reassess — many households find they don’t need the expensive items at all.

When to Call a Professional

A DIY audit finds the obvious problems. A professional audit finds the hidden ones.

What professionals add:

  • Blower door test — depressurizes the house and quantifies total air leakage, letting you compare before and after
  • Infrared scan — reveals missing insulation and thermal bridges inside walls and ceilings
  • Duct leakage testing — measures how much conditioned air you’re actually losing
  • Combustion safety testing — checks for carbon monoxide and proper appliance venting

It’s worth paying for when: your bills are high and you cannot find a cause; you’re planning a major upgrade and want to know where to spend; you have comfort problems that sealing hasn’t fixed; or you want to verify contractor work afterwards.

Many utilities offer free or heavily subsidized audits, and some offer rebates that require one. Check with your utility before paying full price.

Why This Works

Your bills contain a diagnostic signal. The shape of a twelve-month usage curve separates envelope problems from baseload problems better than any walkthrough, because it reflects the whole house over a full year of real weather. Starting with the data means the physical inspection happens with a hypothesis instead of a clipboard.

Air sealing beats insulation per dollar, because heat moves with air. Air moving through a gap carries far more energy than conduction through the same area. Sealing gaps addresses the dominant mechanism at a fraction of the cost of adding material — and it makes subsequent insulation more effective, because still air is what actually insulates.

Small continuous loads add up because they never stop. A device drawing a few watts seems irrelevant until you multiply by 8,760 hours. A 5-watt standby draw is about 44 kWh a year — modest alone, but there are usually dozens of them.

The ranking matters more than the list. Every item on a generic checklist helps somewhat; the differences in return between them are large. Doing the top four items typically outperforms doing items five through ten.

Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate is a DIY audit compared to a professional one? It reliably finds the large, accessible problems — which is where most of the savings are. It will miss hidden voids, internal duct leaks, and combustion safety issues.

What is a normal baseload for a household? It varies enormously with housing type, appliances, and whether water heating is electric. The useful measure is not the absolute number but whether your baseload is a small or large share of your peak usage — that tells you where to look.

Should I seal windows with expanding foam? Not on sashes you want to open, and not without checking clearances around the frame. Use removable rope caulk or weatherstripping on operable windows.

Where are the biggest air leaks usually located? Not at windows. Typically where framing meets the foundation, around plumbing and wiring penetrations, at the attic hatch, and around recessed lighting.

How much insulation should my attic have? Most US climates call for roughly R-38 to R-60, which is usually around 12–17 inches depending on material. If the joists are clearly visible above the insulation, you need more.

Do programmable thermostats actually save money? Yes, when the setback is real. Savings scale with how long and how many degrees you set back, because heat loss tracks the indoor-outdoor temperature difference.

Is it worth unplugging phone chargers? Barely. Modern chargers draw a fraction of a watt when idle. Focus on entertainment centres and devices with external power bricks.

How often should I redo an audit? Every few years, and always after a major renovation or when you notice a step change in bills that weather does not explain.
