How to Clean a Dishwasher So It Actually Cleans Dishes

Home Hacks Appliances Published October 17, 2026 9 min read

A dishwasher that leaves grit, cloudy glasses, or a smell is almost always described as “not cleaning properly.” That framing sends people to detergent, rinse aid, and eventually a repair call.

The more useful framing is that the machine is cleaning itself badly. A dishwasher is a recirculating system: it sprays water through the load and then sends that same water back around. Everything it removes from your dishes passes through a filter, and if the filter is loaded, the water it sprays back is dirty. Grit and film are the visible result of that loop.

The parts that matter, and what each one does when it fails:

PartFunctionSymptom when neglected
FilterTraps food so it does not recirculateGrit on dishes, film, smell, poor wash
Spray armsDistribute waterBlind spots, dishes dirty on one side
SumpCollects water for the pumpStanding water, smell
Door sealKeeps water inLeaks, mould, smell at the door
Detergent dispenserReleases at the right timeTablets left undissolved or released early

The filter is first because it is the one that affects everything downstream. If you have already cleaned the washing machine, the logic will feel familiar — see how to clean a washing machine for the same recirculating-system problem in a different appliance.

Step 1: Find and Remove the Filter

Almost every modern dishwasher has a removable filter assembly at the bottom of the tub. It is usually two pieces: a cylindrical or basket-shaped coarse filter and a flat fine filter beneath or inside it.

How to get to it:

  1. Pull out the bottom rack completely.
  2. Look at the floor of the tub. There will be a round or rectangular assembly, often with a handle or a quarter-turn lock.
  3. Twist counter-clockwise (most brands) and lift straight up.
  4. Separate the coarse and fine elements if they come apart.

Check the manual for your model — a minority of machines have a self-cleaning filter with a grinder instead, in which case there is nothing to remove and you can skip to Step 4. Do not force an assembly that will not release; some require a specific rotation or a small latch.

Step 2: Wash the Filter Properly

This is the step where people underperform, and where the whole problem usually lives.

Do not just rinse it. A rinse removes the loose debris and leaves the fine mesh partly blinded by fat and starch film. The mesh is the part that matters, and it is the part a rinse does not fix.

The method:

  1. Rinse off loose food into the bin, not the sink — you are trying not to send it back into the plumbing.
  2. Scrub both elements with a soft brush and hot soapy water, working along the mesh rather than across it.
  3. For greasy film, soak in hot water with a few drops of dish soap for ten minutes, then scrub.
  4. For mineral film, soak in white vinegar for ten to fifteen minutes.
  5. Rinse thoroughly and hold it up to the light. You should be able to see through the mesh. If it looks dull or blocked, wash it again.

That last check is the whole point of the step. A filter that looks clean but does not pass light is still restricting flow.

How often: rinse weekly to fortnightly, deep clean monthly. Households that pre-rinse less, or that wash very greasy loads, need it more often.

Step 3: Clear the Spray Arms

The spray arms are the rotating tubes with holes. Food particles and mineral scale block the holes, which produces the characteristic symptom of dishes that are clean on one side and dirty on the other, or a whole zone that never gets clean.

Most spray arms lift straight off — unscrew a retaining nut by hand, or pull the arm up from its hub. Check the manual if it resists.

Once removed:

  1. Hold each arm up to a light and look through the holes.
  2. Clear blockages with a toothpick, a pipe cleaner, or a straightened paper clip.
  3. Flush the arm by running water through the hub end and watching whether it emerges evenly from every hole.
  4. Soak in vinegar if the holes are scaled rather than blocked with food.
  5. Check the arm for cracks and check that it spins freely on its hub after refitting — a seized arm sprays in one place only.

Also check the water inlet where the arm connects. A screen or seal there can trap debris.

Step 4: Clean the Sump and Check for Standing Water

With the filter out, look into the recess it came from. This is the sump.

  • Wipe it out with paper towels, removing the sludge that collects at the bottom.
  • Check for standing water. A small amount is normal; a deep pool that does not drain suggests a blocked drain path or a pump issue rather than a cleaning problem.
  • Clean the float switch, if you can see it. A float gummed up with grease can make the machine think it is full, which stops it filling properly.
  • Check the drain hose for kinks or a blockage if water sits.

Do not pour water aggressively into the sump. It is designed to hold water, but you do not want to push debris into the pump.

Step 5: Clean the Door Seal and the Door Edge

The seal around the door is the part that produces most dishwasher smells, and it is the part almost nobody cleans.

  • Wipe the whole gasket with a cloth dipped in warm soapy water, going into the fold rather than just across the surface. Food and grease collect inside the fold.
  • Look for black spots. Mould in the fold is common and needs a vinegar or a dilute bleach wipe, then thorough drying.
  • Clean the bottom edge of the door and the tub lip where water sits after a cycle.
  • Dry it and leave the door slightly open between uses so it can ventilate. A machine kept shut while damp will smell no matter how well you clean it.

Step 6: Run a Maintenance Cycle

Once the mechanical parts are clean, a hot cycle clears what is left in the pipes and pump.

The simple version: run the hottest cycle with the machine empty, with a cup of white vinegar standing upright in the top rack. Do not add detergent.

The better version, for a machine with scale or odour:

  1. Put a cup of vinegar in the upper rack and run a hot cycle.
  2. Then sprinkle baking soda across the floor of the tub and run a short hot cycle.
  3. Wipe out the remaining residue afterwards.

About dishwasher cleaning tablets: they are convenient and do help, particularly with scale. They are not a substitute for cleaning the filter — a tablet cannot clear a blocked filter, and using one while the filter is loaded produces a clean-smelling machine that still washes badly.

Do not use dish soap or hand-washing detergent in a maintenance cycle. It foams, and the foam can overflow and damage components.

Step 7: Fix the Habits That Foul It Fast

Once clean, the machine stays cleaner if the loading and pre-treatment habits are right.

  • Scrape, do not rinse. Modern machines and detergents handle food residue. Pre-rinsing adds water use and removes the material detergent enzymes act on.
  • Do not overload. Water must reach every surface. Nesting bowls create dry pockets that no cleaning routine can fix.
  • Load the soiled side facing the spray. Bowls and cups angled down and inward.
  • Use the right amount of detergent and replenish rinse aid — cloudy glasses are usually a rinse aid or water hardness issue, not a dirty machine.
  • Run a hot cycle occasionally if you normally use eco settings, particularly in hard water areas.
  • Check the filter monthly and keep a spare if your model’s filter is fragile.

Why This Works

A dishwasher is a closed recirculating loop, and that structure explains its failure mode. Water is sprayed onto the load, falls to the floor of the tub, is filtered, and is pumped back to the spray arms. The filter is the only thing separating “water that has just removed food” from “water about to be sprayed onto dishes.”

When the filter blinds over, the loop degrades in a specific way: the machine still sprays, still drains, still sounds normal, but it is redistributing a suspension of fine food and fat rather than clean water. The dishes come out with grit, and the film that develops on glasses is that suspension drying onto the surface. Nothing is broken; the loop is just running dirty.

This is why detergent changes do not fix it. Detergent chemistry cannot compensate for a mechanical restriction upstream of it, and adding more detergent to a dirty loop simply adds more chemistry to a suspension that is already saturated.

The spray arm symptom is different and easier to diagnose: if the blockage is in the arms, the failure is spatially uneven — clean on one side, dirty on the other, or one rack affected and not the other. A filter problem is uniform; an arm problem is patchy. That distinction tells you which part to open.

The door seal matters out of proportion to its size because it is the one part that stays wet between cycles. A dishwasher’s tub drains, but the seal fold holds a film of water and food, and a closed door keeps it humid. That combination is a growth environment, which is why the smell persists even after the interior is spotless.

Finally, the standing-water check separates two problems that look alike. Water that will not drain is a drainage or pump fault, not a cleaning task, and no amount of vinegar will fix it. Knowing which one you have prevents the common mistake of repeatedly cleaning a machine that needs a repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I clean the dishwasher filter?

Rinse it every week or two and wash it properly once a month. Households that wash very greasy loads, or that do not pre-rinse, need it more often.

Why are my glasses coming out cloudy?

Usually a rinse aid or water hardness issue rather than dirt. Cloudiness that does not wipe off is etching from soft water and too much detergent; a white film that wipes off is hard water mineral deposit.

Does a dishwasher cleaning tablet actually work?

It helps with scale and odour in the pipes and pump, but it cannot clear a blocked filter. Use it after the mechanical parts are clean, not instead of cleaning them.

Why does my dishwasher smell even after cleaning?

Most often the door seal fold, the filter housing, or the bottom edge of the door where water sits. Also check for standing water in the sump.

Can I use vinegar and baking soda together?

Do them in sequence, not together — they neutralise each other. Vinegar first for scale, then baking soda for odour and residue.

How do I know if my dishwasher has a filter to clean?

Look at the floor of the tub with the bottom rack out. A removable assembly with a handle or lock is a manual filter. Models with a hard food disposer have a self-cleaning filter and no cartridge to remove.

Why are dishes dirty on one side only?

A blocked spray arm hole or a seized arm. Lift the arm off, clear the holes, and confirm it spins freely.

Is it normal to have water in the bottom after a cycle?

A small amount is normal. A deep pool that persists indicates a drainage restriction or pump problem.

Can I clean the spray arms in the dishwasher itself?

Better to remove and clean them separately, since you can see through the holes and flush them properly. A cycle will not clear a blocked jet.

Does hard water make this worse?

Yes. Scale blocks spray arm jets and coats the filter mesh, so hard water areas need more frequent descaling with vinegar or a citric-acid based cleaner.
