How Often to Wash Pillows (and How to Do It)

Home Hacks Fabrics Published October 24, 2026 10 min read

A pillow that has been slept on for two years weighs roughly 10% more than it did when it was new. The difference is not mysterious: sweat (about a cup per person per night), skin cells, sebum, dust mite colonies and their droppings, and whatever the pillow picks up from hair products. None of that is visible from the outside, which is why pillows get washed years later than they should. The mattress underneath has the same problem at a larger scale, and deep cleaning a mattress is the other half of the bedroom job.

Washing it is straightforward — for some fillings. For others it is a destructive act that ruins the pillow in one cycle. So the first decision is not “how” but “whether,” and that depends entirely on what is inside.

The two hard nos are memory foam and latex. Both are solid foam, and foam plus mechanical agitation plus water equals a pillow that comes out in pieces or develops a permanently compressed, crumbly core. A memory foam pillow can only be vacuumed, aired, and spot cleaned — nothing more.

Buckwheat and hull pillows are a category people get wrong. The shell is washable but the filling is not, so they have to be emptied into a bag first, and the hulls should be sun-dried rather than washed.

How Often, by Household

The generic advice — every six months — is a reasonable average that is wrong for both ends of the range.

HouseholdPillow washing interval
No allergies, uses a pillow protector and a pillowcaseEvery 6 months
Allergy or asthma in the householdEvery 2 to 3 months
Night sweats, or sleeps hotEvery 3 months
Illness with fever, or recovering from a coldWash immediately after recovery
Pets sleeping on the bedEvery 3 months
No protector, pillowcase changed weeklyEvery 3 to 4 months

The pillowcase is changed weekly and that handles the surface. What accumulates below it is why the protector matters: a zippered pillow protector under the pillowcase cuts the washing frequency roughly in half, because it is a barrier you can wash far more easily than the pillow itself.

Before You Wash: The Two-Minute Check

Three things to check before the pillow goes in the machine.

1. Are there tears or open seams? A split seam in a down pillow empties the drum and clogs the drain. Sew it closed first, or put the pillow inside a zippered pillowcase for the wash.

2. Is it still worth washing? Fold the pillow in half and squeeze out the air. If it does not spring back open on its own, the filling has collapsed and washing will not restore it. A flattened pillow is a replacement, not a cleaning job.

3. Does it smell? A sour or musty smell that persists after airing for a day means microbial growth inside the filling. That is the same problem as mildew smell in laundry, with the same rule: you cannot rinse a smell out of a filling that never dried. Washing may help a polyester pillow; for foam it means replacement, because the growth is inside the cells.

Washing Polyester and Down-Alternative Pillows

The easy category, and the one most pillows fall into.

  • Two at a time if the machine allows. A single pillow can unbalance a load; two opposed pillows balance each other. This is the single most useful trick for machine-washing pillows.
  • Gentle or delicate cycle, cold or warm water — never hot, which can shrink the shell and set the sweat odor in.
  • A tablespoon of mild liquid detergent. Not powder, which can leave granules inside the filling, and not fabric softener, which coats the fibers and reduces loft.
  • An extra rinse. Pillows hold detergent inside the filling more than flat fabric does, and residue makes them smell stale faster.

Washing Down Pillows

Down needs different handling, and the differences matter more than most guides admit.

  • Front-loading machine only. A top-loader’s agitator tears the shell and wrings the down into felted clumps that never fully separate again. If you only have a top-loader, hand wash in a bathtub instead.
  • Use a down-specific or mild detergent, never a standard laundry powder. Down carries a natural oil coating that keeps the clusters able to loft. Alkaline detergents strip it, and stripped down compacts into flat sheets.
  • No softener, ever. It coats the clusters the same way it coats fabric.
  • Two rinses minimum. Detergent trapped in down is the main cause of the clumped, sour-smelling pillow that never recovers.
  • Low heat, long cycle, with two or three dryer balls or clean tennis balls. More on this below.

Feather pillows are more forgiving than down but heavier, and they take longer to dry than people expect — often three hours on low.

What the Tennis Balls Actually Do

The tennis-ball trick is repeated everywhere without the mechanism, which leads people to use it wrong.

The balls do not “fluff” the pillow by magic. They mechanically beat the filling as the drum turns, breaking up the clumps that form when wet down mats together. Without them, a wet down pillow dries as one dense slab in the middle and a dry shell on the outside — which feels fine when you take it out and smells musty three days later.

Three practical points:

  • Only in the dryer, never in the wash. Tennis balls in a washing machine drum are a good way to crack the drum or tear the shell.
  • Two or three, not more. Too many and the pillow gets shoved into a corner and does not tumble.
  • Stop the dryer every 20 to 30 minutes, take the pillow out, and break it apart by hand. This is the step that actually prevents clumping. The balls help; your hands finish the job.

Dryer balls (wool or rubber) work as well as tennis balls and do not have the dye-transfer risk that a cheap tennis ball does. If you use tennis balls, use new ones and check that the label says they are colorfast.

Drying Is the Whole Job

A pillow that is 95% dry is a pillow that will smell. This is the most common failure in the entire process.

How to tell it is dry:

  • Squeeze it against your ear. Any faint damp mustiness means more time.
  • Feel the center, not the edges. The corners dry first, and the middle is what matters.
  • Weigh it if you have a kitchen scale — compare against a known-dry pillow of the same type.

Polyester: low heat, 60 to 90 minutes, with balls. It tolerates air drying but will feel stiff until it is tumbled briefly.

Down: low heat, 2 to 4 hours, with balls, interrupted every half hour to break up clumps by hand. Do not rush this with higher heat — high heat makes the shell brittle and can scorch the filling. A duvet is the same process at four times the volume, and the duvet washing guide starts with the capacity check that decides whether it is possible at all.

Air drying works for polyester on a sunny, breezy day if you flip it twice and prop it upright on its edge so air moves through the filling. It does not work for down, which mats and grows musty before it dries through.

Memory Foam and Latex: What You Can Actually Do

Since neither can be washed, the maintenance is different.

  • Vacuum both sides with the upholstery attachment, monthly. This removes dust and skin cells from the surface without water.
  • Spot clean with a barely damp cloth and a drop of mild soap, then blot dry immediately. Never soak.
  • Air it out — an hour in the shade on a dry day. Not direct sun, which degrades the foam.
  • Sprinkle baking soda, wait 30 minutes, vacuum it out for odor. This is the one place baking soda on a pillow is appropriate, because the vacuum removes it fully.
  • Use a waterproof protector. For foam, this is not optional — once liquid gets into the cells, it stays.

Do not put a memory foam pillow in the freezer to kill dust mites. A domestic freezer reaches about 0°F (−18°C), and dust mites survive 48 hours at that temperature in most studies. Even when it works, the allergen remains in the pillow; killing the mite does not remove what causes the reaction.

Similarly, sun-drying is overrated as a disinfectant. UV penetrates fabric poorly, so the benefit is drying, not sterilization.

Getting It Back Together

Put the pillow through the dryer one extra cycle without balls on air-only if it still feels damp anywhere. Then reassemble in the right order: pillow, then protector, then pillowcase. The protector is the layer that gets washed monthly and keeps the pillow’s interval at six months instead of three.

Rotate two pillows if you have them, and replace rather than wash when the fold test fails. A pillow that no longer springs back is holding dust mite allergen in a compressed core that washing cannot reach.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I wash pillows in a regular washing machine?

Polyester, microfiber, and down-alternative pillows yes, on a gentle cycle with two at a time to balance the load. Down pillows need a front-loading machine, because a top-loader's agitator tears the shell and felts the filling. Memory foam and latex should never be machine washed at all.

How often should pillows be washed?

Every six months for most households using a protector, every two to three months if anyone has allergies or asthma, and every three months for night sweaters or households with pets on the bed. Wash immediately after any illness with fever.

Can you wash a memory foam pillow?

No. Water breaks down the viscoelastic cell structure and the pillow crumbles or compresses permanently. Vacuum it monthly, spot clean with a barely damp cloth, air it in shade, and use a waterproof protector. Replace it when it stops springing back.

Why do tennis balls go in the dryer with pillows?

They beat the filling mechanically as the drum turns, breaking up the clumps that wet down forms. Without them a down pillow dries as a dense slab in the middle and a dry shell outside, which smells musty days later. Never put tennis balls in the washing machine.

How long does it take to dry a pillow?

Polyester takes 60 to 90 minutes on low. Down takes two to four hours on low, interrupted every half hour so you can break the clumps apart by hand. A pillow that is 95% dry will smell within days, so check the center rather than the surface.

How do I know when to replace a pillow instead of washing it?

Fold it in half and squeeze the air out. If it does not spring back open on its own, the filling has collapsed and washing will not restore it. Yellow staining that persists after washing and a smell that returns within days are the other two signals.

Does putting a pillow in the freezer kill dust mites?

Not reliably. A domestic freezer reaches about 0°F, and dust mites survive 48 hours at that temperature in most studies. Even when it works, the allergenic protein stays in the pillow, so killing the mite does not remove what triggers the reaction.

Can I dry a pillow in the sun instead of a dryer?

For polyester, on a dry breezy day with flipping, yes. For down, no — it mats and grows musty before the center dries. And do not count on sunlight to sterilize anything: UV penetrates fabric poorly, so the benefit of sun drying is drying, not disinfection.
