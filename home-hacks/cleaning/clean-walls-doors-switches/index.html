How to Clean Walls, Doors and Light Switches

Home Hacks Cleaning Published October 24, 2026 10 min read

Three surfaces in every home get cleaned less than once a year despite being touched hundreds of times a week: the wall behind the light switch, the door around the handle, and the switch plate itself.

The reason is not laziness. It is that people try once, leave a shiny patch on a matte wall, and conclude the wall cannot be cleaned. That conclusion is correct for the method they used and wrong for the wall. The finish decides whether you can scrub, wipe, or only dust — and almost nobody checks the finish before starting.

Step 1: The Finish Test

Paint comes in sheens, and the sheen is a measure of how much surface texture the paint has. More texture means it scatters light (matte), and it also means it traps soil and cannot be rubbed without flattening that texture — which reads as a permanent shiny spot.

SheenCan it be washed?Method
Flat / matteNo — dust and blot onlyVacuum brush, dry microfiber, damp blot. Never rub
EggshellLightlyDamp cloth with a drop of dish soap, gentle pressure
SatinYesDamp cloth, mild detergent, then dry
Semi-glossYes, including light scrubbingDamp cloth, melamine sponge for stubborn marks
Gloss / high-glossYes, most durableAnything non-abrasive, then buff dry

The test: pick a spot behind a piece of furniture where nobody will see it. Rub with a damp white cloth for five seconds, let it dry, and look at it at an angle in daylight. If there is a lighter or shinier patch, the paint is flat or matte and you cannot rub it. If nothing shows, you can wash it.

Do this before you touch a wall in a hallway, because hallway walls are almost always flat.

Step 2: Order of Operations

Cleaning a room top-down is standard advice, but for walls and doors the more useful order is dry-then-wet:

  1. Vacuum first — walls, door tops, baseboards, switch plates, and the window and door tracks — with the brush attachment. The tracks hold more grit than any other frame surface, and cleaning window tracks is the same dry-first rule applied to the worst offender. Dust that goes straight into a wet cloth turns into mud that smears and dries into a visible film.
  2. Work from the top down so drips do not run over cleaned areas.
  3. Doors and frames before walls, because door cleaning splashes.
  4. Switch plates last, with the cloth that is already damp, so you are not carrying grime from the wall onto a surface hands touch.

Step 3: Walls

The soil on walls is specific, and it differs by height:

  • Above 4 feet: dust, cooking aerosol, and smoke if anyone smokes indoors.
  • Between 1 and 4 feet: handprints, pet contact, furniture scuffs, and whatever children were holding.
  • Below 1 foot: vacuum brush marks, shoe scuffs, mop splash.

For matte and flat paint:

Vacuum with the brush attachment first, then use a dry microfiber cloth in a light circular motion — dry microfiber lifts a surprising amount of dust and light soil without wetting the surface. For anything stuck, dampen a cloth with warm water (no detergent), blot rather than rub, and dry immediately with a second cloth.

Do not use a melamine “magic eraser” on flat paint. It is a fine abrasive, and on flat paint it removes the texture in a visible patch. It is genuinely useful on semi-gloss and on scuffed baseboards; it is a mistake on walls.

For eggshell through gloss:

One teaspoon of mild dish soap in a quart of warm water. Dampen a microfiber cloth, wring it so it is barely wet, and wipe in overlapping circles with light pressure. Follow immediately with a clean dry cloth — the dry pass is what prevents streaks and water marks.

For a stubborn mark on satin or gloss, a dab of dish soap directly on the cloth, worked with a fingertip, is usually enough. Rinse with a clean damp cloth afterward, or the soap residue attracts dust and the spot returns darker.

Two things that surprise people:

  • Crayon, marker, and ink come off with isopropyl alcohol on a cloth, not with soap. Test in a hidden spot first, and on flat paint use the minimum amount.
  • Grease near a stove or kitchen doorway needs a degreaser, not soap — the same problem as grease on kitchen cabinets, and the same solution. Dish soap at higher concentration works; so does a dedicated degreaser.

Step 4: Doors, Frames, and Handles

Doors have three distinct zones and each needs a different approach.

Around the handle is the worst — a mix of hand oils, skin cells, and whatever was on the hands. Soap alone smears this; you need a degreaser. A drop of dish soap on a damp cloth, worked in small circles around the handle, then rinsed and dried. On white doors this zone is often visibly grey compared to the rest of the door, and it takes two passes to even out.

The bottom edge and lower panel collects shoe scuffs. These are usually rubber transfer, and they come off with a melamine sponge on semi-gloss doors, or with a cloth and a small amount of rubbing alcohol. Do not use an abrasive on flat-painted doors.

The top edge is pure dust, and nobody looks. Vacuum brush once a year, especially on doors under an air vent, where dust settles in a visible line.

Frames and jambs are usually semi-gloss and can take a damp cloth with soap. The part that matters: wipe the strike plate and latch area, where hand contact and metal dust combine into a dark smear that looks like damage and is not.

Door handles themselves: metal handles are the dirtiest objects in most homes. Wipe with a disinfecting wipe or a cloth with soap, then dry — a wet handle invites water spots on brushed nickel. Do not spray cleaner directly into a lock cylinder; it flushes the lubricant out.

Step 5: Light Switches and Outlet Plates

Switch plates are touched more than any other surface in the house apart from handles, and they are almost never cleaned. On a white plate the grime shows as a grey halo around the toggle.

The safe method:

  1. Turn the switch off and, for an outlet, do not plug anything in while cleaning.
  2. Wipe with a barely damp cloth — not wet. Water pushed into the gap around a toggle can reach live parts.
  3. Use a cotton swab or a toothbrush for the gap between the plate and the wall, which is where the dust line forms.
  4. Never spray cleaner at the plate. Spray the cloth, then wipe. This applies to every electrical surface in the house.

For yellowed plastic plates, the yellowing is usually UV and heat degradation of the plastic itself, not soil — so cleaning does not fix it. A paste of baking soda and water, worked with a toothbrush, will lift surface grime and lighten the top layer slightly, but it also dulls the finish. Replacement plates cost about a dollar each and are held by two screws; if the yellowing bothers you, replace rather than scrub.

Screws: a slightly loose switch plate screw is why plates crack when you press them. Check that the screws are snug while you are there — hand-tight, not torqued.

Step 6: The Marks That Are Not Dirt

Three common wall marks are not soil and cannot be cleaned off:

  • Scuffs that are paint transfer. When furniture or a shoe deposits its own material onto the wall, or removes paint down to the layer beneath, no cleaner helps. On flat paint this often looks like a lighter smear — you are seeing the primer. It needs touch-up paint, and on flat paint a touch-up is genuinely invisible because there is no sheen to mismatch.
  • Pile reversal on carpet is the analogue, but on walls the equivalent is sheen burnishing — the shiny patch left by rubbing. It is permanent. If it is in a visible place, the fix is repainting that wall — and reducing what settles in the first place is the cheaper strategy, which is the argument in reducing dust at the source.
  • Water marks and efflorescence near a ceiling or along a baseboard are not cleaning problems. They are moisture problems, and the moisture checklist is the right starting point before you paint over them.

How Often

SurfaceInterval
Light switch plates and door handlesWeekly, or with the regular clean

Walls in bedrooms and low-traffic roomsAnnually

The cheap habit that changes the interval: wipe the switch plate and the door handle whenever you clean the bathroom, since the cloth is already in your hand. Those two surfaces account for most of the visible grime in a hallway.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I clean matte painted walls?

Only by dusting and blotting, not by rubbing. Matte paint has surface texture that scatters light, and rubbing flattens it into a visible shiny patch that cannot be undone without repainting. Vacuum with a brush attachment, use a dry microfiber cloth, and blot anything stuck with a barely damp cloth.

How do I get scuff marks off a wall?

If it is rubber or dirt transfer on semi-gloss or gloss, a melamine sponge or a cloth with a little rubbing alcohol works. If the mark is lighter than the wall, it is usually paint transfer or a burnished sheen, and neither comes off — that needs touch-up paint or repainting the wall.

What is the best way to clean light switch plates?

Turn the switch off, then wipe with a barely damp cloth — never spray directly at the plate. Use a cotton swab for the gap between plate and wall where the dust line forms. Yellowed plates are usually degraded plastic rather than dirt, so replacement is cheaper than scrubbing.

Why do my walls look streaky after cleaning?

Two causes: too much water, or no dry pass. Wring the cloth until it is barely damp, work in overlapping circles, and follow immediately with a clean dry cloth. Soap residue left behind also attracts dust, so the streaks return darker within weeks.

How do I clean the grey area around a door handle?

That is hand oil, not dust, so soap alone smears it. Use a drop of dish soap on a damp cloth worked in small circles, then rinse with a clean damp cloth and dry. Expect two passes to even out the transition to the clean part of the door.

Is a magic eraser safe on walls?

On semi-gloss and gloss, yes, with light pressure. On flat or matte paint, no — it is a fine abrasive and removes the texture, leaving a lighter patch. Use it on baseboards and door bottoms instead, where the sheen matters less.

Can I wash walls with vinegar?

For light soil, a dilute vinegar solution is fine on satin and gloss. It does nothing for grease, which is the main soil on kitchen walls, and it does not remove paint transfer or scuffs. Dish soap at low concentration is a better general choice.

How do I clean textured or popcorn walls?

Do not wet them. Vacuum with a brush attachment and use a dry microfiber cloth, because moisture softens the texture and any rubbing breaks it off. For stains, blot with the minimum moisture and accept that some marks cannot be removed without damaging the texture.
