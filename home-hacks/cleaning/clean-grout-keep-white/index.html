How to Clean Grout and Keep It White Longer

Home Hacks Cleaning Published October 14, 2026 7 min read
Advertisement

Unsanded tile grout is essentially cement — a porous, alkaline material. That porosity is why grout stains: dirt, soap scum, and body oils penetrate into the material rather than sitting on top of it. Scrubbing the surface removes almost none of it.

The method that works: an oxygen bleach (sodium percarbonate) solution that penetrates the pores and oxidizes the stain, applied with enough dwell time, followed by mechanical agitation and a full rinse. For sealing afterward — which is what keeps grout white long-term — use a penetrating sealer.

Two distinctions matter more than technique:

  1. Oxygen bleach, not chlorine bleach. Oxygen bleach is safe on colored grout and does not produce the fumes or the damage that chlorine bleach does.
  2. Sealing determines the outcome. Cleaning grout without sealing it means repeating the job in three months. Sealing is what makes grout stay white.

The critical check: Before applying anything, verify the tile is not natural stone. Acid-based cleaners (vinegar, citric acid, some commercial products) etch marble, travertine, and limestone permanently. If your tile is natural stone, use only pH-neutral cleaners.

Test on an inconspicuous area — behind a toilet or in a corner — before treating the whole floor.

Step 1: Remove Loose Dirt and Dry-Dust

Applying cleaner over loose dirt means the dirt mixes with the solution and gets pushed deeper into the grout pores.

  1. Vacuum or sweep the floor thoroughly, including along the grout lines.
  2. If you are cleaning wall tile, dust it first with a dry microfiber cloth.
  3. Let the surface dry. Wet grout absorbs less of the cleaning solution.

Step 2: Make the Oxygen Bleach Solution

  1. Mix 2 tablespoons of oxygen bleach powder with 2 cups of warm water. Stir until fully dissolved — undissolved granules sit on the grout and can leave marks.
  2. Add 1 tablespoon of dish soap. This helps the solution cling to vertical surfaces and slightly breaks down grease.
  3. For extra clinging power on walls, add 1 teaspoon of baking soda to thicken the mixture.
  4. Transfer the solution to a spray bottle or a squeeze bottle with a narrow tip for precise application along grout lines.

Note on concentrations: For light cleaning, 1 tablespoon per 2 cups is sufficient. For heavy or blackened grout, use 3–4 tablespoons per 2 cups. More is not unlimited — over-concentrated solutions can lighten colored grout.

Step 3: Apply and Let It Dwell

This step is what separates cleaning grout from scrubbing grout. Dwell time does most of the work.

  1. Apply the solution along the grout lines using the spray bottle or squeeze bottle. Be generous — the grout must be saturated, not damp.
  2. Do not spray the entire floor at once. Work in sections of roughly 4 feet by 4 feet so nothing dries before you reach it.
  3. Let it sit for 10–15 minutes. For heavily soiled grout, leave it 20–30 minutes.
  4. If it starts to dry, reapply. A dry oxygen bleach solution stops working. This is the most common reason the method appears to fail.
  5. For wall tile, apply in 3-foot sections so the solution does not run down or dry out.

Safety note: Wear gloves and ventilate the room. Oxygen bleach is far gentler than chlorine bleach, but prolonged skin contact still causes irritation.

Step 4: Agitate with the Right Brush

  1. Use a stiff nylon grout brush — narrow, with stiff bristles designed to get into the line.
  2. Never use a wire brush. Metal bristles scratch grout and, on soft tile, the tile itself.
  3. Scrub along the grout lines, not across them. A back-and-forth motion along the line is more effective and less likely to chip the tile edge.
  4. Work in short strokes, concentrating on the dirtiest sections.
  5. Do not over-scrub. If the stain is not lifting after 30 seconds of scrubbing, add more solution and more dwell time rather than scrubbing harder. Aggressive scrubbing erodes grout.

Step 5: Rinse Thoroughly

Residual cleaner left in grout attracts dirt and can leave a white haze.

  1. Mop or wipe with clean water. For floors, use a mop rinsed frequently.
  2. Change the water often. Dirty rinse water redistributes the residue you just lifted.
  3. For wall tile, use a clean damp cloth and rinse it frequently.
  4. Inspect the grout lines. A hazy white film means incomplete rinsing — rinse that section again.
  5. Dry with a clean towel or let it air dry with ventilation.

Step 6: Seal the Grout (The Step That Matters Most)

Sealing is what keeps grout white. Unsealed cement grout absorbs everything that touches it. Sealed grout resists penetration, and future cleaning takes a fraction of the time.

Test whether you need to seal

  1. Drip a few drops of water onto the grout.
  2. If the water beads up → the grout is still sealed, no need to reseal yet.
  3. If the water soaks in and darkens the grout → it is unsealed or the sealer has worn off. Seal it.

How to seal

  1. The grout must be completely clean and dry. Sealing over dirt traps the dirt, and sealing over moisture traps the moisture. Wait at least 24 hours after cleaning.
  2. Choose a penetrating sealer, not a topical sealer. Penetrating sealers soak in and block absorption; topical sealers sit on the surface and peel.
  3. Apply with a small foam brush or an applicator bottle designed for grout lines.
  4. Work in small sections — roughly 3 feet at a time.
  5. Let it absorb for the time the label specifies (usually 5–15 minutes).
  6. Wipe off the excess from the tile surface with a clean dry cloth. Excess sealer on tile leaves a hazy film.
  7. Allow 24–72 hours to cure before exposing the area to water. Check the label.
  8. Reseal every 6–12 months, or when the water-drop test shows absorption again.

Note on epoxy grout: epoxy grout does not need sealing. It is non-porous by nature.

Step 7: For Very Stubborn or Blackened Grout

If oxygen bleach and dwell time do not resolve it:

Option A — stronger oxygen bleach concentration: Increase to 4 tablespoons per 2 cups and extend dwell time to 45 minutes. Cover the grout with plastic wrap to prevent drying.

Option B — baking soda paste: Mix baking soda with just enough water to form a thick paste. Apply along the grout line, let it sit 15 minutes, then scrub. The abrasive action helps with surface staining.

Option C — commercial grout cleaner: Products containing sulfamic acid or phosphoric acid are effective but aggressive. Never use them on natural stone tile. Test in a hidden area, ventilate, wear gloves and eye protection, and rinse thoroughly.

Option D — accept that some grout is permanently stained: Grout that has absorbed oil-based stains for years may not return to its original white. In that case, grout colorant or grout paint is a legitimate option — it coats the grout line and provides a uniform color, and it also seals.

The key insight is that grout is porous, not dirty on the surface. That is why scrubbing hard with a general-purpose cleaner produces disappointing results. The stain is inside the material, and only a chemical agent with sufficient dwell time can reach it.

Maintaining White Grout

  1. Squeegee shower walls after every use. This alone prevents most bathroom grout staining.
  2. Seal grout every 6–12 months. Test with a water drop to know when.
  3. Clean grout monthly with a diluted oxygen bleach solution — five minutes of prevention beats an hour of restoration.
  4. Use a pH-neutral floor cleaner for regular mopping. Alkaline and acidic cleaners both degrade sealers over time.
  5. Address spills immediately, especially oil, wine, and coffee. Grout absorbs quickly and permanently.
  6. Keep a grout brush handy for spot cleaning. Spot cleaning takes seconds; whole-floor cleaning takes hours.
  7. Ventilate bathrooms to prevent mold in grout — mold in grout is a moisture problem, not a cleaning problem.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best way to clean grout lines?

A solution of 2 tablespoons of oxygen bleach powder in 2 cups of warm water, applied along the lines with a squeeze bottle, left to dwell 10–30 minutes, then agitated with a stiff nylon grout brush and rinsed thoroughly. The dwell time is what makes it work — brushing alone cannot reach stains inside the grout.

Does vinegar clean grout?

Poorly, and it can cause damage. Vinegar is an acid, and grout is alkaline cement-based material — repeated acid exposure degrades grout over time. Vinegar also etches natural stone tile permanently. Oxygen bleach is the appropriate cleaner for grout.

Can I use bleach on grout?

Chlorine bleach kills mold and whitens white grout, but it strips the color from colored grout permanently and produces irritating fumes in enclosed bathrooms. Oxygen bleach achieves similar stain-lifting without those drawbacks and is the safer choice.

How do I keep grout white after cleaning?

Seal it with a penetrating sealer and reseal every 6–12 months. Sealing blocks the absorption that causes staining in the first place. Test with a water drop — if water soaks in, it is time to reseal.

Why does my grout keep getting dirty so fast?

Because it is unsealed. Cement grout is porous and absorbs whatever touches it. Once sealed, dirt stays on the surface and wipes away. If you have cleaned grout repeatedly without sealing, that is the missing step.

Should I use a steam cleaner on grout?

Steam can work on ceramic tile grout and avoids chemicals, but the risk of damaging sealer and, on some grout, loosening it, is real. If you use steam, keep the nozzle moving, do not hold it against one spot, and expect to reseal sooner. Do not use steam on natural stone.

How do I clean grout on a natural stone floor?

Use only pH-neutral cleaners. Acidic cleaners (vinegar, citric acid, oxygen bleach) etch marble, travertine, and limestone permanently — there is no recovery. Test any product in an inconspicuous area, and expect grout on natural stone to be more difficult to whiten.

Can permanently stained grout be fixed?

Sometimes not by cleaning. Grout that has absorbed oil-based stains over years may not return to its original color. In that case, grout colorant or grout paint, applied after thorough cleaning and drying, restores a uniform appearance and provides sealing at the same time.
