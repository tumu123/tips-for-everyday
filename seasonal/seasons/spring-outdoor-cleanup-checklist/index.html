Spring Outdoor Cleanup: What to Do First

Seasonal & Holiday Seasons Published October 19, 2026 10 min read

Spring outdoor work fails for one reason: it gets done in the wrong order.

The typical sequence is to wash the siding on the first warm Saturday, then discover in April that the downspout has been dumping water against that same wall all winter, that the mulch has washed out, and that the paint is failing at the bottom two feet. Now the washing has to be redone, and the wall has a soft spot that was invisible while it was dirty.

The correct order is not intuitive, but it is simple: water first, structure second, cleaning third, plants last. Everything downstream depends on the step before it.

Step 1: Read the water before you touch anything

Walk the perimeter slowly, in the rain if you can, and look for where winter’s water went. This fifteen-minute walk determines what the rest of the list should contain.

What to look for:

  • Stained or eroded mulch in a line below a downspout — that is the discharge point, and it is too close to the wall.
  • Soil piled against the siding or the rim joist — a common result of winter plowing and bed edging.
  • Standing water marks or a silt line on the foundation, which shows the high-water mark from snowmelt.
  • Ruts in the lawn leading away from a low spot, which traces the actual drainage path.
  • Ice damage: spalled concrete, cracked pavers, a heaved walkway, a bent gutter section.

Photograph what you find. The list you build here is the actual work plan; the rest of this article is the order to execute it in.

Step 2: Clear the roof drainage first

Nothing else in the yard matters if the roof is still discharging at the foundation. Do the gutters before the beds, the siding, or the windows.

  • Scoop and flush, working from the far end toward the downspout. Spring debris is seed pods, catkins, shingle grit, and the leaves that blew in after your fall cleaning.
  • Check the downspout flow at the bottom, not just at the top. A downspout that trickles is a downspout that will overflow in the first heavy rain.
  • Extend discharge 4 to 6 feet from the wall and confirm it points downhill, away from the house and off any walkway.
  • Look at the fascia behind the gutter. Peeling paint or soft wood there means water has been getting behind the gutter for a while.

Step 3: Fix the grading, which is the actual fix

Downspout extensions buy you a few feet. Grading buys you the house.

The standard is 6 inches of fall over the first 10 feet away from the foundation — roughly a 5% slope. Most settled yards have less, and some have negative slope where beds were built up against the wall.

The workable corrections, cheapest first:

  • Add soil to create the slope. Use a mix that drains, not pure topsoil, and keep the finished grade at least 6 inches below the siding so the wall can dry.
  • Re-cut the bed edge so mulch sits below the siding rather than against it.
  • A shallow swale — a broad, gently sloped depression — can redirect surface water around the house rather than through the basement.
  • Never fill against the foundation above the sill plate. Raising grade is the fastest way to rot a rim joist.

If water still enters the basement after grading, the next step is a French drain or a sump, not more soil.

Step 4: Inspect the roof and envelope from the ground

You do not need to get on the roof. Binoculars from the lawn plus a walk around the perimeter cover most of what matters in spring.

  • Shingles: lifted, missing, or creased tabs, especially on south and west slopes where freeze-thaw does the most work.
  • Flashing: at the chimney, the skylight, and the roof-wall junction. Look for rust streaks or a lifted edge.
  • Soffit and fascia: stains, peeling paint, or a gap where a bird has moved in.
  • Siding and trim: any spot where paint is bubbling or the material feels soft when pressed.
  • Foundation: new cracks wider than 1/8 inch, or a stair-step crack at a corner.

This is also the moment to check the attic for winter moisture — a stain on the ceiling below the roof deck is the earliest sign of an ice dam leak, and it is much cheaper to fix in April than in July.

Step 5: Caulk and paint before washing, not after

Counterintuitive but correct: seal first, wash second. Washing forces water into exactly the gaps you are about to seal, and caulk applied over a wet joint fails within a season.

  • Caulk when the temperature will stay above 40�F (4�C) for 24 hours, and when rain is not forecast. Most exterior caulks will not cure below that.
  • Cut out old caulk rather than caulking over it. New caulk over failing caulk peels within a year.
  • Use the right type: paintable acrylic latex for siding and trim joints, silicone for metal and glass, polyurethane for masonry and high-movement joints.
  • Paint when temperatures are between 50 and 85�F, and not in direct sun on a hot afternoon. Paint applied to a hot surface skins over before it bonds.

Step 6: Wash the siding from the bottom up, rinse from the top down

Washing order matters because dirty water runs down. Start at the lowest point and work upward in sections, then rinse the whole wall from the top so streaks do not set.

  • Use a garden hose, a soft brush on an extension pole, and a mild detergent. Most exterior dirt is biological — algae, mold, pollen — and a diluted bleach solution (1 part bleach to 3 parts water) with a surfactant removes it better than pressure.
  • Pressure washing: 1,200 psi maximum on wood and vinyl, with a wide fan tip, held at least 12 inches away. Higher pressure drives water behind the siding and strips paint.
  • Never pressure wash window seals, door seals, or vents. Forced water inside a wall shows up as rot two years later.
  • Rinse plants before and after, and cover anything you cannot move. Bleach solution kills grass.
  • Work top-down for the rinse, one wall at a time, so the wall can dry completely before you move on.

Step 7: Do the windows, screens, and tracks

This is the detail work that makes a house look cared for, and it takes a fraction of the time of the siding.

  • Screens out, washed with a soft brush and hose, dried flat. Never scrub screens with a stiff brush; the mesh deforms and never sits flat again.
  • Tracks vacuumed first, then wiped — a wet paper towel in a dry track just makes mud. A shop vacuum with a crevice tool is the right tool.
  • Weep holes cleared. These small slots at the bottom of the frame drain water that gets behind the sash; a blocked weep hole is why a window sill rots.
  • Glass on a cloudy day. Sun on warm glass dries the cleaner before you wipe, which is what leaves streaks.

Step 8: Inspect and reseal the deck

A deck is the exterior structure most likely to be neglected and most expensive to replace. Spring is when you can see the damage.

  • Check the ledger board where the deck attaches to the house. Soft wood, missing flashing, or rusted bolts are structural, not cosmetic.
  • Test the boards with a screwdriver. If it sinks in easily, the board is rotting from the inside.
  • Push on the railings. A railing that moves means a loose post, and a loose post is a fall hazard.
  • Clean with an oxygenated deck cleaner, not bleach, which damages wood fibers. Let it dry 48 hours.
  • Seal when the wood is dry and the temperature is 50 to 80�F, and not in direct sun. Sealer applied to damp wood traps moisture and peels.
  • Never paint a deck that was previously sealed without stripping. Paint over sealer fails within a season.

Step 9: Wait for the soil before you touch the beds

The most common spring gardening mistake is working soil too early, and the damage is invisible until midsummer.

  • Do not walk on or dig in wet soil. Compaction from one pass in March can take years to undo. Test by squeezing a handful: if it holds a ball and drips, wait.
  • Wait for soil temperature around 50�F (10�C) before planting or applying mulch. Cold soil stalls root growth and mulch insulates it further.
  • Cut back perennials when you see new growth at the crown, not on a calendar date. The old stems protect the crown through late frosts.
  • Leave the leaf litter a little longer. Many pollinators overwinter in stems and leaf litter; a mid-April cleanup is better than a March one.
  • Edge the beds, then mulch to 2 to 3 inches, keeping mulch off the crowns and away from the siding.

Step 10: Service the equipment before the season starts

The tools fail in May if they are not prepared in April.

  • Mower: oil, spark plug, air filter, and a sharpened blade. A dull blade tears grass and invites disease.
  • Irrigation: run each zone and look for a broken head or a geyser. Check the backflow preventer.
  • Hose and spigots: turn the outdoor supply on slowly and check for a split from the winter freeze.
  • Air conditioning: clean the condenser coil, clear debris from around the unit, and replace the filter. Book a service call in March if you use one; schedules fill by May.

Why This Works

The order follows dependency, not preference. Drainage determines where water goes; sealing determines whether water gets in; cleaning only looks right if both are done first; and planting only succeeds if the soil is ready.

Doing it in this sequence converts a list of chores into a single pass around the house. Done in the intuitive order — wash, plant, then notice the drainage — every task gets done twice and the underlying problem survives the season.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should be done first in spring yard cleanup?

Roof drainage. Clear the gutters and confirm the downspouts discharge 4 to 6 feet from the wall before doing anything else. Then correct the grading, seal and caulk, wash the siding, and only then work on the beds. Cleaning before drainage means cleaning twice.

When should you start spring cleanup?

When the ground is workable and the risk of hard freeze has passed, but before plants break dormancy. Do structural work — gutters, grading, caulk — as soon as the temperature holds above 40�F. Wait for soil around 50�F before planting or mulching.

How do you wash house siding properly?

Wash bottom-up in sections with a garden hose, soft brush, and mild detergent, then rinse top-down. Use a bleach solution (1:3) for algae and mildew. If pressure washing, stay at or below 1,200 psi on wood and vinyl with a wide fan tip held 12 inches away, and never direct it at seals or vents.

When can you reseal a deck?

When the wood is thoroughly dry — at least 48 hours after cleaning — and temperatures are between 50 and 80�F, out of direct sun. Sealer applied to damp wood or in direct sun fails within a season. Never paint over a previously sealed deck without stripping first.

Should you remove leaves and stems in spring or fall?

Leave them through winter and cut back perennials when you see new growth at the crown in spring. Stems and leaf litter shelter pollinators and protect crowns from late frosts. Clean up the beds in mid-April rather than March in most climates.

How do you fix water pooling near the foundation?

Create a 6-inch fall over the first 10 feet away from the wall, extend downspouts 4 to 6 feet, and keep the finished grade at least 6 inches below the siding. If water still enters the basement, the next step is a French drain or sump, not more topsoil.

What spring maintenance prevents summer problems?

Clean AC condenser coils and change the filter before the first hot week; service the mower (oil, plug, sharp blade); test every irrigation zone; and check the attic for winter moisture stains. These are the items that turn into emergency calls in June.
