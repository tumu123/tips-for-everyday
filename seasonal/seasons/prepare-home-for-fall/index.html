How to Prepare Your Home for Fall

Seasonal & Holiday Seasons Published October 16, 2026 11 min read

Fall is the highest-value maintenance season of the year for one reason: the damage you prevent shows up in winter, and winter damage is expensive. A clogged gutter that costs 30 minutes in October becomes ice dams, rotted fascia, and water in the basement by February.

There’s also a deadline that doesn’t exist in other seasons. Once the first hard freeze arrives, caulk won’t cure, exterior paint won’t dry, and you can’t safely clean gutters. Fall work must be done before that date, not on a convenient weekend after it.

Step 1: Tackle the gutters and drainage first

Everything else depends on water being directed away from the house.

  1. Clean gutters after most leaves have fallen but before the first hard freeze. Timing matters — too early and they refill, too late and the debris is frozen in place.
  2. Remove debris by hand or with a scoop, not by flushing into the downspouts. Flushing debris down a downspout creates a clog you’ll find in the worst place.
  3. Then flush each downspout with a hose and confirm water flows freely at the outlet.
  4. Check the gutter slope. Water should flow toward downspouts, not pool. Pooling water in a gutter indicates a sagging section or a clogged section.
  5. Seal leaking seams and joints with gutter sealant while the gutters are dry and accessible.
  6. Add or clear downspout extensions. Water should discharge 4 to 6 feet from the foundation, not against it. This single item prevents a large share of basement water problems.
  7. Check the grading around the foundation. Soil should slope away from the house. Add soil where it has settled toward the house.
  8. Look at the roof from the ground for damaged, lifted, or missing shingles — and check flashing around chimneys and vents.

An inch of rain on a roof is hundreds of gallons — fall gutter and yard cleanup covers the drainage order.

Step 2: Air-seal before you insulate

Air sealing always comes first. Insulation slows heat conduction; air sealing stops the convective loss that moves far more heat.

  1. Find air leaks with a flashlight in the attic during daylight, or feel for drafts on a cold day. Check where the wall meets the ceiling, around recessed lights, around any penetration, and at the top of the foundation.
  2. Seal around windows and doors with caulk inside and out. Re-caulk anywhere the existing bead has cracked or pulled away.
  3. Seal utility penetrations — pipes, cables, ducts, and vents entering from outside.
  4. Weatherstrip all exterior doors so they seal on all four sides.
  5. Replace or adjust door sweeps that no longer contact the threshold.
  6. Seal the attic hatch or access door with weatherstripping and insulate the back of it.
  7. Seal the rim joist area in the basement. This is where a lot of air movement happens and it’s almost always unsealed.

Note on the plastic-window-film debate: shrink film works and is genuinely useful for single-pane windows you can’t replace. It is not a substitute for sealing air leaks, and it does nothing for the windows themselves. Do air sealing first; add film only for windows that remain cold. The cheap options and what they actually save are covered in How to Lower Your Heating Bill.

The seams that leak heat are the seams that admit insects. For the species that uses them as a winter shelter, see air sealing before the cold arrives.

Air sealing is usually framed as a heating measure. For the insect that uses the same opening as a winter shelter, and the four-week window to close it, see the gap above the soffit line.

Step 3: Handle the heating system before you need it

The first cold snap is the worst possible time to discover a problem.

  1. Change the furnace filter. This is the cheapest, highest-impact item on the list. A dirty filter restricts airflow, which reduces efficiency and can trip the system.
  2. Schedule a professional service if you haven’t in over a year. They check the heat exchanger, burners, and blower — items that aren’t DIY-safe.
  3. Test the heating system before the first cold night. Run it for 20 minutes on a mild day so you find problems on your schedule.
  4. Vacuum the vents and returns. Dust in the returns gets recirculated through the whole house.
  5. Remove obstructions from around the furnace and check the flue for blockages.
  6. Check the chimney and fireplace if you burn wood. Have the chimney swept if it’s been used for a season; creosote buildup is a fire risk.
  7. Test the thermostat and replace batteries. Consider a programmable thermostat if you don’t have one — the setback schedule is the largest single heating saving available.

Also on the water heater: sediment settles over the year, reducing efficiency. Fall is a good time for a flush — Water Heater Maintenance.

Step 4: Do the exterior work that requires warm weather

These items have a temperature requirement, so they must happen before the freeze.

  1. Paint and caulk exterior wood. Most products need temperatures above 50°F (10°C) to cure properly.
  2. Seal the deck if it needs it — same temperature constraint.
  3. Repair concrete cracks in driveways, walks, and steps. Water entering a crack freezes and expands, turning a hairline crack into a structural one.
  4. Touch up exterior paint on trim, especially at joints where water gets in.
  5. Check and clean exterior light fixtures and replace bulbs; you’ll use them much more in winter.
  6. Drain and store garden hoses and shut off exterior faucets. A hose left connected traps water in the faucet and can split it.
  7. Drain the irrigation system and blow out the lines if you have them.

Step 5: Prepare the yard and the exterior of the house

  1. Rake and remove leaves, especially from the lawn. A thick leaf mat smothers grass over winter and creates a mold habitat.
  2. Cut the lawn slightly shorter for the final mow of the season, but not scalped.
  3. Fall fertilize the lawn — this feeds root growth, which is what winter survival depends on. This is the most important lawn feeding of the year.
  4. Plant spring bulbs before the ground freezes.
  5. Prune dead or damaged branches, especially any overhanging the house or power lines. Snow and ice load will bring them down.
  6. Cut back perennials and remove annuals after the first frost, but consider leaving some stems for pollinators — many overwinter in hollow stems.
  7. Move firewood away from the house and elevate it.
  8. Store outdoor furniture and cushions properly. Cushions must go indoors — see How to Protect Outdoor Furniture in Summer for the storage method, which applies year-round.
  9. Drain and store the mower, service the snow equipment.

Outdoor food sources decide how much pressure the house faces. For the species question — and the bat confusion that changes everything — see rat or mouse, decided by the droppings.

The yard side of the house is where autumn pests stage before they move in. For the bug that gathers by the thousand on one elevation, see handling insects on the exterior wall.

Step 6: Prepare for winter storms before they arrive

Fall is when you buy what you’ll need in a storm, not during one.

  1. Restock emergency supplies: batteries, flashlights, non-perishable food, water, medications, a manual can opener.
  2. Check the snow removal equipment — shovel, snow blower, ice melt. Test the snow blower before the first snow.
  3. Store de-icing materials where you can reach them, not buried in the garage.
  4. Review your winter safety setup: carbon monoxide detectors working, generator fuel stabilized.
  5. Know where your water shutoff is and make sure it operates. If a pipe bursts, knowing this saves thousands — that’s the emergency side of How to Prevent Frozen Pipes.

Step 7: Do the indoor items that make winter easier

  1. Have the chimney and dryer vent cleaned if needed. A clogged dryer vent is a fire risk and gets worse with winter’s heavier laundry loads.
  2. Check the attic insulation level. Most homes in cold climates are under-insulated. Add insulation where you can see the joists.
  3. Reverse ceiling fans to clockwise for winter, pushing warm air down from the ceiling.
  4. Test smoke and CO detectors and replace batteries.
  5. Deep clean and seal windows — a fall clean plus air sealing makes the whole season noticeably more comfortable. See Spring Cleaning Checklist for the methods, which transfer.
  6. Check the basement and crawlspace for moisture and address it before the damp season.

Why This Works

Fall maintenance is fundamentally about the physics of water and heat, and both are governed by the freeze-thaw cycle. Water expands roughly 9 percent on freezing, and that expansion is what destroys materials: it splits pipes, pops concrete, opens caulk joints, and lifts roofing. Every fall task that keeps water out of a material directly prevents that failure.

Gutters illustrate the point. Their job isn’t to move water away for aesthetic reasons — it’s to prevent water from reaching the roof edge and foundation. When gutters clog, water backs up under the shingles, saturates the fascia, and refreezes. The ice dam that forms then forces water further under the roof covering and into the structure. A seasonal cleaning prevents a failure mode that costs thousands.

Air sealing ahead of insulation is the order dictated by how heat moves. Conductive loss through insulation is proportional to the temperature difference; convective loss through a crack moves air directly from inside to outside, bypassing the insulation entirely. A small unsealed gap can lose more heat than a large insulated area gains. This is why air sealing is consistently the first recommendation in energy audits, and why it’s more cost-effective than adding insulation in most homes.

Weatherproofing products have temperature requirements because curing is a chemical reaction, and reactions slow with temperature. Caulk below 50°F doesn’t cure properly and fails within a season. This is why the fall deadline isn’t a preference — the products simply stop working.

Testing heating equipment before it’s needed is about scheduling risk rather than efficiency. Heating systems fail during the first cold snap because that’s when they’re first loaded to full capacity and when demand for repair services peaks. Running the system in advance surfaces latent failures when you have time and options.

Lawn fertilization in fall works because the plant’s priorities change. In spring it’s pushing top growth; in fall it’s storing carbohydrate in the roots. Feeding at that point builds the reserves that determine winter survival and spring green-up, which is why the fall application is the one that matters most.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start preparing my home for fall?

Early fall, before daytime temperatures drop below 50°F. Exterior caulking and painting require warm temperatures to cure, so those items have the hardest deadline. Gutter cleaning is best done after most leaves have fallen but before the first hard freeze.

What is the most important fall home maintenance task?

Cleaning gutters and ensuring water drains away from the foundation. A clogged gutter leads to ice dams, rotted fascia, and basement water — the most expensive category of winter damage, and the easiest to prevent.

Should I insulate or air seal first?

Air seal first. Convective loss through gaps bypasses insulation entirely, so sealing leaks typically saves more than adding insulation for the same effort and cost.

Do I really need to change my furnace filter in fall?

Yes. It's the cheapest maintenance item with the largest effect. A restricted filter reduces airflow, lowers efficiency, and can cause the system to trip or overheat.

How do I stop ice dams?

Keep gutters clear, ensure the attic is properly insulated and ventilated so the roof deck stays cold, and seal air leaks into the attic. Ice dams form when the roof is warm enough to melt snow that then refreezes at the cold eaves.

Should I disconnect garden hoses in fall?

Yes, always. A hose left connected traps water in the faucet and supply line, and freezing splits the pipe. Disconnect, drain, and shut off the interior supply valve.

What temperature is too cold for exterior caulking?

Below 50°F (10°C) for most products. Check the specific product — some allow lower temperatures, but cure time extends significantly and adhesion suffers.

Is fall fertilization really better than spring for lawns?

For root development, yes. Fall feeding supports the root growth and carbohydrate storage that determine winter survival, while spring feeding mainly pushes top growth. The fall application is the more valuable of the two.
