How to Keep a House Safe in Extreme Heat Without AC

Seasonal & Holiday Seasons Published October 24, 2026 11 min read

Most advice about heat in the home is about comfort: shade the windows, run the fans, avoid the oven. All of it is reasonable, and none of it is the point when the outdoor temperature stays above body temperature for days and there is no air conditioning.

The distinction that matters is between a house that is uncomfortable and a house that is dangerous. A healthy adult in a 90°F room is miserable. An eighty-year-old on a diuretic in the same room, on the third day, with the windows shut for security, is in a medical situation. The measures that make the first person comfortable do not necessarily protect the second, and some of them actively fail.

What Heat Actually Does

The body dumps heat in four ways: radiation to cooler surroundings, convection to cooler air, conduction to something cooler that it touches, and evaporation of sweat. As air temperature approaches skin temperature — roughly 95°F — the first two stop working, and evaporation becomes the only remaining route.

That is why humidity is decisive. Sweat only cools if it evaporates, and in humid air it does not. A 95°F day at 30 percent humidity is survivable; the same temperature at 70 percent humidity is a different hazard entirely, because the body’s last cooling mechanism has been removed.

This is also why electric fans have a limit. A fan moves air across the skin, which helps evaporation — but when the air is hotter than the skin, the fan is blowing heat onto a body that is trying to shed it. Above roughly 95°F, especially at high humidity, a fan can make things worse rather than better. It is still useful for moving air out of a house at night, which is a different job.

Know the Signs

Heat illness is a progression, and the two stages that matter are treated very differently.

StageSignsWhat to do
Heat exhaustionHeavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache, cool clammy skin, crampsMove to a cool place, loosen clothing, cool with water and airflow, sip fluids, rest
Heat strokeConfusion, slurred speech, hot skin, no sweating (not always), fainting, temperature at or above 104°FEmergency. Call for help, begin cooling immediately with cold water, do not wait

The dividing line is mental state. Someone with heat exhaustion is uncomfortable but thinking clearly. Someone whose behaviour is off — confused, agitated, unsteady, not making sense — is a heat stroke case until proven otherwise, and cooling has to start before the ambulance arrives. Cold water immersion is the fastest method; a bath, a shower, or repeatedly soaked towels with a fan will do if immersion is not possible.

Who Is Actually at Risk

The people most likely to be harmed are usually not the ones complaining about the heat.

GroupWhy the risk is higher
Adults over 65Reduced thirst response, reduced sweating, more chronic medication
Infants and young childrenPoor thermoregulation, cannot remove clothing or get water themselves
People on diuretics, beta blockers, antihistamines, or antipsychoticsMany impair sweating or fluid balance
People with heart, kidney, or lung diseaseLess capacity to compensate
People with limited mobilityCannot move to a cooler room, cannot open a window
People living aloneNobody notices the deterioration
Outdoor workers and athletesCumulative exposure, not a single hot afternoon

Two practical consequences. Check on the vulnerable in person, twice a day during a heat wave, not by text — a confused person will reply “fine”. And treat medication as part of the heat plan: several common drug classes reduce the ability to sweat, and some medications need to be stored below 86°F, which a house without AC will exceed.

Defend One Room, Not the Whole House

Cooling an entire house without mechanical cooling is a losing project. Cooling one room is achievable, and sleeping in it is what gets a household through a multi-day event.

Choose the room by these criteria, in order:

  1. Lowest floor available. Heat stratifies: an upstairs bedroom can be 8 to 12°F hotter than the ground floor.
  2. North or east facing. Afternoon sun on a west wall turns that room into an oven regardless of outside air.
  3. Fewest and smallest windows.
  4. A door that closes, so cooled air stays where it is put.
  5. Near water and a toilet, because moving through a hot house at night defeats the purpose.

Then defend it: keep the door closed during the day, block the sun before it enters, and add airflow only when the air in the room is cooler than the body. If the room has a window that faces away from the sun, opening it at night with a fan pulling air through is the single most effective passive cooling move available.

Work With the Daily Cycle

A house has a thermal rhythm, and the whole game is to keep outdoor heat out during the day and flush indoor heat out at night.

During the day: close windows, close blinds and curtains, and block the sun on the outside of the glass if possible — an exterior shade or a reflective panel works far better than an interior blind, because the heat has already crossed the glass by the time it reaches a curtain.

At night, when the outdoor temperature drops below the indoor temperature: open windows on opposite sides of the house and put a fan in one of them, blowing out. Exhausting hot air is more effective than blowing cool air in, because it uses the pressure difference rather than fighting it. Close everything again by mid-morning.

Do not open the house in the afternoon because it feels stuffy. That is the moment when outdoor air is at its hottest, and the humidity that comes with it is worse than the stale air it replaces.

Cool the Person, Not Just the Air

Physiological cooling is faster and cheaper than cooling a room, and it is the part people skip.

  • Cool water on the skin beats cool air. A tepid shower, a basin of water for hands and forearms, or a wet cloth on the neck, wrists, armpits, and groin cools the blood that is returning to the core.
  • Feet in cold water is disproportionately effective and easy to sustain while sitting.
  • Damp sheet plus a fan is the classic low-tech solution for sleep: the evaporation does the cooling, and it works even when the air is warm, provided the air is not saturated.
  • Loose, light, single-layer clothing — and remove it when sleeping alone. Cotton holds moisture and cools; synthetics that trap heat do not.
  • Eat cold, cook nothing. Every oven, hob, kettle, and dishwasher adds heat to the house and moisture to the air.

Hydration, With a Caveat

Drinking water is necessary and, on its own, not sufficient. Sweating loses salt as well as water, and someone who drinks several litres of plain water while sweating heavily can end up with low sodium — which presents as headache, nausea, and confusion, and looks like heat illness while being something else.

The practical version: drink before you are thirsty, eat something salty with the water, and use an electrolyte drink if sweating heavily for hours. Avoid alcohol, which increases fluid loss, and be careful with caffeine in large amounts. For infants, formula or breast milk is the fluid; extra water is not appropriate in the first months.

Sleep Strategy

Nights are where heat waves do their damage, because sleep is when the body recovers and when nobody is watching for symptoms.

  • Sleep on the lowest floor, in the defended room, even if that means a mattress on the floor for a few nights.
  • Start the night cooler than you need it. Flush the room at nightfall, then close it before dawn.
  • Two cool showers — one before bed, one in the middle of the night if you wake up hot.
  • A damp towel over the chest with a fan on low is more effective than a fan alone.
  • Do not swaddle infants. The risk in a hot room is overheating, not catching cold.

Fans: Where They Help and Where They Do Not

SituationFan use
Outdoor air cooler than indoor, nightExcellent — exhaust hot air out of one window
Indoor air below about 90°F, low humidityHelpful — increases evaporation
Indoor air above 95°F, high humidityLittle benefit, possibly harmful — do not aim at people
Room closed with no air exchangeUseless — it moves hot air around
Anyone with heat exhaustionUseful as part of evaporative cooling with wet skin

A fan is a tool for moving air, not for making it colder. The exception is when it is moving air out of the house at night, and that is the job it does best.

When to Leave the House

Leaving is the correct answer more often than people accept. Go somewhere with cooling — a library, a mall, a cooling centre, a friend’s house, a cinema — for the hottest hours, and treat that as part of the plan rather than an overreaction.

Leave for the duration when: indoor temperature stays above 90°F through the night; anyone in the household is in a high-risk group and cannot be kept cool; the power is out and the outdoor heat is extreme; or someone shows signs of heat exhaustion that do not resolve within an hour of cooling.

A heat wave that overlaps with a power outage is the worst case, and it needs a plan made before it happens rather than during — preparing for a power outage covers the sequence, including the one room to keep habitable and how to decide when to leave.

After the Heat Breaks

Three things to check once the temperature drops.

The house itself: look for warped doors, cracks at the ceiling line, or a swollen floor — sustained heat and humidity move materials, and those are the places that show it.

The food: a refrigerator above 40°F for more than four hours, or a freezer that has thawed and refrozen, needs to be assessed rather than assumed. The temperature rules are in the temperature danger zone.

The people: the second day of recovery is when the effects of dehydration and poor sleep show up. Keep drinking, and keep checking on the vulnerable for a day or two after the heat ends.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what indoor temperature is a house dangerous?

Above about 90°F sustained, especially overnight, risk rises sharply for older adults, infants, and anyone with chronic illness. Above 95°F, a fan no longer helps and the body's evaporative cooling starts to fail in humid conditions. There is no single number — humidity, duration, and who is in the house all change it.

Do fans help in extreme heat?

Below about 90 to 95°F, yes. Above that, particularly with high humidity, a fan aimed at a person can add heat rather than remove it. The reliable use of a fan in extreme heat is moving air out of the house at night, not blowing it at people during the day.

Is it better to open windows at night or keep the house closed?

Open at night when outdoor air is cooler than indoor air, and close by mid-morning. Leaving windows open into the afternoon lets in the hottest, most humid air of the day, which is worse than the stale air it replaces.

How do I cool a room with no AC and one window?

Block the sun from outside the glass during the day, keep the door closed, and at night put a fan in the window blowing out to exhaust the hot air. For the person, use wet skin and airflow — a damp sheet with a fan cools far more effectively than the fan alone.

What is the fastest way to cool someone down?

Cold water immersion, if it is available and the person is conscious. Otherwise, soak towels in cool water and apply them to the neck, armpits, and groin while moving air over the body. Do not wait for emergency services to begin cooling someone with signs of heat stroke.

Can I use a basement as a cool room?

Yes, and it is often the best option — basements stay close to ground temperature. Check for damp and ventilation first, and make sure the route down is safe at night. A dehumidifier helps comfort but adds heat, so run it during the day rather than at night.

How do I keep a baby or toddler cool?

Light clothing, frequent feeding, cool baths, and a room on the lowest floor with airflow. Do not cover the cot with a blanket in a hot room, and do not rely on a fan aimed directly at an infant for long periods. Check the back of the neck rather than the hands to judge temperature.
