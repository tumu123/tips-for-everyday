The First Frost Checklist

Seasonal & Holiday Seasons Published October 24, 2026 11 min read

The first frost of the year is the only deadline in the garden that cannot be negotiated. Everything else — pruning, mulching, dividing — can slide a week without consequence. Frost arrives on a specific night, and whatever is still outside at dawn is either protected or damaged.

The good news is that a frost forecast almost always arrives with a day or two of warning, and that a day or two is enough. What follows is the order to do things in, because doing the right tasks in the wrong order wastes the window: there is no point covering a bed you have not harvested, and no point watering a plant you are about to cut down.

Step 0: Read the forecast properly

“Frost” and “freeze” are used interchangeably in conversation and mean different things in practice.

TermTemperatureWhat it does
Frost advisoryAir 33 to 36°F, clear sky, light windGround surfaces can drop to 32°F; tender plants damaged
Light freeze29 to 32°FTender annuals killed, hardy plants injured
Moderate freeze25 to 28°FMost annuals and many perennials damaged; root crops usually fine
Hard freeze24°F or below for 4 or more hoursSoil begins to freeze at the surface, hoses and exposed pipes at risk

Three things in the forecast matter as much as the number:

The dew point. The closer the dew point is to the air temperature, the more likely frost forms — moisture condensing on a leaf releases heat, but the same moisture settling as ice is what damages tissue. A dry, clear night with a low dew point is the classic frost setup.

Wind. A light breeze mixes the air and can prevent frost; a dead calm night with clear skies is the worst case, because the ground radiates heat away undisturbed.

The minimum time. A low of 34°F at 6 a.m. is a frost event at ground level. A low of 34°F for one hour is not the same as five hours at 29°F, and the duration is what decides damage.

Put an outdoor thermometer at ground level, not on the wall of the house, and check it at dawn. The number that matters is the one two inches above the soil, where the plants actually are.

Step 1: Water deeply the day before

This is the step that surprises people, and it works for two reasons.

Wet soil holds far more heat than dry soil, and it releases that heat slowly overnight — a watered bed can be 2 to 4°F warmer at the surface than a dry one at dawn. And a well-hydrated plant tolerates cold better than a drought-stressed one, because its cells are fully turgid rather than already struggling.

Water in the morning or early afternoon, not at dusk. Wet foliage overnight is an invitation to disease, and the soil does not need to be wet on the surface to do the job. The target is deep moisture at the root zone, not a damp mulch layer.

Step 2: Harvest what will not survive

Pick before you protect. Anything that is going to be destroyed by the cold should be in the kitchen rather than under a sheet.

Harvest now: tomatoes (including the green ones — they ripen off the vine), peppers, aubergines, beans, cucumbers, courgettes, basil, and any tender herbs.

Can stay a while: kale, chard, spinach, cabbage, carrots, parsnips, leeks, and Brussels sprouts all improve or hold through light frosts. Root crops in the ground are usually the safest food on the property, and many are sweeter after a frost.

Green tomatoes are worth picking even if you have no plan for them; they ripen on a tray indoors over a few weeks, and the alternative is losing them overnight.

One detail: harvest basil entirely, at the stem, before the frost. It is the most cold-sensitive common herb — damaged at temperatures well above freezing — and it does not recover.

Step 3: Cover, with the right material

A cover works by trapping the heat that the soil releases overnight. That means it must go on before sunset, not at midnight, because there is no soil heat left to trap by then.

What works:

  • Row cover fabric, which is designed for this and transmits light and water.
  • Old cotton sheets and lightweight blankets, which are the standard household substitute.
  • Straw or mulch over root crops and low perennials.
  • Cloches, upturned buckets, or large plant pots over individual plants.

What does not work, or does more harm than good:

  • Plastic sheeting in contact with foliage. Plastic conducts cold to whatever it touches and the leaves freeze where they meet it. If plastic is all you have, drape it over a frame so it never touches the plant, and remove it in the morning.
  • Covering wet foliage. Shake the water off first, or cover earlier.
  • Sealing a cover to the ground completely on a sunny morning. Air it out, or the plants cook.

Hold covers down with stones, clips, or hoops. A cover that blows off at 3 a.m. has done nothing, and that is the most common failure of an otherwise correct frost plan.

Step 4: Bring in the containers and the tender bulbs

Anything in a pot is far more exposed than the same plant in the ground, because the root ball is above ground and freezes from all sides.

  • Move containers against the house wall, under an overhang, or into a garage or shed for the night.
  • Group them together — clustered pots insulate each other.
  • Wrap the pot, not just the plant, with burlap or an old blanket; the roots are the vulnerable part.
  • Lift tender bulbs and tubers — dahlias, gladioli, cannas — after the foliage blackens, not before. They need the frost to signal dormancy.

Step 5: Disconnect the hoses

The single most expensive first-frost mistake is a hose left connected.

A hose full of water freezes, expands, and splits — and because it is attached to the tap, the ice has nowhere to expand except into the faucet body and the pipe behind it. A split hose bib can leak inside the wall for weeks before it is noticed, and that repair costs far more than everything else on this list combined.

Disconnect every hose, drain it, coil it, and store it indoors or in a shed. Then close the valve that feeds the outdoor faucet if the house has one, and open the faucet itself to drain what is left.

Step 6: Cap and cover the outdoor faucets

Once the hose is off, the faucet is still exposed.

  • Fit an insulated faucet cover over each hose bib — a cheap foam dome that costs very little and prevents most freeze damage.
  • If there is an interior shut-off valve, close it, then open the outdoor faucet to let the remaining water drain and leave it open for the winter.
  • Never leave a hose bib dripping as freeze protection — that works for a slow drip in a brief cold snap, not for a hard freeze, and it wastes water while giving false confidence.

Step 7: Drain the irrigation system

Sprinkler and drip systems are the other freeze-prone plumbing on the property.

  • Drain what can be drained by opening the low point and the zone valves.
  • Blow the lines out with compressed air if the system requires it — this is one job worth paying for once a year if you do not own a large enough compressor, because a split line under a driveway or patio is expensive to find and fix.
  • Remove and store the backflow preventer if it is the removable type, and insulate the housing if it is not.

Step 8: Check the pipes that freeze first

The first frost is a good reminder for the house, not just the garden, because the same night that kills the basil also finds every uninsulated pipe.

The pipes that fail first are almost always in the same three places: against an exterior wall, in a crawl space or unheated basement, and in a garage or porch ceiling. Insulate those runs, seal the gaps where cold air reaches them, and on the night of the frost, leave the cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls open so warm air reaches the pipes.

If the forecast is a hard freeze rather than a light one, the full sequence — including what to do about a pipe that has already frozen — is in preventing frozen pipes.

Step 9: Test the heating before you need it

The first cold night is when the heating system reveals whether it works. Better to find out the day before.

  • Run the heating for twenty minutes and check that warm air reaches every room.
  • Replace the filter if it has been in since spring.
  • Check the pilot or ignition on a gas appliance, and the condensate drain on a high-efficiency furnace.
  • Test the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, since the heating season is when both matter most.

The wider version of this work — air sealing, insulation, roof and attic — is in winterizing a home, and the time to start it is before this checklist, not after.

Step 10: The ten-minute house pass

Once the garden is handled, walk the outside of the house once:

  • Storm windows and screens: swap or close them.
  • Door sweeps and thresholds: check the exterior doors for daylight underneath.
  • Outdoor furniture and cushions: bring in or cover.
  • Grills and propane: cylinders stay outside, in a sheltered spot — never brought indoors, even for one cold night.
  • Ladders, hoses, and garden tools: into the shed, so they are not frozen to the ground in the morning.

After the Frost: What to Do and What Not To

Do not prune frost damage immediately. The blackened foliage looks terrible and it is still protecting the tissue below it. Wait until spring growth shows you where the live wood is, then cut back to that point.

Do not walk on frozen grass. Grass blades are brittle at frost, and foot traffic breaks the crowns. It is the cheapest lawn damage to avoid and the most common.

Do not dig or till frozen ground. Wait for it to thaw; working it wet or frozen destroys structure that takes a season to rebuild.

Do harvest the root crops that survived. Parsnips, carrots, and leeks are sweeter after frost, and they can stay in the ground under mulch for weeks.

Do plant garlic and spring bulbs. The first frost is the traditional signal for both, and they need the cold to root.

Do leave seed heads and stems on perennials through the winter — they feed birds and shelter beneficial insects, and they are cut back in spring, not autumn. The spring-side version of this list is in getting the garden ready for spring.

What NOT to Do

Do not cover plants with plastic that touches the leaves. It concentrates cold at the contact points and does more damage than no cover.

Do not water at night in the hope of preventing frost. Wet foliage increases damage; wet soil helps, but it should be watered earlier in the day.

Do not leave the hose connected “just for one more night”. That is how hose bibs split.

Do not fertilise to “strengthen” plants before frost. Nitrogen pushes soft new growth, which is the most frost-sensitive tissue a plant can produce.

Do not assume a covered plant is safe below 28°F. A single sheet adds a few degrees, not ten. In a hard freeze, harvest rather than protect.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what temperature should I cover my plants?

Cover when the forecast low is 36°F or below with clear skies and light wind, because ground-level temperatures will be lower than the forecast air temperature. For tender plants, cover at any forecast below 40°F.

What is the difference between a frost and a freeze?

A frost is ice forming on surfaces when they drop to 32°F, and it can happen while the official air temperature is a few degrees above freezing. A freeze is the air temperature itself dropping below 32°F, and a hard freeze — 24°F or lower for several hours — is what damages plumbing and kills most annuals.

Will a sheet actually protect plants?

Yes, for a light frost. A fabric cover traps heat rising from the soil and adds roughly 2 to 4°F at the plant surface, which is often the difference. It will not save tender plants through a hard freeze, and it must be put on before sunset to trap the day's heat.

Should I pick green tomatoes before the frost?

Yes. They ripen indoors on a tray at room temperature, and a single hard frost ruins them on the vine. Pick them with the stem on if you can, and sort out any with damaged skin.

Can I leave root vegetables in the ground?

Usually yes. Carrots, parsnips, leeks, and Brussels sprouts tolerate hard frost, and several improve in flavour. Mulch heavily over them so the ground stays diggable, and harvest through the winter.

Why did my hose bib burst when I only left the hose on?

Because the water had nowhere to expand. A disconnected hose lets the faucet drain; a connected one traps water in the faucet body, and the ice splits the casting or the pipe behind the wall. The damage is often invisible until the first thaw.

Do I need to do all of this for one cold night?

Do the first four steps — water, harvest, cover, and disconnect the hose — for any frost advisory. The rest of the list can wait for the first hard freeze, with one exception: the heating check, which is worth doing before the first cold night regardless.
