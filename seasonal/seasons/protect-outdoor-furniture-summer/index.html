How to Protect Outdoor Furniture in Summer

Seasonal & Holiday Seasons Published October 16, 2026 9 min read

Most outdoor furniture doesn’t fail because of sun or rain individually. It fails because of the combination: UV breaks down the surface, then moisture gets into the damage and expands, freezes, or grows mold. A finish that stays intact keeps water out, which is why protection is mostly about maintaining a barrier.

Material determines everything. The advice that saves teak will ruin metal, and the advice that saves metal does nothing for resin.

Step 1: Identify what your furniture is actually made of

Before any product decision, confirm the material. Marketing names obscure this.

MaterialTelltale signsMain enemy
Teak / hardwoodVisible grain, heavy, warm to touchUV greying, moisture cycling
Metal (aluminum, steel, iron)Cool to touch, uniform surfaceOxidation and rust, especially at welds and joints
Resin / polypropyleneLight, uniform matte finishUV embrittlement and fading
Wicker / rattan (natural)Woven, irregular textureMoisture → rot and mold
Wicker (synthetic)Woven, uniform, slightly plastickyUV fading, cracked weave
Fabric cushions—UV fade, mildew in the foam core

The most important distinction: natural wicker and synthetic wicker need completely different care and are frequently confused. If it’s natural, moisture is a serious threat. If it’s synthetic, the frame underneath matters more.

Step 2: Clean first — protection over dirt won’t hold

  1. Wash the frames with mild dish soap and water. Use a soft brush for textured surfaces.
  2. Rinse thoroughly. Soap residue attracts dirt and interferes with any finish you apply.
  3. Clean from the top down, letting dirt run off rather than re-soiling cleaned areas.
  4. Deal with mold on cushions before anything else — a UV spray over mold just seals it in. Most cushion covers unzip; wash per the tag with a mild detergent and dry fully in the sun.
  5. Let everything dry completely before applying any protectant or covering. Sealing moisture in is worse than leaving the surface bare.

Step 3: Treat each material on its own terms

Teak and hardwood

  1. Decide whether you want the grey. Weathered teak grey is a stable, protective surface layer — it does no harm, and many people prefer it. Teak’s natural oils make it genuinely weather-resistant without treatment.
  2. If you want to keep the golden color, clean with a teak cleaner (oxalic acid based), then apply teak oil or tung oil once or twice a season.
  3. Do not varnish or lacquer outdoor hardwood. Film finishes crack under expansion and contraction, and then trap water against the wood in the cracks. That’s worse than untreated wood.
  4. Sand lightly before oiling if the grain has raised. It also opens the pores to accept oil.

Metal frames

  1. Wax the frame with automotive paste wax once at the start of the season. This is the single highest-return maintenance step for metal furniture and takes about 20 minutes.
  2. Treat rust spots immediately with fine steel wool or a sanding block, then touch up with matching paint. How to Remove Rust Stains covers the chemistry — the same principle applies here.
  3. Inspect welds and joints, especially where paint is thinnest. That’s where rust always starts.
  4. For aluminum, don’t panic about rust — aluminum oxidizes, but the oxide layer is protective and stops further corrosion. White powdery film is different from steel rust.
  5. Remove salt spray or pool chlorine residue by rinsing monthly if the furniture sits near either.

Resin and polypropylene

  1. Wash with mild soap. Never use abrasives — they permanently dull the surface.
  2. Apply a UV protectant spray made for plastics. Resin fails through UV embrittlement, not water.
  3. Avoid bleach on colored resin — it fades pigment as well as mold.

Natural wicker and rattan

  1. Keep it under cover. This is the whole strategy; natural wicker will not survive a full season exposed.
  2. Treat mildew with diluted vinegar, not bleach, then dry thoroughly in sun.
  3. Rinse and dry after rain rather than letting it sit wet.
  4. Don’t apply oil to natural wicker — it softens the weave and attracts dust.

Synthetic wicker

  1. Wash with soap and water, rinse.
  2. Apply UV protectant. The weave cracks and fades under sun before anything else fails.
  3. Check the frame underneath — on cheap synthetic wicker, the aluminum frame is the weak point.

Step 4: Cushions are the short-lived part — treat them that way

Cushions typically outlive their usefulness long before the frames fail, and the failure is usually the foam core.

  1. Store cushions out of rain whenever possible. Even “weather-resistant” fabric is waterproofed on the cover, not the foam inside.
  2. Ensure water can drain. Water pooling in a cushion seat is what grows mildew in the foam, and the foam won’t dry before the next rain.
  3. Use a deck box with ventilation, not a sealed one. Sealed boxes trap humidity and grow mildew regardless of rain.
  4. Spray fabric with UV protectant each season. Fading is UV, not rain.
  5. Buy cushions with removable, washable covers when replacing. Non-removable covers means a stained cushion is a permanent cushion.
  6. Never use a plastic tarp over cushions — it traps condensation against the fabric.

Step 5: Cover correctly, or don’t cover at all

Bad covering causes more damage than no covering.

  • Use breathable covers only. Plastic sheeting and non-vented tarps trap moisture and grow mold and mildew. This is the most common covering mistake and it directly damages the furniture.
  • Cover when the furniture is completely dry. Covering damp furniture guarantees mold.
  • Secure covers so they can’t flap. A flapping cover acts like sandpaper on finishes and paint.
  • Slope covers so water runs off. A cover that pools water is a bathtub sitting on your furniture.
  • Don’t cover during long dry, sunny spells if you can avoid it. Ventilation and drying do more good than the cover.
  • If you cover for winter, elevate the furniture slightly so air moves underneath. A tarp directly on the ground traps ground moisture.

Step 6: Fix the placement causes of damage

Location damages furniture faster than weather alone.

  1. Move furniture out of the harshest sun if you can. A shaded spot can double the life of resin and cushion fabric.
  2. Get it off wet ground. Standing water at the base is where rot and rust begin on legs.
  3. Keep it away from sprinklers. Constant spray accelerates everything — rust, mold, and UV damage all at once.
  4. Watch the trees. Sap, bird droppings, and falling fruit all damage finishes; siting matters.
  5. Add a shade structure if the space allows. This is the most effective single protection for both furniture and people.

Step 7: At the end of summer, do the winterization pass properly

The transition from summer to winter is when most furniture dies — not from summer.

  1. Clean everything thoroughly before storage. Dirt holds moisture against the surface for months.
  2. Dry completely. Two full dry days in sun.
  3. Treat wood with oil and metal with wax before storing.
  4. Store cushions indoors in a dry, ventilated space. This is the single most important step; cushions left out over winter are usually ruined.
  5. Stack or store furniture so air can circulate — never wrap in plastic.
  6. For metal frames, add silica gel packets to the storage area if it’s damp.
  7. Elevate off concrete floors, which wick moisture year-round.

Why This Works

Outdoor furniture degradation is driven by three mechanisms, and each material is vulnerable to a different one. UV radiation breaks chemical bonds in polymers and pigments — that’s why resin goes brittle and cushions fade, and it’s the reason sunscreen products help those materials specifically. Water drives hydrolysis, freeze-thaw cycling, and biological growth — that’s what destroys natural wicker and untreated hardwood. Oxidation requires water and oxygen at a metal surface — that’s rust.

Coating strategies work by interrupting the specific mechanism, which is why one product can’t do it all. An oil finish on teak replaces the natural oils that UV and rain leach out, restoring the water-shedding surface. Wax on metal creates a physical barrier between the steel and both water and oxygen. A UV spray on plastic absorbs the radiation before it reaches the polymer chains.

Film finishes like varnish fail on outdoor wood because wood moves. Wood expands and contracts with humidity far more than any coating can accommodate, so the film cracks. Once cracked, it traps water against the surface and holds it there — worse than the untreated case, where water at least evaporates freely.

Breathable covers versus plastic follows the same logic. The point of a cover is to stop direct rain while allowing water vapor to escape. Plastic stops rain and traps vapor, and the result is a warm, dark, humid microclimate — ideal conditions for mold and mildew, which then damage the furniture in a way rain alone wouldn’t.

Foam cushion cores can’t be waterproofed effectively because water entering through seams and zippers has nowhere to go. A wet foam core takes days to dry in still air and grows mildew within two. That’s why the recommendation is always to keep cushions out of rain rather than to buy “waterproof” ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I cover outdoor furniture every night?

No. Covering nightly traps moisture and is more work than it's worth for most furniture. Cover for extended rain periods and for winter storage, and keep cushions inside when not in use.

Is it better to let teak weather or oil it?

Both are fine. Weathered teak develops a stable grey surface layer that protects the wood. Oiling preserves the golden color but requires reapplication once or twice a season. Varnishing is the one option to avoid.

How do I stop metal patio furniture from rusting?

Wax the frame with automotive paste wax at the start of each season, and touch up any rust spot immediately by sanding to bare metal and repainting. Keep it off wet ground and away from sprinklers.

Why does my resin furniture turn brittle and crack?

UV embrittlement. The polymer chains break down under ultraviolet light, which is why resin fails in sun rather than rain. A UV protectant spray applied each season substantially extends the life.

Can outdoor cushions be left out in the rain?

Occasionally, yes. Routinely, no. The waterproofing is on the cover fabric, not the foam core, and a wet core mildews within days and doesn't dry in still air.

What's the best way to store patio cushions over winter?

Wash them, dry them completely in the sun, and store indoors in a dry, ventilated space. Never store them damp, and never seal them in plastic.

Why is there mold on my furniture if I cover it?

The cover is trapping moisture rather than keeping it out. Use breathable covers, only cover fully dry furniture, and ensure air can circulate underneath.

How often should I clean outdoor furniture?

Wash monthly during the season, and do a full clean plus treatment at the start and end of the season. Rinse more often if the furniture is near a pool, the sea, or sprinklers.
