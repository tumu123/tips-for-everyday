Holiday Safety Checks: Lights, Trees, Candles, Heaters

Seasonal & Holiday Holidays Published October 19, 2026 10 min read

December is the only month when a typical living room contains four independent ignition sources at once: a string of lights with a hundred connections, a tree that dries out a little more every day, open flame, and a heater running because the room is cold.

Each one is manageable on its own. The reason holiday fires happen is that they are combined in a small space, for weeks, while everyone is distracted.

The good news is that the failure modes are well documented and the fixes are cheap. None of them require giving up the tree, the candles, or the lights.

Step 1: Know what actually burns in a holiday fire

A fresh-cut tree is difficult to ignite — the sap is still water-rich. A tree that has been indoors for three weeks with a dry stand is a different material entirely, and the difference is measurable: needles that shatter when bent, a trunk that has stopped absorbing water, and branches that feel light.

Once a dry tree ignites, the fire spreads through the needles in seconds and the room reaches flashover — everything combustible igniting at once — in under a minute. That is why the tree fire videos from fire departments look unreal, and why the entire safety strategy is about keeping the tree from drying out and keeping ignition sources away from it.

Step 2: Choose and site the tree with the fire in mind

  • Buy a tree that is still fresh. Run a branch through your hand: needles should bend, not shed. Tap the trunk on the ground — a shower of needles means it has been cut for weeks.
  • Cut 1 to 2 inches off the trunk before putting it in the stand. A sealed trunk cannot absorb water, and this is the single most common reason a tree dries out early.
  • Keep the stand full. A cut tree can take up to a gallon of water a day in the first week. Check it morning and night for the first three days.
  • Site it at least 3 feet from any heat source — fireplace, radiator, space heater, heating vent, and television. Heat dries the needles and creates the ignition path.
  • Do not block an exit. A tree between the sofa and the only door is a problem in a fire and a problem every day.
  • Anchor it. A tree that falls over is a broken ornament problem and, if it lands on a heater, a fire problem. A fishing line from the trunk to a wall hook is enough.

Step 3: Test the tree weekly, and remove it when it fails

The test is simple and takes five seconds: bend a needle. If it bends, the tree is fine. If it snaps or crumbles, the tree is dry and should come out of the house.

Additional signals:

  • The water level stops dropping even though the stand is full.
  • Branches feel noticeably lighter.
  • Needles fall when you brush past.
  • The tree has been indoors more than four weeks.

A dry tree is not a decoration to keep until New Year. Most municipal collection programs start the first week of January for exactly this reason.

Step 4: Use the right lights, and count the strands

LEDs run cool enough to touch and use a fraction of the power of incandescent. They are the safer choice, and for a tree indoors they are the correct one.

For any string, regardless of type:

  • Look for a UL or ETL listing mark on the plug or the tag. An unlisted string from a discount site is the single highest-risk item in holiday decorating.
  • Follow the manufacturer’s end-to-end limit. For incandescent strings that is often three strands; exceeding it overheats the wire in the first cord. LED strings usually allow far more, but the number is printed on the tag — read it rather than guessing.
  • Inspect every string before it goes up. Look for cracked sockets, frayed insulation, and missing bulbs. A missing bulb in a series string breaks the circuit; a damaged socket is a shock and fire risk.
  • Never use indoor-rated lights outdoors. Outdoor strings are rated for wet locations and cold; indoor ones are not, and the failure is not immediate, which makes it worse.

Step 5: Extension cords are where most holiday electrical fires start

The lights are usually fine. The cord feeding them is often not.

  • Match the gauge to the load. 16 AWG for light loads under 25 feet, 14 AWG for a 15-amp circuit run, 12 AWG for anything heavier. A thin cord on a long run heats up.
  • Never daisy-chain two extension cords into one another. Every connection is resistance, and resistance is heat.
  • Never run a cord under a rug, through a doorway, or pinched in a window. All three hide damage and trap heat.
  • Outdoors: use a GFCI-protected outlet, and keep every connection off the ground — a cord end sitting in snow or a puddle is a shock waiting to happen.
  • Unplug by the plug, never by the cord, and unplug the tree before bed and before leaving the house.

Step 6: Treat candles as an open flame, because they are

Candles cause a large share of December decoration fires, and almost all of them happen when someone leaves the room.

  • 12 inches of clear space in every direction, and nothing above the flame. Curtains, paper, and evergreen garlands are the classic combination.
  • A non-tipping holder on a stable, non-flammable surface, and a glass hurricane if there are children or pets.
  • Never leave a room with a candle burning, and never sleep with one lit. This is the entire rule; everything else is secondary.
  • Keep matches and lighters out of reach, and place candles above the height a child can reach or a tail can sweep.
  • Extinguish with a snuffer, not by blowing, which sends sparks and wax.
  • Battery candles exist and are indistinguishable at a distance. In a house with young children, they are the right answer.

Step 7: Space heaters need three feet and a wall outlet

A space heater is the appliance most likely to be involved in a fatal home fire, and the mechanism is almost always clearance or a cord.

  • 3 feet of clear space on all sides, and nothing draped over it. A towel or a blanket within a foot is the classic ignition.
  • Plug directly into a wall outlet. Never an extension cord, never a power strip. A 1,500-watt heater draws 12.5 amps, which is most of a 15-amp circuit.
  • One heater per circuit. Two heaters on the same circuit, or a heater sharing with a microwave, trips breakers at best.
  • Look for a tip-over switch and an overheat shutoff, and buy a unit with a thermostat rather than a simple on-off.
  • Never use a fuel-burning heater indoors without ventilation and a carbon monoxide alarm. Kerosene and propane units produce CO, and the symptoms — headache, nausea, drowsiness — are easy to mistake for a cold.

Step 8: Check the alarms, and check them again

Holiday season is when alarms get tested least and fires happen most.

  • Test every smoke alarm with the test button, and replace the batteries if they are more than a year old.
  • Confirm there is an alarm on every level and inside or outside every sleeping area.
  • Add a CO alarm if you have a fireplace, a gas appliance, an attached garage, or use any fuel heater.
  • Never disconnect an alarm because cooking sets it off. Move it, or use a photoelectric type near the kitchen.
  • Check the fire extinguisher pressure gauge and its location. It should be in the kitchen, not in the basement behind the holiday boxes.

Step 9: Check the loads you have added to the panel

December adds lighting, heating, and cooking loads at the same time, and older homes were not wired for it.

  • Know which breaker controls the room you are decorating, and confirm it is not already loaded with a heater and a kitchen appliance.
  • Feel the cover plates on outlets running a heater or a large light display. Warm is normal; hot is a problem.
  • Never replace a breaker with a larger one to stop tripping. The breaker is protecting the wire, and a bigger breaker means a hotter wire in the wall.
  • If a breaker trips repeatedly, unplug things and move the load rather than resetting it again.

Step 10: Have a plan for the two minutes that matter

If a fire starts, the decisions are made in under two minutes:

  • Get everyone out first, then call. Do not stop for extinguishers, pets, or belongings.
  • Close doors behind you as you leave. A closed door slows a fire more than most people believe.
  • If the tree is burning, do not fight it. A dry tree fire grows faster than an extinguisher can handle; leave.
  • Agree on a meeting place outside, and practice it once — the same walk-through you would do for a fire drill.
  • Keep the tree between the room and the exit clear of clutter, so the path is usable in the dark.

Why This Works

The four hazards are addressed at their specific failure points rather than with general caution. Lights fail at the connection and the cord, so the rules are about listing marks and gauge. Trees fail when dry, so the rules are about water and the needle test. Candles fail when unattended, so the rule is absence. Heaters fail on clearance and current, so the rules are distance and a wall outlet.

Together they keep the ignition sources and the fuel separated, which is the only strategy that scales to a room full of both.

Frequently Asked Questions

How dangerous is a dry Christmas tree?

Very. A tree that has stopped absorbing water can ignite from a small flame and reach flashover in under a minute, because the needles are thin, dry, and full of resin. The needle-bend test tells you the condition: bending is fine, snapping means remove it.

Are LED Christmas lights safer than incandescent?

Yes. LEDs generate very little heat at the bulb, use roughly a tenth of the power, and put far less load on the cord and the circuit. They are the right choice for an indoor tree, especially a real one.

How many strands of Christmas lights can you connect together?

Follow the number printed on the tag — commonly three for incandescent strings and considerably more for LEDs. The limit exists because the wire in the first cord carries the current for all the strands after it, and exceeding it overheats that wire.

How far should a Christmas tree be from a heater or fireplace?

At least 3 feet from any heat source, including radiators, space heaters, heating vents, and televisions. Heat dries the needles and creates the ignition path. Also keep the tree out of the path to an exit.

Is it safe to leave Christmas lights on overnight?

No. Unplug the tree and any decorative lighting before bed and before leaving the house. Timers help, but a timer that turns lights on at 6 a.m. is not a substitute for unplugging them at night.

How much space does a space heater need?

3 feet of clear space on all sides, plugged directly into a wall outlet, with nothing draped over it. One heater per circuit. Look for a tip-over switch and an overheat shutoff, and never use an extension cord with a 1,500-watt unit.

Do I need a carbon monoxide alarm for holiday decorations?

You need one if the house has a fireplace, any gas appliance, an attached garage, or uses a fuel-burning heater. CO is colorless and its early symptoms — headache, nausea, fatigue — are easy to dismiss. A battery-powered alarm on each level is inexpensive.

What should I do if a Christmas tree catches fire?

Leave immediately and call from outside. A dry tree fire grows faster than an extinguisher can control, and the smoke is dense and toxic. Close doors behind you, get everyone to the agreed meeting place, and do not go back for anything.
