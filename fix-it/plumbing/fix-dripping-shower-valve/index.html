How to Fix a Shower That Drips After You Turn It Off

Fix It Plumbing Published October 17, 2026 7 min read

If your shower drips after you shut it off, replacing the shower head will not fix it — and that is the single most common wasted purchase in this situation.

The head is downstream of the valve. Water leaves the shower head only if something upstream lets it through. When the drip continues after the handle is fully closed, the shut-off is happening at the valve, not at the head.

There are three valve designs in common use, and they fail differently:

Valve typeHow you can usually tellWhat wears out
Compression (two-handle)Two separate hot and cold handles; you turn several full rotationsRubber washer on the stem, or the seat it presses into
Ball (single-handle)One handle that rotates; the handle feels like it moves over a ballInternal springs and rubber seats behind the ball
Ceramic disc or cartridge (single-handle)One lever that lifts and swings; short travelThe cartridge — seals, ports, or a cracked disc

The good news is that all three are repairable at the valve, and none of them require opening the wall.

Step 0: Confirm the Drip Is Really the Valve

Before you take anything apart, spend two minutes ruling out the two innocent causes.

Cause A: the head is still draining. A shower head holds water after shut-off, and it can dribble for a minute or two as that water drains out. Watch the drip for five minutes. If it stops on its own, there is no problem.

Cause B: it is a tub spout diverter. If you have a combined tub and shower, pull the diverter up and down a few times. A diverter that does not fully seat sends water to the shower head continuously while the tub spout runs. That is a diverter issue, not a valve issue.

If the drip is steady and continues past a few minutes, it is the valve. Note the drip rate — a few drips per minute points to a worn seal, while a steady thin stream usually means a cracked cartridge or a damaged seat.

Step 1: Shut Off the Water and Verify

Close the shower’s own stops if the valve has integral stops — many single-handle valves have screw slots on either side of the valve body that turn a quarter turn with a flathead screwdriver.

If there are no local stops, close the main supply or the fixture branch. Open the shower after closing to confirm the water actually stopped, and leave it open to relieve pressure.

Do not skip the verification. A valve that still has pressure behind it will spray when you pull the cartridge, and it will spray in the direction of your face.

Step 2: Remove the Handle and Document Everything

Remove the handle screw — often hidden under a small cap that pries off with a flathead, sometimes under the index button on a lever handle.

Pull the handle straight off. If it is stuck, do not lever against the wall; use a handle puller or apply penetrating oil and wait.

Photograph everything before removing parts. Screws, plate orientation, and the order of washers behind the handle all matter on reassembly. This is the step people skip and then pay for.

Remove the escutcheon (the trim plate). On some valves the plate is also the retainer that holds the cartridge in.

Step 3: Identify What You Are Looking At

Now you can see the actual mechanism.

Two stems with hex bases → compression valve. The repair is a new rubber washer on the end of the stem, and possibly a new seat if the old one is scored.

A single brass or plastic ball with slots → ball valve. The ball lifts out; the springs and rubber seats sit behind it.

A cylindrical cartridge with a stem → cartridge or ceramic disc valve. There is usually a retaining clip or a pair of screws holding it in.

Look closely at the cartridge before pulling it. Note its orientation — many are keyed and only go in one way, but noting the current position costs nothing and saves a re-do.

Step 4: Pull the Cartridge or Stem

Remove the retaining clip with needle-nose pliers. Do not lose it — it is small and it is not always included with a new cartridge.

If the cartridge does not come out by hand, use the puller that ships with most replacement cartridges. The puller threads into the cartridge stem and gives you something to pull straight against.

Pull straight out, in line with the valve body. Angled force is how cartridges break off inside the valve, and removing a broken cartridge is a genuinely unpleasant job that can require a specialty extractor.

If the cartridge is stuck hard, penetrating oil and patience beat force. Give it time.

Step 5: Inspect the Valve Body Before Installing Anything

This step is where most cartridge replacements fail.

Look inside the valve body with a flashlight. You are checking for:

  • Mineral scale on the internal seats and walls
  • Scoring or pitting where the old seals seated
  • Debris from a failing cartridge or from pipe work upstream

Clean scale with vinegar on a brush — do not use anything abrasive on the sealing surfaces. If you can feel a groove with a fingernail, a new cartridge will seal imperfectly on that surface no matter how new it is.

If the seats are damaged, the cartridge alone will not fix the drip. You need a seat dressing tool (for compression valves) or a new valve body, which means opening the wall. Check this before you assume a cartridge solved it.

Step 6: Match the Replacement Correctly

Take the old cartridge with you to the supplier, or photograph it with something for scale.

Cartridges that look similar are not interchangeable. The differences that matter are the manufacturer, the series, the length, and the port pattern. A cartridge from the same brand but a different series will fit in the opening and still leak.

If the valve has no visible brand, look at the escutcheon — the brand is usually stamped on the back of the trim plate even when the valve body is unmarked.

Step 7: Lubricate and Install

Coat the new cartridge’s rubber seals with silicone plumber’s grease. This is not optional — a dry seal will drag and tear as you push it in, and a torn seal drips.

Use silicone grease, not petroleum jelly. Petroleum products swell and degrade the rubber compounds used in these seals.

Push the cartridge in straight, in the same orientation as the old one, until it seats fully. Reinstall the retaining clip. Confirm the clip is actually seated — a partially seated clip lets the cartridge walk forward under pressure and that alone causes a drip.

Step 8: Reassemble and Test in Stages

Reinstall the trim plate and handle. Do not fully tighten the handle screw until you have tested.

Turn the water back on with the shower valve in the off position, and listen. Then open it and check for normal flow, then close it again and watch for two minutes.

Test with the handle off first. If water comes out with the valve closed, stop and recheck the cartridge seating before you put everything back together.

Check that hot and cold are not crossed, then tighten the handle screw and fit the cap.

Step 9: If It Still Drips, Check the Pressure Balance Unit

On pressure-balancing and thermostatic valves there is a second serviceable element behind or beside the main cartridge. These units compensate for pressure changes elsewhere in the house, and when they fail the symptom is often a drip plus poor temperature stability.

If you replaced the cartridge correctly and the drip persists, this is the next thing to look at — and your valve’s brand and model determines whether it is a homeowner-serviceable part.

Why This Works

A dripping shower is a sealing problem, and all three valve types seal the same way: a rubber or ceramic element presses against a machined surface to block the flow path. Wear, scale, and age attack exactly that interface. Everything else in the valve — the handle, the trim, the body — is structure around it.

That is why the repair is so consistent across designs: replace the sealing element, prepare the surface it seals against, and lubricate so it seats without tearing. The three valve types differ only in what the sealing element looks like and how it is retained.

It also explains the two failure modes people hit. Replacing the cartridge without cleaning the seat leaves the new seal pressing against scale, so it drips again. Installing without grease tears the seal on the way in, so it drips immediately. Both failures look identical from the outside, which is why they are so often blamed on a defective part.

What NOT to Do

Do not replace the shower head first. It is downstream of the problem. This is the most common wasted purchase for this symptom.

Do not overtighten the handle to stop a drip. If the valve needs force to stop dripping, the internal seal is already gone. Extra force damages the cartridge further and can crack the valve body.

Do not use petroleum jelly on the seals. It degrades the rubber compounds used in cartridges and O-rings.

Do not force a stuck cartridge. Angled force breaks it off in the body. Penetrating oil and a proper puller exist for exactly this.

Do not skip the seat inspection. A new cartridge against a scored seat is a drip you will redo in a month.

Do not leave the retaining clip partly seated. It looks installed and is not. Check it by feel, not by eye.

Do not work with pressure on the line. Verify the water is off by opening the valve before disassembly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my shower drip only sometimes?

Temperature-dependent expansion is common: the seal leaks more when the valve is cold, then swells slightly as warm water runs through and temporarily seals. It is the same worn seal, just more obvious at some temperatures.

How long does a shower cartridge last?

Typically ten to fifteen years in average water, considerably less with hard water or high sediment. If your cartridge is younger than five years and already failing, look for a pressure or water quality problem rather than blaming the part.

Is a dripping shower worth fixing, or can I ignore it?

A drip of one per second wastes roughly 2,500 gallons a year. At typical combined water and sewer rates that is real money, and if the drip is hot water you are paying to heat it as well.

Can I fix it without turning off the main water supply?

Yes if your valve has integral stops — the screw slots flanking the valve body. Turn them a quarter turn with a flathead screwdriver. If it does not have them, you need the branch or main shut-off.

The handle turns but the drip does not change. What does that mean?

Usually that the cartridge's internal port is no longer being blocked, which points to a cracked cartridge body or a failed internal seal rather than simple wear. Replace the cartridge; if that does not fix it, inspect the seats.

Do I need to drain the water heater to change a shower cartridge?

No. Closing the supply to the fixture is enough. You are working on the branch, not the whole system.

Can I replace just the rubber seals instead of the whole cartridge?

On compression valves, yes — that is the normal repair. On cartridge valves the seals are integrated, and replacing the whole cartridge is the standard and more reliable repair.

Why did the drip come back a few weeks after I replaced the cartridge?

Three likely causes: the seats were scored and not dressed, the new cartridge was not the exact match, or the retaining clip was not fully seated and let the cartridge shift.

Is this the same job as fixing a dripping kitchen faucet?

Mechanically very similar — the sealing principle is the same. The difference is access and part identification, and shower valves are more likely to have integral stops. If you have done one, the other is familiar.
