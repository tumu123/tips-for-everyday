How to Fix a Toilet That Rocks on the Floor

Fix It Plumbing Published October 17, 2026 6 min read
Advertisement

A toilet that rocks is not a cosmetic annoyance — it is a seal that is actively being destroyed.

The wax ring under a toilet is not a gasket; it is a deformable plug. It seals because installing the toilet compresses it into the gap between the bowl outlet and the closet flange. Once the toilet can move, that compression is no longer static: every rock flexes the wax, and repeated flexing opens a path. Water seeps out, and sewer gas comes up.

The three causes are different, and they need different fixes:

CauseHow to tellFix
Shims were never installed or have worked looseToilet moves as a rigid unit; no crack visibleShim
Floor is uneven or has settledToilet does not sit flat; a gap runs along one sideShim, possibly a repair flange
Closet flange is below floor level or brokenFlange sits low or is cracked; bolts will not holdFlange repair or spacer, then shim

Almost every case ends with shimming. The question is whether shimming alone is enough.

Step 0: Confirm What Is Actually Moving

Sit on the toilet and shift your weight. Then kneel and push on the bowl rim side to side.

Watch the base, not the bowl. If the whole toilet moves as one piece and the floor does not flex, the problem is the gap under the base. If the bowl moves but the tank moves differently, the tank bolts are loose — that is a separate, easier fix.

If you see water appearing at the base when you rock it, the seal is already compromised. Do not delay this repair.

One thing this is not: a toilet that runs continuously is a tank problem, not a base problem. If yours runs, see how to fix a running toilet instead — the two symptoms are unrelated and the repairs do not overlap.

Step 1: Shut Off and Drain

Close the angle stop behind the toilet. Flush to empty the tank, then hold the handle down to drain as much as possible. Sponge the remaining water out of the tank and the bowl.

Disconnect the supply line from the tank. Have a towel ready — there is always some water left in the line and the tank.

Step 2: Remove the Toilet (Yes, Really)

Take off the bolt caps at the base. These are often corroded; if they will not unscrew, cut or crack them off carefully with a putty knife and pliers.

Remove the nuts with a wrench. If the bolt spins with the nut, hold the bolt with pliers from above or cut the bolt.

Lift straight up. A toilet is heavy and awkward, and the water left in the trap will spill. Have a helper if you can, and lift with your legs. Set it down on old towels or cardboard.

Do not set it down on its side — water in the trap will run out onto whatever is underneath.

Step 3: Inspect the Flange and the Floor

This is the step that determines what you actually need.

Look at the closet flange:

  • Is it cracked? A cracked flange will not hold bolts and must be repaired or replaced.
  • Is it below the finished floor? If the flange sits lower than the flooring, the wax ring has to bridge a gap it was never designed to bridge. A flange spacer or a jumbo wax ring handles this.
  • Is it loose in the floor? The flange should be screwed down. Loose flange screws are often the real cause of a toilet that will not stay put.

Look at the floor itself:

  • Is it level across where the base sits?
  • Is there rot or soft wood around the opening? Soft flooring must be addressed first — shimming a toilet onto rotted subfloor fixes nothing.

Step 4: Clean Off the Old Wax

Scrape the old wax off both the flange and the bottom of the toilet outlet with a putty knife.

Get the old wax off the flange completely. New wax does not bond to old wax reliably, and lumps will prevent the toilet from seating flat.

If you are switching to a wax-free rubber seal, follow that product’s instructions — some seat directly on the flange, others need a specific surface.

Wipe the area clean. This is a good moment to check for old leaks and staining on the subfloor.

Step 5: Set the Toilet Dry and Find the Gap

Before applying any seal, set the toilet back onto the flange with no wax and no shims. Press it down and check with your level across the bowl rim, both front-to-back and side-to-side.

Note where the gaps are and roughly how big they are. Slide shims in at the low points until the toilet does not rock and the bowl is level.

Do this dry so you know exactly what you are dealing with. Once wax is involved you get one chance at a clean set.

Lift the toilet back off and leave the shims where they are, or note their positions.

Step 6: Install the Seal

Fit the new wax ring. If you have a flange that sits low, use a jumbo ring or a ring with an integrated horn — a standard ring will not bridge the gap.

Do not stack two standard wax rings to make up a gap. Stacked rings can shift against each other and fail. Use the correct single product for the height you need.

Press the ring onto the flange or onto the toilet outlet, depending on the product, and make sure it is centered.

Step 7: Set the Toilet — One Motion, Straight Down

Pick the toilet up and lower it straight over the bolts. Look down through the bowl opening to line it up if you can; otherwise line up the bolt holes by eye and lower carefully.

Press down firmly and evenly. Your weight compresses the wax into the gap. Do not twist or slide the toilet once it contacts the wax — twisting smears the seal.

Rock it slightly front-to-back as you press to seat it, then stop.

Step 8: Insert Shims and Tighten Gradually

Slide the shims in at the low points you identified. Tap them in until snug; do not drive them so hard that they lift the toilet.

Use plastic or composite shims, not wood. Wood shims compress, wick moisture, and eventually rot — which reintroduces exactly the movement you are fixing.

Fit the washers and nuts on the closet bolts and tighten gradually and alternately, a quarter turn at a time on each side.

Check with your level as you go. Alternate until the toilet is solid and the bowl is level.

Stop tightening when it stops rocking. Overtightening is how bowls crack, and a cracked bowl means a new toilet.

Step 9: Trim the Shims and Caulk

Trim the shims flush with the base using a hacksaw or oscillating tool. Leave them in place — they are structural now.

Apply a bead of silicone caulk around the base, leaving a gap at the back.

That gap is deliberate. If the seal ever fails again, you want the water to come out where you can see it rather than being trapped behind caulk and rotting the floor.

Step 10: Reconnect and Test

Reconnect the supply line and turn the angle stop back on. Let the tank fill and flush several times.

Check around the base with a dry tissue after each flush. Then push on the bowl — it should not move at all.

Wait a day and check again before you consider it done; a slow seep may not show immediately.

Why This Works

The wax ring seals by compression, not by adhesion. That single fact explains both the failure and the fix.

Movement is what kills it. Every time the toilet rocks, the wax gap opens on one side and closes on the other, and the wax — which is not elastic — does not return to its original shape. Over time it takes a permanent set with a channel through it. That is why a slowly-worsening rock often precedes a mysterious damp patch at the base by weeks or months.

Shimming works because it turns a dynamic load into a static one. The toilet stops moving, so the wax stays compressed, so the seal stays intact. The shims are not holding the toilet up — the floor is. They are filling the void so the floor can do that job evenly.

The gradual alternating tighten matters for the same reason: uneven force compresses the wax unevenly, leaving a thin side that will fail first.

What NOT to Do

Do not caulk around a rocking toilet and call it fixed. Caulk hides the movement and the leak, and the leak continues into the subfloor.

Do not use wood shims. They compress and rot, which is a slow return of the same problem.

Do not overtighten the closet bolts to stop the rock. That is how bowls crack. If it still rocks when the bolts are snug, you need more shim, not more torque.

Do not stack two wax rings to bridge a low flange. Use a jumbo ring or a flange spacer.

Do not seal the entire base with caulk. Leave the back open so a future leak is visible.

Do not shim over rotted subfloor. The movement will return, and so will the leak.

Do not twist the toilet once it touches the wax. Set straight down, or lift and re-set.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I fix a rocking toilet without removing it?

You can sometimes drive shims in and they will reduce the movement, but you cannot confirm the seal is intact, and you cannot clean the old wax or check the flange. If the toilet has been rocking long enough to notice, the seal is suspect — remove it and check.

How do I know if the wax ring is already leaking?

Water at the base after flushing, a persistent sewer smell in the bathroom, or staining on the ceiling below if the bathroom is upstairs. Any of these means replace the ring, not just shim.

Should I use a wax ring or a wax-free seal?

Wax-free rubber seals tolerate some movement better and are reusable if you mis-set the toilet. Wax is cheaper and more forgiving of an imperfect flange. If your flange is slightly low or uneven, wax-free is often the safer choice.

Why does my toilet still rock after shimming?

Usually the shims are not far enough in, or the flange itself is loose or below floor level. Check the flange screws and its height relative to the finished floor.

How tight should the closet bolts be?

Snug enough that the toilet does not move, and no tighter. Alternate sides and check with a level rather than tightening to a feel.

Is a rocking toilet an emergency?

Not in minutes, but it is not a deferred repair either. A failed wax ring leaks into the subfloor and the damage grows quietly. Fix it within days, not seasons.

Can an uneven tile floor cause this?

Yes — tile thickness varies, and a toilet set on tile often has a gap along one side. That is exactly what shims are for.

Should the toilet be level, or just stable?

Both. Level matters because the bowl is designed to hold a specific water level, and a tilted bowl changes where the water sits in the trap.
