How to Fix a Leaky Outdoor Faucet (and Keep It From Freezing)

Fix It Plumbing Published October 17, 2026 6 min read

An outdoor tap leaks for the same reason an indoor one does — a worn seal — but it freezes for a reason that has nothing to do with the drip. Fixing both means understanding they are separate problems.

Why outdoor taps freeze: water expands when it freezes. If water is trapped in a section of pipe between the valve and the outdoors, and that section freezes, the expansion has nowhere to go and splits the pipe or the tap body. The failure often shows up as a flood in spring, when the ice thaws and the crack opens — which is why a burst outdoor tap is frequently discovered months after the freeze.

Two designs behave very differently:

TypeHow to tellFreezing behaviour
Standard hose bibShort body; the valve seat is right at the outside wallWater sits in the body outdoors. Freezes easily
Frost-free (frost-proof) sillcockLong body, sloping down toward the inside; the handle is often at a slight angleThe valve seat sits inside the heated space, and the body drains out when closed

If you have a standard bib in a freezing climate, the winter routine below matters a great deal. If you have a frost-free one, the routine is much simpler — but only if the hose is removed.

Step 0: Identify the Leak

There are two places an outdoor tap leaks, and they are different repairs.

Leak from the spout, when closed → the stem washer or seat inside the valve. This is the classic drip.

Leak from the handle area, when running → the packing around the stem, not the washer.

Leak at the hose connection, only with a hose attached → the hose washer, not the tap at all. Replace the rubber washer in the hose coupling before you do anything else.

Knowing which one you have takes one observation and saves you rebuilding the wrong part.

Step 1: Shut Off the Supply

Find the shut-off valve for the outdoor tap, usually in the basement or a utility space, on the pipe feeding that wall.

Close it, then open the outdoor tap to confirm the water stopped and to relieve pressure.

If there is no indoor shut-off, you will need to shut off the main supply, or do the repair on a section you can isolate. This is worth knowing before the first hard freeze, not during it.

Step 2: Remove the Handle and Packing Nut

Remove the handle screw and pull the handle off.

Unscrew the packing nut — the hex nut the stem passes through. A deep socket usually works better than an open-ended wrench here.

If it is stuck, apply penetrating oil and wait. Outdoor fittings corrode, and forcing an old packing nut can crack the tap body.

Step 3: Remove the Stem and Inspect

Unscrew the stem by turning it in the same direction you turn the handle to open. It threads out.

Look at the end of the stem:

  • The rubber washer is held by a brass screw. If it is flattened, cracked, or hardened, replace it.
  • The seat inside the tap body — feel it with your finger or look with a flashlight. If it is scored or pitted, a new washer will seal imperfectly on it.

If the seat is damaged, use a seat dressing tool to resurface it, or fit a seat and washer combination. Skipping this is the most common reason an outdoor tap drips again shortly after repair.

Step 4: Replace the Washer and Packing

Fit a new washer of the correct size. Take the old one with you when buying — these come in several sizes and are not interchangeable.

Replace the stem packing at the same time if the tap leaked around the handle. Packing comes as string, washers, or O-rings depending on the design.

Do both while you are in there. The parts cost very little, and you do not want to take it apart twice.

Reassemble: stem in, packing nut snug (not overtight — it should not bind the handle), handle on.

Step 5: Check the Vacuum Breaker

Many outdoor taps have a vacuum breaker — a small cap or collar on the spout, often with a set screw, that prevents backflow into the house supply.

If it is cracked, missing, or stuck open, replace it. It is a cheap part and it is a backflow prevention device, not decoration.

If water sprays from it when you run the tap, that is a symptom it has failed.

Step 6: Winterise Correctly — Remove the Hose

This is the single most important step, and it is the one people forget.

Remove every hose before the first freeze. A hose left attached traps water in the tap body and prevents the frost-free design from draining. A frost-free sillcock with a hose on it can freeze and burst exactly like a standard one.

Drain the hose, coil it, and store it.

Step 7: Shut Off and Drain the Supply

For a standard hose bib:

Close the indoor shut-off valve. Open the outdoor tap fully and leave it open — this lets the trapped water drain out and gives expanding ice somewhere to go.

Leave it open for the winter. A closed tap on a live line is a tap that can freeze and burst.

For a frost-free sillcock:

Close the tap from inside after removing the hose. The body is designed to drain; you do not need to leave the outdoor handle open, though some people do.

Step 8: Fit an Insulated Cover

A foam or fabric tap cover goes over the outside of the tap for the winter.

Use a cover designed for this. It adds a meaningful amount of protection, particularly on a standard bib, and it costs very little.

Do not wrap the tap in tape or cloth and call it insulated — improvised coverings hold moisture against the metal, which makes corrosion worse and provides little real protection.

Step 9: Check Before Spring

When you turn the supply back on in spring, do it with the tap open and watch.

Open the indoor valve slowly and check for leaks at the tap, and check indoors along the pipe run.

If you find a leak inside the wall, turn the supply off immediately. That is a burst from winter freezing, and it is a bigger job than the drip you started with.

Why This Works

The leak and the freeze are governed by different mechanisms, which is why fixing one does not fix the other.

The drip is a sealing failure, identical in principle to any indoor tap: a rubber washer pressing against a machined seat, worn by cycles and by grit in the water. Replacing the washer restores the seal, provided the seat is still sound — which is why inspecting the seat is part of the job rather than an optional extra.

The freeze is a containment problem. Water occupies more volume as ice, and in a closed section of pipe that expansion has no escape. The fix is therefore not insulation alone but removing the water: shutting off the supply and leaving the tap open eliminates the trapped column, and a frost-free design moves the vulnerable part indoors so there is nothing outside to freeze.

That is also why leaving a hose attached defeats both. A hose holds water in the body and blocks the drain path, so a frost-free sillcock performs no better than a standard one. The hose, not the tap, is the variable that matters most in that case.

What NOT to Do

Do not leave hoses attached over winter. This is the most common cause of a burst outdoor tap, including on frost-free models.

Do not close the outdoor tap and leave the supply on if it is a standard bib in a freezing climate. Shut off indoors and leave the tap open.

Do not force a corroded packing nut. Use penetrating oil and patience; a cracked body means replacing the tap.

Do not replace only the washer if the seat is scored. It will drip again. Dress the seat.

Do not ignore a missing or cracked vacuum breaker. It is backflow protection.

Do not wrap the tap in tape or rags as insulation. Use a proper cover.

Do not turn the supply back on in spring without watching. A winter burst shows up exactly then.

Do not assume a frost-free tap cannot freeze. With a hose attached, it can.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I leave the outdoor tap open or closed in winter?

For a standard bib with the indoor supply shut off, leave it open so any trapped water can drain and expand harmlessly. For a frost-free sillcock, closing it after removing the hose is normally sufficient.

Can a frost-free tap still freeze?

Yes, if a hose is left attached or if it was installed without the correct downward slope. The slope is what lets it drain.

Why does my outdoor tap drip only when a hose is connected?

Usually the hose coupling's rubber washer, not the tap. Replace the washer in the hose end first.

How do I know if I have a frost-free sillcock?

The body is noticeably longer and slopes downward from outside to inside, and the valve seat sits well inside the wall rather than at the spout.

What temperature should I worry about?

Any sustained freeze. The combination that causes bursts is freezing temperature plus trapped water — both matter.

Is a burst outdoor tap an emergency?

Once you discover it, shut off the supply to it. Water entering a wall cavity does damage quickly.

Can I fix the drip in winter?

Yes, but shut off the indoor supply first and do not leave water in the line afterwards. Repairing and then winterising in the same visit is the sensible order.

How much does a tap cover actually help?

It provides real additional protection for a standard bib, particularly in a sheltered location. It is not a substitute for shutting off and draining.
