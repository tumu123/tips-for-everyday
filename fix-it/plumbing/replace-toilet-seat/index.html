How to Replace a Toilet Seat

Fix It Plumbing Published October 17, 2026 6 min read

Replacing a toilet seat is one of the simplest jobs in a house, and it has exactly two ways to go badly:

  1. The old bolts will not come off. They have been sitting in a damp environment for years and the nuts have corroded onto the threads.
  2. The new bolts get overtightened, which cracks the porcelain — the most expensive possible outcome of a fifteen-minute job.

What makes this job awkward: the nuts are underneath the bowl, mostly out of sight and hard to reach. You are working blind, with a wrench, in a confined space, on a material that breaks rather than bends.

The two things that matter most:

  1. Buy the right shape. Round and elongated bowls are not interchangeable, and guessing is the most common reason a new seat does not fit.
  2. Tighten less than you think. The seal does not come from force; it comes from the rubber expansion washer doing its job.

Step 0: Measure the Bowl Before Buying

Do this first — it determines everything else.

  1. Measure from the centre of the hinge bolt holes to the front edge of the bowl.
  2. Round bowls are roughly 16.5 inches (42 cm). Elongated bowls are roughly 18.5 inches (47 cm).
  3. Also measure the width at the widest point and the spacing between the bolt holes, since these vary slightly by manufacturer.
  4. Take a photo of the old seat’s packaging if you still have it, or of the bowl with the tape measure visible.
  5. Choose the shape to match the bowl, not the old seat. If the previous owner fitted the wrong shape, buying the same one repeats the problem.

A quick visual check: an elongated bowl is visibly oval; a round bowl is closer to circular. If you are between the two, measure — do not guess.

Step 1: Remove the Old Seat

  1. Lift the hinge covers at the back of the seat. Most seats have two small plastic caps that flip or pry open.
  2. Hold the bolt head from above with a screwdriver while you loosen the nut from below.
  3. If the nut will not turn, apply penetrating oil to the threads from underneath and let it sit — ten minutes is better than two.
  4. Try again, holding the bolt head firmly. If the whole bolt spins instead of the nut turning, you need to grip the head harder or, failing that, cut it.
  5. Wear eye protection for any cutting.

If the nut is completely seized:

  1. Cut through the bolt between the nut and the bowl using a hacksaw blade, or an oscillating tool in a tight space.
  2. Protect the porcelain — put a cloth between the blade and the bowl, and work slowly. Porcelain chips very easily.
  3. Cut the nut side first if access allows, since that is where the corrosion is.
  4. Do not lever against the bowl. Prying is how bowls crack.

Step 2: Clean the Area Thoroughly

This step is skipped almost always, and it is why new seats work loose.

  1. Remove all old debris — corrosion, old washers, and any broken plastic.
  2. Clean the bolt holes and the area around them with rubbing alcohol and a cloth.
  3. Wipe the rim where the seat will sit. Grime and limescale here prevent the new seat from seating flat.
  4. Check the porcelain around the holes for cracks. If there is damage, address it before fitting a new seat.

Step 3: Fit the New Bolts

  1. Insert the new bolts into the holes from above, with the rubber expansion washers fitted as the manufacturer specifies.
  2. The rubber washer goes into the hole and expands as the bolt is tightened, which is what holds the bolt in place and creates the seal.
  3. Push the bolts down so they sit square, not angled.
  4. Do not tighten yet — the seat has to go on first.
  5. If your new seat uses a different fastening system, follow its instructions; some use a top-mount expansion fitting instead of a through-bolt.

Step 4: Position the New Seat

  1. Place the seat on the bowl and align the hinge posts with the bolts.
  2. Check the alignment front-to-back. The seat should sit evenly, with the front edge following the bowl’s shape.
  3. Adjust using the hinge slots — most seats allow a little fore-and-aft movement.
  4. Step back and look at it from above before tightening, because adjustment is much easier now than later.
  5. Fit the hinge covers loosely for now so you can still reach the bolts.

Step 5: Tighten From Below — Gently

  1. Start the nuts by hand to avoid cross-threading.
  2. Tighten by hand until snug.
  3. Then use a wrench for about a quarter turn — no more.
  4. Hold the bolt head from above with a screwdriver so the bolt does not spin.
  5. Check the seat for movement. Tighten in small increments, a fraction of a turn at a time, and check after each.

This is the step where bowls crack. The rubber washer does the sealing and the gripping; the nut only needs to compress it. More force does not create a better seal — it creates a fracture.

Step 6: Check That It Is Actually Secure

  1. Try to move the seat side to side. It should not shift.
  2. Lift the front slightly — there should be no play at the hinges.
  3. If it still moves, tighten a small fraction more and re-check. Repeat rather than tightening hard in one go.
  4. If it keeps coming loose, the bolt or washer is probably the wrong size for the hole. Replace with the correct size rather than tightening harder.
  5. Close the lid and seat and check that both sit flat and aligned.

Step 7: Finish Up

  1. Snap the hinge covers closed.
  2. Wipe down the seat and the bowl.
  3. Check for any leak or movement after a day or two of use, and tighten a fraction if needed — new fittings settle slightly.

The mechanism is that the fastening is a blind, low-tolerance joint in a brittle material. Success comes from letting the rubber washer do the work and from never using force as a substitute for patience.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if I need a round or elongated toilet seat?

Measure from the centre of the hinge bolt holes to the front edge of the bowl. Round is roughly 16.5 inches (42 cm); elongated is roughly 18.5 inches (47 cm). An elongated bowl is visibly oval, but measuring is more reliable than looking.

How do I remove a toilet seat with seized bolts?

Apply penetrating oil to the threads from underneath and let it sit for at least ten minutes. Hold the bolt head from above with a screwdriver while loosening the nut. If it still will not move, cut through the bolt with a hacksaw blade, protecting the porcelain with a cloth — never pry against the bowl.

How tight should toilet seat bolts be?

Hand-tight plus about a quarter turn with a wrench. The rubber expansion washer does the gripping and sealing. Overtightening cracks the porcelain, which is the most expensive thing that can go wrong on this job.

Why does my toilet seat keep coming loose?

Usually the wrong bolt or washer size for the hole, or debris left under the hinge. Clean the area, fit the correct size, and tighten in small increments while checking for movement. If it still loosens, replace the hardware rather than tightening harder.

Why does the whole bolt spin when I try to undo the nut?

The nut has corroded onto the bolt. Hold the bolt head firmly from above with a screwdriver or pliers; if that is not possible, cut the bolt between the nut and the bowl.

Can I replace a toilet seat without removing the toilet?

Yes. The seat bolts are independent of the toilet's floor fixings. You work from above and below the bowl at the hinge only; the toilet itself stays in place.

What size bolts do toilet seats use?

Most use a standard size, but the bolt and rubber washer must match the hole in your bowl. If the seat came with hardware, use it; if the holes are worn or oversized, a slightly larger washer or an oversized repair kit is the fix.

How do I stop the seat from shifting side to side?

Check that the hinge slots are adjusted so the seat sits square, then tighten incrementally and test after each small turn. Persistent shifting usually means the washer is not gripping, which is a size problem rather than a tightness problem.

Should I replace the hinge bolts when fitting a new seat?

Yes, whenever possible. New bolts and rubber washers are supplied with most seats, and reusing corroded old hardware is a common cause of a seat that will not stay tight.
