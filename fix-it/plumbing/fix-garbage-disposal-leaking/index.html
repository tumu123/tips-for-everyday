How to Fix a Garbage Disposal That Leaks

Fix It Plumbing Published October 17, 2026 6 min read
Advertisement

A leaking garbage disposal has three possible sources, and they are not interchangeable fixes. Guessing costs money and achieves nothing, so spend five minutes finding the actual source first.

The three leak locations:

WhereWhat is failingUsual fix
Top — at the sink openingSink flange seal or mounting bolts have loosenedRe-seat or re-seal the flange
Side — at the dishwasher or discharge connectionHose clamp or gasket at the connectionTighten or replace the clamp and gasket
Bottom — from the unit bodyInternal seal failureUsually replace the unit

The one that matters most is the bottom leak, because it is the one that usually means the disposal is at the end of its life. The top two are ordinary maintenance.

Step 0: Find the Source (Do Not Skip This)

Dry everything thoroughly with towels — inside the cabinet, on the disposal body, and on the pipes.

Put a shallow pan or a sheet of paper towel under the disposal, then run water into the disposal for thirty seconds without turning it on.

Look for where the first drop appears, and trace it upward. Water runs down and along, so the lowest wet spot is often not the source.

Use a flashlight and, if you have one, a small mirror to see the top of the unit where it meets the sink.

Repeat the test with the disposal running (with water) — some leaks only appear under vibration, particularly at the dishwasher connection.

Step 1: Rule Out the Simple Things First

Before assuming the disposal is at fault, check two other things that present identically.

Is it actually the sink, not the disposal? Water can run down the outside of the sink basin from a leak at the faucet base or the sprayer hose, and pool in the cabinet looking exactly like a disposal leak. Dry everything and watch.

Is the dishwasher the source? A dishwasher whose drain hose connection is loose will leak only during the drain cycle, which can be long after anyone used the sink. If the puddle appears at odd times, check the dishwasher hose.

Step 2: Fix a Top Leak — Tighten the Mounting First

A leak at the sink flange is usually a mounting assembly that has worked loose.

Look under the sink at the mounting ring. There are typically three bolts that pull the whole assembly up tight against the sink.

Try tightening them first. Tighten evenly, a little at a time on each, going around rather than fully tightening one. This alone stops many flange leaks.

Do not overtighten — the assembly compresses a gasket, and more is not better.

If tightening does not stop it, the seal itself has failed and you need to reset the flange.

Step 3: Reset the Sink Flange

This is a straightforward but fiddly job. Turn off the power at the breaker first — the disposal is hardwired or plugged in under the sink, and you will be working right next to it.

Disconnect the disposal from the mounting assembly. Most units release by rotating the mounting ring with a screwdriver or an Allen wrench, which lets the unit drop free. Support the unit — it is heavy.

Remove the old putty or sealant from the flange and the sink opening with a putty knife. Clean both surfaces properly.

Apply a fresh bead of plumber’s putty (or silicone, depending on your sink material — check what is compatible with stainless or composite) under the flange.

Re-seat the flange, reinstall the mounting assembly from below, and tighten the bolts evenly.

Reattach the disposal, remove any excess putty from inside the sink, and test.

Step 4: Fix a Side Leak at the Dishwasher Connection

The dishwasher drain hose connects to a small nipple on the side of the disposal.

Check the hose clamp. Clamps loosen over time from vibration, and this is a very common leak source.

Tighten the clamp, and if the hose itself is cracked or hardened, replace it. A hose that has gone rigid will not seal regardless of clamp tension.

Make sure the hose is pushed fully onto the nipple before tightening, and position the clamp over the nipple’s barb if there is one.

If your disposal has a knock-out plug that was never removed when the dishwasher was connected, that is a separate issue worth checking — the dishwasher cannot drain properly through a blocked port.

Step 5: Fix a Side Leak at the Discharge Tube

The discharge tube is the connection where waste leaves the disposal and enters the drain piping.

The gasket there degrades with age and vibration. Replacement gaskets are usually part of an installation kit, or available separately.

Remove the screws holding the discharge tube, replace the gasket, and reinstall with the screws tightened evenly.

Do not overtighten these screws — they are small and the flange they clamp against can crack.

Step 6: Deal With a Bottom Leak

If water is coming from the disposal body itself, the internal seals have failed.

There is no realistic DIY repair for this. The unit is sealed, and the cost of parts and labour to open and reseal it exceeds the cost of a replacement in essentially every case.

Confirm it before you buy. Run the water test once more and watch the very bottom of the housing. If water seeps from the body rather than from a joint, that is the diagnosis.

A useful rule of thumb: if the disposal is more than about eight years old and leaking from the bottom, replace it. If it is under warranty, check the warranty first — many units carry five to ten years.

Step 7: Test Properly

Run water through with the unit off, then with it on. Let it run for a full minute each time.

Check with a dry paper towel rather than by eye — wipe the joints and look at the towel. Small seeps are invisible but show immediately on paper.

Leave a dry towel under the unit overnight and check it in the morning. Some leaks are slow enough to miss during a short test.

Why This Works

The method works because it converts an ambiguous symptom — “there is water under the sink” — into a specific location, and each location has a bounded set of causes.

Gravity is why the location test has to be done on dry surfaces. Water from the sink flange travels down the outside of the unit and drips from the bottom, so a bottom drip frequently means a top leak. Drying first and watching for the very first drop is what distinguishes them.

Testing with the unit running as well as off matters because vibration changes the picture. The dishwasher connection in particular is subject to shaking, and a joint that holds under static water can weep when the motor runs.

The reason a bottom leak means replacement is economic rather than technical. The seals are internal, the housing is not designed to be opened in service, and a new unit costs less than the labour of resealing an old one. Recognizing that saves a pointless afternoon.

What NOT to Do

Do not replace the disposal before finding the leak. Most disposal leaks are at the top or side, and those are cheap fixes.

Do not put your hand into a disposal. Even switched off. Disconnect power and use tools.

Do not work on the disposal without switching off the breaker. You will be working directly beside an appliance with a cutting mechanism.

Do not use chemical drain cleaners in a leaking disposal. They do not fix leaks and they damage the seals further.

Do not overtighten the mounting bolts or discharge screws. Evenly snug is correct; more cracks things.

Do not ignore a slow drip. It rots the cabinet base, which is a far more expensive repair than the disposal.

Do not assume the leak is the disposal. A faucet base leak or a dishwasher hose produces the identical puddle. Confirm the source.

Do not skip the overnight test. Slow leaks do not show up in a one-minute check.

FAQ

Can a leaking disposal be repaired, or does it need replacing? Depends entirely on where it leaks. Top and side leaks are normal repairs. A leak from the unit body usually means replacement.

How do I know if it is leaking from the bottom? Dry everything, put paper towel underneath, run water, and watch where the first drop appears. Water that seeps from the housing rather than from a joint is a body leak.

Is a disposal leak dangerous electrically? Water near an electrical appliance under a sink is worth taking seriously. Switch off the breaker before working on it, and do not leave a known leak running.

Why does it only leak when I run the disposal? Vibration, or a leak at a joint that only opens under movement. The dishwasher hose connection is the usual candidate.

How long do garbage disposals last? Commonly eight to fifteen years depending on use and what goes into them. Hard items, grease, and fibrous material shorten that.

Can I use plumber’s putty on any sink material? Check compatibility. Some composite and stone sinks require silicone rather than putty, and putty can stain certain materials.

My disposal is leaking but is only two years old — is it covered? Many units carry a five to ten year warranty. Check the documentation before you buy a replacement.

Should I replace the whole mounting assembly when I change the disposal? Usually the new unit comes with one. If you are reseating an existing flange, replacing the gasket is sensible while you are there.
