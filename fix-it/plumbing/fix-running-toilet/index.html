How to Fix a Running Toilet (and What It Costs You)

Fix It Plumbing Published October 14, 2026 8 min read

A toilet that runs for 30 seconds after a flush is normal. A toilet that keeps running, hisses, or refills in short bursts when nobody has touched it is losing water continuously — and the cost is larger than most people assume.

How much it costs: a flapper that leaks steadily can waste 200 gallons (about 750 liters) per day, which is roughly 6,000 gallons a month. A slower, intermittent leak still wastes tens of gallons a day. In most water systems that is a visible line on the bill, and in a metered sewer system you pay for it twice.

The good news: nearly every running toilet is caused by one of four parts, and three of them are inexpensive and easy to replace.

Step 0: Diagnose which part is failing

Lift the tank lid and watch a full cycle. Each symptom points at a specific part.

SymptomLikely causeFix
Water keeps trickling into the bowl after the flush settlesFlapper not sealingReplace the flapper
Water level rises past the overflow tube and runs into the bowlFill valve set too high, or fill valve failingAdjust float, or replace fill valve
Toilet refills by itself for a few seconds, repeatedly, hours laterSlow flapper leakReplace the flapper
Hissing sound with the tank fullFill valve failing to shut offReplace fill valve
Water level slowly drops in the tank with no flushFlapper or flush valve seatReplace flapper, check seat
Water runs and the tank never fillsFill valve or supply issueCheck supply valve, inspect fill valve

The dye test. Put a few drops of food coloring or a dye tablet in the tank. Do not flush. Wait 15 minutes. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. This is the definitive test and takes no tools.

The shutoff test. Turn the supply valve off at the wall. If the running stops, the problem is in the tank (flapper or fill valve) and you can fix it yourself. If water still runs into the bowl with the supply off, the leak is in the flush valve seat or below the tank, and the tank may need to come off.

A rocking toilet stresses the wax seal until it leaks — stopping a toilet that rocks covers the flange and shim fix.

Step 1: Turn off the water and drain the tank

  1. Close the supply valve behind or beside the toilet, turning clockwise until it stops. If it will not turn or snaps, stop — that valve needs replacing by a plumber before you proceed.
  2. Flush the toilet to empty most of the tank.
  3. Sponge out the remaining water with a sponge or a small cup. Work from the low end. You want the tank dry enough to see the parts clearly and to avoid soaking your hands.
  4. Lay towels around the base, because a wet flapper job makes a mess.

Now you have a dry tank, a visible flapper, fill valve, and overflow tube, and no water pressure. Everything from here is mechanical.

Step 2: Replace the flapper (the most common fix)

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush and drops to seal the flush valve. It is the number one cause of a running toilet, and it is the cheapest part in the tank.

Why flappers fail: the rubber hardens, warps, and mineral deposits build up on the sealing edge. A flapper that is more than about five years old is a strong suspect.

  1. Disconnect the flapper from the flush lever chain — usually a clip or a hook at the top.
  2. Slide the flapper off the overflow tube pegs. Most systems have two flexible tabs that slip over two small pegs on the overflow tube. Pull gently.
  3. Take the old flapper to the store or photograph the tank model number. Match the size and shape; flappers are not universal.
  4. Inspect the valve seat where the flapper seals. Run a finger around the rim. If it feels rough or has mineral crust, clean it gently with a rag or fine abrasive pad — a rough seat will destroy a new flapper within weeks.
  5. Install the new flapper on the pegs and reconnect the chain.
  6. Set the chain length so that there is about half an inch (1 cm) of slack. Too tight and the flapper cannot seal; too loose and the chain tangles under the flapper. This is a common reason a brand-new flapper still leaks.

Refill and test. Open the supply valve, let the tank fill, and flush. Watch a full cycle. If the water stops cleanly and the tank is silent, done. If it still trickles, recheck the chain slack and the seat.

Step 3: Adjust or replace the fill valve

If the flapper is fine and the toilet still runs, the fill valve is the next suspect. The fill valve refills the tank after each flush and shuts off when the water reaches the set level.

First, try adjusting the level

The water level should sit about 1 inch (2.5 cm) below the top of the overflow tube. If it is above the tube, water constantly spills into the bowl and the toilet runs forever.

  • Float-cup or float-ball type: adjust the screw or clip to lower the float. Turn the adjustment screw counterclockwise or slide the clip down.
  • Modern diaphragm type (common in newer toilets): turn the height adjustment on the shaft, or squeeze the clip and slide the valve body down.

If the level will not adjust, the diaphragm inside is failing and the valve needs replacement.

Replacing the fill valve

  1. Shut off supply and empty the tank (Step 1).
  2. Disconnect the supply line from the bottom of the fill valve with pliers. Expect a little water; have a towel ready.
  3. Loosen the lock nut under the tank and lift the old fill valve out.
  4. Install the new valve, matching the height so the critical level mark sits above the overflow tube, and tighten the lock nut by hand plus a quarter turn. Do not overtighten plastic.
  5. Reconnect the supply line and the refill tube. The refill tube must clip into the top of the overflow tube, not hang inside the tank — if it is not clipped, the bowl will not refill properly.
  6. Open the supply valve slowly and check for leaks at the connections.
  7. Adjust the water level to about 1 inch below the overflow tube top.

Step 4: Check the flush valve seat and the flapper’s other failure modes

If a new flapper still leaks, the problem is usually one of these:

  • Mineral buildup on the seat. Clean it with a mild descaler or a fine abrasive pad, then rinse thoroughly.
  • A warped or cracked flush valve seat. This is the plastic rim the flapper seals against. If a new flapper cannot seal on it, the seat is damaged and the standard repair is a flapper with a larger, softer sealing ring, or replacing the flush valve assembly (a bigger job that may need the tank removed).
  • Chain interference. The chain hangs under the flapper or the flapper catches on the chain. Shorten or reroute.
  • Wrong flapper size. A flapper that is too small or too stiff will not seal. Match the model.

A note on chemical tank cleaners. Drop-in bleach tablets are a leading cause of premature flapper failure — they degrade the rubber and corrode the fill valve. If your toilet runs every year or two, stop using them. They cost far more than they save.

Step 5: Verify the fix and confirm the savings

After the repair, do the dye test again: dye in the tank, wait 15 minutes, check for color in the bowl. No color means you are done.

Then confirm the water meter is not moving. With every fixture in the house off, watch the meter for a couple of minutes. If it is still, the leak is fixed.

What the repair is worth: replacing a flapper is a small parts cost, often under the price of two coffees, and takes about fifteen minutes. If the running toilet was wasting 200 gallons a day, that single part is one of the highest-return repairs available in a home.

Why This Works

The toilet has only two water paths: the flush valve (controlled by the flapper) and the fill valve. Any continuous water loss is one of those two failing. That is why the diagnosis table is short — the system is genuinely simple.

The dye test works because the tank is a closed volume. If water crosses from the tank into the bowl with no flush, it can only do so through the flush valve, which points at the flapper or its seat.

Replacing rather than adjusting is the right move for the flapper because the failure mode is material degradation. You cannot re-soften hardened rubber. For the fill valve, adjustment is worth trying first because a float that is simply set too high is a five-minute fix with no parts.

Avoiding drop-in bleach tablets matters for the same reason: the parts that fail are rubber and plastic, and oxidizing bleach attacks both. Removing it from the tank is a maintenance decision, not a cleaning preference.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water does a running toilet actually waste?

A steady flapper leak can waste around 200 gallons per day. Even a slow, intermittent leak wastes tens of gallons daily. Over a month that is thousands of gallons, and if your sewer charges are metered you pay for the wasted water twice.

Can I fix a running toilet myself?

Yes, in most cases. Flapper and fill valve replacement are beginner-level repairs that need only a screwdriver or pliers. The main exception is a damaged flush valve seat or a seized supply valve, both of which are reasonable points to call a plumber.

Why does my toilet run for a few seconds at random times?

A slow flapper leak lets the tank level drop gradually, and the fill valve tops it up briefly when the level falls far enough. That short spontaneous refill is a classic flapper symptom, even when the toilet looks fine during a flush.

How do I know if the flapper or the fill valve is the problem?

Run the dye test for the flapper. If no dye crosses into the bowl but the tank overfills or the toilet hisses with a full tank, the fill valve is the culprit. The two tests separate the two parts cleanly.

How often should a flapper be replaced?

Expect three to five years from a flapper with normal use, and much less if tank bleach tablets are used. If you cannot remember replacing it, it is a candidate.

Do bleach tablets really damage toilets?

Yes. Oxidizing bleach attacks the rubber flapper and the plastic and rubber parts of the fill valve, and it commonly voids toilet warranties. A bowl cleaner applied to the bowl avoids the problem entirely.

What if the water level keeps rising even after adjustment?

If the level will not stay below the overflow tube after adjustment, the fill valve diaphragm is failing and the valve needs replacement. This is a straightforward swap with a repair kit or a complete valve.

Is it worth calling a plumber for a running toilet?

Only after you have checked the flapper and the fill valve, which cover the overwhelming majority of cases. If you have replaced both and it still runs, or if the supply valve is seized or the tank needs removing, that is when a plumber earns the call.
