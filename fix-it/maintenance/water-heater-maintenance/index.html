How to Keep Your Water Heater Running Longer

Fix It Maintenance Published October 14, 2026 8 min read
Advertisement

A water heater is one of the most expensive appliances in a home to replace, and it is also one of the easiest to extend. Two maintenance tasks — flushing sediment and replacing the anode rod — add years to its life and cost very little.

How long it should last:

TypeTypical lifespanWith maintenance
Tank, gas8–12 years12–15 years
Tank, electric10–15 years15–20 years
Tankless20 years20+ years with descaling

The difference between a heater that lasts eight years and one that lasts fifteen is usually sediment and the anode rod.

Step 0: Identify what you have and where it is

Before doing anything, confirm four things:

  1. The type — gas or electric, tank or tankless. This determines which tasks apply.
  2. The age. Find the serial number on the label and decode the manufacture date. Most manufacturers encode the year in the serial number. If the heater is over ten years old, maintenance extends life, but plan for replacement.
  3. The shutoffs — the gas valve or the electrical breaker, and the cold-water shutoff valve on the inlet pipe.
  4. Where the drain goes — the drain valve needs a hose route to a floor drain, a sump, or outside.

For gas heaters: note the flue and the burner access. Do not tamper with gas fittings yourself; call a professional if the burner or gas line needs work.

For electric heaters: the power must be off at the breaker before draining, or the upper heating element will burn out.

Step 1: Flush the tank (annually)

Sediment — minerals that settle out of the water — is the primary killer of tank water heaters. It accumulates at the bottom, insulates the burner or heating element from the water, and forces the unit to work harder.

Symptoms that sediment is already a problem:

  • Popping, rumbling, or banging sounds during heating
  • Reduced hot water capacity
  • Higher energy bills for the same usage
  • Discolored or cloudy hot water

The flushing procedure

  1. Turn off the heat source. For gas, set the thermostat to “pilot” or off. For electric, turn off the breaker.
  2. Turn off the cold-water inlet valve at the top of the tank.
  3. Connect a garden hose to the drain valve at the bottom and route it to a floor drain, sump, or outside. Hot water will damage grass, so route it carefully.
  4. Open a hot-water faucet somewhere in the house. This prevents a vacuum lock that would stop the tank from draining.
  5. Open the drain valve and let the tank drain. Full draining lets you flush sediment out completely.
  6. Once drained, briefly open the cold-water inlet to stir up and flush the remaining sediment out the drain valve. Repeat a few times until the water runs clear.
  7. Close the drain valve, remove the hose.
  8. Close the hot-water faucet, then open the cold-water inlet valve to refill the tank.
  9. Wait until the tank is completely full before restoring power or gas. Opening a hot faucet and confirming water flows steadily tells you it is full.
  10. Restore the heat source.

The most dangerous step is step 9. Turning on an electric water heater with a partially filled tank burns out the upper element almost immediately. Confirm full water flow from a hot tap before you restore electricity.

If the drain valve clogs with sediment and will not drain, close it, and call a plumber — attempting to force it can break the valve and release the entire tank contents.

Tankless heaters: descale instead

Tankless units do not accumulate sediment in a tank, but they do accumulate scale in the heat exchanger.

  • Descale annually in hard water areas, or as the manufacturer recommends.
  • Use a descaling kit with a pump that circulates a vinegar or citric acid solution through the unit.
  • Symptoms of scale: reduced hot water output, fluctuating temperature, error codes.
  • A water softener significantly reduces descaling frequency.

Step 2: Replace the anode rod (every few years)

The anode rod is the single most effective life-extender, and most homeowners have never heard of it.

How it works: the anode rod is a sacrificial metal rod inside the tank. Because of the electrochemical properties of the metals, the rod corrodes instead of the tank’s steel lining. It is designed to be consumed so the tank is not.

When it is consumed, the tank starts to corrode. That is why heater failures are almost always tank leaks rather than failed heating elements.

Checking and replacing

  1. Locate the anode rod. It is a hex-headed fitting on the top of the tank, usually a few inches from the hot-water outlet.
  2. Turn off the water and power and drain a few gallons to relieve pressure.
  3. Use a socket or wrench to unscrew the rod. It is often very tight, and a breaker bar may be needed.
  4. Inspect the rod. If more than about 50 percent is consumed, or if it is coated in heavy calcium, replace it.
  5. Install the new rod with Teflon tape on the threads, tightening firmly but not over-tightening.
  6. Refill the tank fully and restore power before use.

How often: check every two to three years depending on water quality. In hard or aggressive water, more often.

Types of anode rods:

TypeBest for
MagnesiumStandard choice; works well in most water
AluminumSofter water; less suited to hard water
Powered (electric)Longest life; works in all water types
Aluminum-zincWater with a sulfur smell, or where magnesium causes odor

If your hot water smells like sulfur, the anode rod is often the cause. Switching to an aluminum-zinc rod usually eliminates the smell.

Some tanks have a combination anode and hot outlet, making replacement harder. If the rod will not come out, a plumber can advise.

Step 3: Do the simple checks

These take minutes and catch problems early.

CheckFrequencyWhat it catches
Look for leaks or corrosion at fittings, the drain valve, and the tank baseMonthlyEarly tank or fitting failure
Check for rust in the hot waterMonthlyTank corrosion
Test the temperature and pressure relief (TPR) valveAnnuallyA safety valve that prevents a pressure explosion
Check the flue and draft on gas unitsAnnuallyCombustion safety and CO risk
Check the pilot light or ignition on gas unitsAs neededIgnition problems
Vacuum around the base and the burner area (gas)AnnuallyDust and lint that affect combustion

Check for water on the floorMonthlyIndicates a leak, which becomes damage

The TPR valve test is a genuine safety check. The temperature and pressure relief valve prevents the tank from exploding if pressure or temperature exceeds limits. Test it annually: lift the lever briefly and confirm water discharges, then release and confirm it reseats. If it drips afterward or does not discharge, have it replaced.

The 120°F setting is a good default. It is hot enough for most household use, reduces scald risk, and reduces standby heat loss. Some dishwashers require 140°F, so check your appliance guidance if you have one.

Step 4: Reduce the workload

Beyond direct maintenance, a few changes reduce stress on the heater.

  • Fix dripping hot-water faucets. A leaking hot tap constantly draws fresh cold water into the tank, so the heater reheats continuously. This is a significant and often overlooked energy cost.
  • Install low-flow fixtures to reduce hot-water demand.
  • Add a water heater blanket to an older tank in a cold space, if the manufacturer allows and if the tank is not insulated already.
  • Insulate the hot-water pipes near the heater — the first few feet especially.
  • Install a water softener in hard water areas. This is the single biggest factor in sediment and scale, and it affects both tank and tankless units.
  • Consider a timer or an occupancy-based setting for periods when hot water is not needed, though standby losses on modern tanks are modest.

Step 5: Know when to replace instead

Maintenance extends life, but a failing tank should be replaced before it floods the home.

Replace now (or very soon)

SignWhy
Water pooling at the base of the tankThe tank has failed and will leak more, not less
Rust-colored hot water from the tankInternal corrosion, usually the tank lining
Visible corrosion or bulging on the tank bodyStructural failure is imminent
Leaks at any fitting on a corroded tankFurther corrosion is likely
Rumbling or popping that persists after flushingHeavy sediment or scale that flushing cannot remove
A tank over 12–15 years old with any symptomPast expected life; replace rather than repair
Repeated element failures (electric)Suggests internal damage
The tank has already leaked onceIt will leak again

The base-of-tank rule is the important one. Water pooling at the bottom of a tank usually means the tank itself has failed, not a fitting. No repair fixes a split tank, and it should be replaced immediately to prevent water damage.

What to do if the tank is leaking right now:

  1. Shut off the cold-water inlet to the heater.
  2. Turn off the heat source — gas to pilot, or electricity at the breaker.
  3. Shut off the main water if the leak is significant.
  4. Drain the tank to reduce the volume of water that can escape.
  5. Call a plumber.

For gas heaters, have a professional inspect combustion, the flue, and the gas connections at least every other year. Carbon monoxide from a poorly vented water heater is a genuine hazard, and it cannot be detected by smell.

Why This Works

Sediment and anode consumption are the two mechanisms that end a tank water heater’s life, and both have a direct countermeasure.

Sediment settles out of hard water and accumulates at the tank bottom. It insulates the water from the burner or element, so more energy is needed for the same result. It also traps water underneath, causing localized overheating of the tank lining. Flushing removes the sediment before it accumulates to the point of damaging the lining. The popping and rumbling sounds people report are literally steam forming under a layer of sediment.

The anode rod is a deliberate electrochemical sacrifice. Two dissimilar metals in contact with an electrolyte — which is what water is — form a galvanic cell. The less noble metal corrodes first. By making the anode rod less noble than the steel tank, the rod corrodes and the tank survives. This is why tank failures are so rarely caused by the heating element: the element is not the part designed to fail last.

The 120°F thermostat setting reduces two things at once: scald risk and the rate of heat loss from the tank to the surrounding air, since the greater the temperature difference, the faster the loss.

The dripping-hot-faucet point is a direct consequence of how a tank works. A tank holds a fixed volume of hot water. Every draw is replaced by cold water that must then be heated. A dripping hot tap becomes a continuous small draw, so the heater cycles repeatedly, which is far more expensive than the drip itself suggests.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should a water heater be flushed? Annually for most homes. In areas with hard water, twice a year. The flush removes sediment, which is the leading cause of premature tank failure and reduced efficiency.

What does the anode rod do? It is a sacrificial metal rod that corrodes instead of the tank’s steel lining, through a galvanic reaction. When it is consumed, the tank itself begins to corrode. Replacing it is the single most effective way to extend a tank heater’s life.

How often should the anode rod be replaced? Check it every two to three years and replace it when about half is consumed, or when it is heavily coated in calcium. In aggressive or hard water, check more often.

Why does my hot water smell like sulfur? Usually the anode rod reacting with bacteria in the water. Switching from a magnesium to an aluminum-zinc anode rod typically eliminates the smell. A water heater flush also helps.

What is the TPR valve and should I test it? It is the temperature and pressure relief valve, a safety device that releases water if the tank exceeds safe limits. Test it annually by lifting the lever briefly and confirming water discharges, then confirming it reseats. If it drips or does not discharge, replace it.

Can I replace an anode rod myself? Often yes, if you are comfortable with basic plumbing and can access the rod. It can be very tight and may require a breaker bar. If the rod is a combination anode and hot outlet, or will not budge, a plumber is the safer call.

What temperature should I set my water heater to? 120°F (49°C) is a good default for efficiency and scald safety. Some dishwashers require 140°F (60°C), so check your appliance guidance. Never exceed the manufacturer’s recommendation.

How long does a water heater last? Eight to twelve years for a gas tank, ten to fifteen for an electric tank, and twenty or more for a tankless unit, with maintenance. Lack of maintenance commonly shortens these by several years.

Should I repair or replace a water heater? Replace if the tank is leaking, if there is rust-colored water, if the tank body shows corrosion or bulging, or if it is past 12–15 years old with any symptom. Repair is reasonable for a failed element, thermostat, or valve on an otherwise sound tank. A leaking tank is never a repair.
