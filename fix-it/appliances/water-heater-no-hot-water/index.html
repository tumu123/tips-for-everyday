No Hot Water? What to Check Before You Call Anyone

Fix It Appliances Published October 24, 2026 11 min read

Hot water almost never fails all at once for no reason. It fails in one of a small number of ways, and each way leaves a different fingerprint: no hot water anywhere, hot water that runs out in three minutes, hot water at one tap but not another, or water that is warm but never hot.

Reading that fingerprint first is what separates a ten-minute fix from a $400 service call — or from a call you didn’t need to make at all.

This guide walks the three heater types separately, because a gas heater and an electric heater with identical symptoms have nothing in common. Checking a gas unit with electric-heater logic wastes an afternoon and can be dangerous.

The single most useful distinction: is the heater failing to make hot water, or failing to deliver it? A unit that runs and heats but delivers nothing usually has a plumbing problem, not an appliance problem. That changes everything about where you look.

If the tank itself is wet at the base — not condensation, but a persistent ring of water — stop here. A leaking tank cannot be repaired, and the priority becomes shutting off the supply and replacing the unit. Repair or replace is an easy call in that case.

Step 2: Confirm It Is Not a Shutoff or a Breaker

Before diagnosing an appliance, rule out the two things that imitate a broken appliance.

Electric: find the breaker labelled for the water heater — often a double-pole 30-amp breaker, sometimes labelled “WH” or “H2O.” Look for one that is tripped (sitting between ON and OFF) or fully off. A tripped double-pole breaker is easy to misread; push it fully to OFF, then firmly to ON. If it trips again immediately, do not keep resetting it. That is a fault inside the circuit, and resetting a breaker that keeps tripping is the wrong move — the breaker is doing its job.

Many electric heaters also have a high-temperature limit switch — a small red button under the upper access panel. If the unit overheated once, this switch pops and stays popped. Pressing it back in restores power. This is common after a heating element fails, so if it pops twice, the element is the real problem.

Gas: check that the gas shutoff valve at the unit is open (handle parallel to the pipe), and confirm other gas appliances still work. A pilot light that is out is visible through the sight glass — but on most modern units the flame is electronic ignition, so there is nothing to light manually.

If you smell gas at all, leave the house and call the gas utility from outside. Do not troubleshoot, do not flip switches, do not use a phone inside. This is not a situation where a cautious attempt is reasonable.

Step 3: Gas Heaters — Pilot, Thermocouple, or Flame Sensor

On a standing-pilot unit, the sequence is mechanical and predictable.

The pilot is out. Relighting is a defined procedure printed on the tank’s label. The critical detail most people miss: hold the gas control knob down for a full 30–60 seconds after the flame lights before releasing. The thermocouple needs that time to heat and hold the valve open. Release early and the pilot goes out again — which is often misread as a broken thermocouple.

The pilot lights but won’t stay lit. That points to the thermocouple — a small probe sitting in the pilot flame that generates the millivolts holding the gas valve open. They fail with age, and replacement is a cheap part. On a unit older than ten years, this is one of the few genuinely worthwhile repairs.

The pilot stays lit but the burner never fires. The thermostat is calling for heat and nothing happens. On a flame-sensor unit, a dirty sensor reads “no flame” and shuts the gas off — cleaning it with fine steel wool often restores operation.

The burner fires and shuts off after a minute or two. Suspect the flue. A blocked or backdrafting flue trips the safety shutoff, and this is a carbon monoxide risk, not a convenience problem. A water heater backdrafting into a basement is a serious hazard. Stop using it and get it inspected.

Step 4: Electric Heaters — Elements and Limits

Electric heaters are simpler and easier to diagnose, but the diagnosis requires a multimeter and comfort working near live 240-volt circuits.

Two elements, two thermostats. A typical tank has an upper element and thermostat, and a lower element and thermostat. They are wired so only one runs at a time: the upper element heats the top of the tank first, then power hands off to the lower one. This is why a failed lower element produces “some hot water, then cold” rather than no hot water at all — and why that symptom is so often misdiagnosed as a small tank.

The classic sequence after a power interruption:

  1. Reset the breaker. Confirm it is actually on, not just not-tripped.
  2. Open the upper access panel and press the red high-limit reset button. It clicks if it was tripped.
  3. If it clicks and trips again within a day, the upper element or thermostat has failed. Stop resetting.
  4. Test the elements with a multimeter on the resistance setting, power off, wires disconnected. A good element reads in the 10–16 ohm range; an open circuit (infinite resistance) means a failed element.

Testing elements is a reasonable DIY job for someone who has used a multimeter before. If you have not, this is not the appliance to learn on — 240 volts at 30 amps is unforgiving, and the access panels expose live terminals even with the thermostat turned down.

The temperature setting itself is worth checking before anything else — a thermostat knocked to “vacation” during a trip explains a surprising number of cold showers. What temperature to set covers the trade-off between safety, savings, and how much hot water you actually get.

Step 5: Tankless Heaters — Error Codes First

A tankless unit will tell you what is wrong if you read the display. Common codes across brands cluster around four causes: insufficient water flow, ignition failure, scale buildup, and a venting fault.

Low flow is the most frequent complaint. Tankless units need a minimum flow rate to fire — often around 0.5 gallons per minute. A clogged inlet filter screen, a partially closed valve, or a flow restrictor in a low-flow showerhead can all drop below that threshold. The heater is fine; it simply never turns on.

Scale is the second. In hard water, the heat exchanger scales up and the unit either derates or shuts down on overheat. Annual descaling with vinegar is the maintenance interval, and skipping it for years is what turns a $40 service into a $600 repair.

Ignition failure on a gas tankless is usually the same thermocouple-and-sensor family of problems as a tank unit, plus the added variable of gas pressure. If other gas appliances work and the tankless unit still won’t fire, gas pressure or the unit’s own regulator is the suspect — a professional call.

Cold water sandwich — hot, then cold, then hot again — is a specific tankless behaviour, not a failure. It happens when the unit is oversized relative to draw or when a recirculation system is cycling. It is annoying rather than broken.

Step 6: When the Water Is Hot at One Tap and Cold at Another

This is the symptom people most often misattribute to the heater, and it almost never is.

A single-handle faucet or shower valve with a failed cartridge can let hot and cold cross inside the valve body. The heater is producing hot water perfectly; the fixture is mixing it away before it reaches the spout. The tell: the tap in question never gets hot even when a nearby tap does.

A crossover is the wider version. If a single-handle fixture, a washing machine valve, or a newly installed appliance connects hot to cold, hot water flows into the cold line. Symptoms: warm water on the cold side, and hot water that runs out suspiciously fast everywhere in the house because hot is escaping into the cold system.

How to test for a crossover in five minutes: shut off the water heater’s cold inlet valve. Open a hot tap. If water keeps running, hot and cold are connected somewhere — that run is coming through the crossover.

Step 7: The Dip Tube Failure Nobody Diagnoses

Inside every tank heater is a plastic dip tube that carries cold water to the bottom of the tank. When it cracks — common in heaters from certain manufacturing years, and in any heater past 15 years — cold water enters near the top instead of the bottom, and mixes with the hot water on its way out.

The result is a heater that runs constantly, delivers lukewarm water, and produces a short hot-water window. Everything looks fine: the elements test good, the thermostat is correct, the tank is not leaking.

It is also a plumbing-hazard issue. Fragments of a disintegrating dip tube can clog aerators and showerheads throughout the house. If you find small white or grey plastic bits in your faucet screens, that is the dip tube.

Replacement requires removing the cold inlet nipple — a job that is often doable but depends entirely on the fittings and how corroded they are. On an old tank, this is the point where replacement usually wins.

Step 8: Sediment, the Slow Failure

Minerals settle out of hard water and accumulate on the tank bottom. Two consequences: the burner or element has to heat through an insulating layer, and the tank makes a popping or rumbling sound as steam forms under the sediment.

Sediment also shortens the tank’s life by trapping heat against the steel floor, which accelerates corrosion from the inside. Flushing the tank annually — connecting a hose to the drain valve and running until the water clears — is the entire prevention. Water heater maintenance covers the flush procedure and the anode rod check that determines whether the tank will last 8 years or 20.

Step 9: Frozen Pipes in Winter

If hot water stopped working on the first genuinely cold morning of the year, and the heater is in an unheated space — garage, crawlspace, attic — the problem may be a frozen supply line rather than the heater itself.

The tell: the heater fires and runs but no water comes out, or flow is a trickle. A frozen line between the tank and the house creates exactly the symptom of a failed heater. Preventing frozen pipes explains the thawing order and the one thing to never do — applying open flame to a pipe.

Step 10: The Decision Point — Fix or Replace

Age of tankRepairs worth doingVerdict
Under 5 yearsAnything under warrantyRepair
5–8 yearsThermocouple, element, thermostat, dip tubeRepair
8–12 yearsElement or thermostat only, if the tank is dryCase by case
Over 12 yearsLeak, tank corrosion, repeated failuresReplace
Any age, leaking tankNoneReplace immediately

The average tank heater lasts 8–12 years. Once past ten, every repair is money spent on a unit that is already near the end, and a failure usually arrives at the worst possible moment — a weekend, a holiday, a house full of guests. Extending appliance life is about spending small amounts early so the decision never arrives on an emergency schedule.

Why This Works

Each heater type has a single chain of causation, and the symptom localises where in that chain it broke. A gas heater needs gas, ignition, a flame proving signal, and a flue that drafts. An electric heater needs two poles of power, a closed limit switch, two elements, and two thermostats. The heater either completes the chain or it doesn’t, and which link failed determines the symptom precisely.

Two-element designs explain the “runs out fast” complaint. Because the upper element heats only the top third of the tank before handing off, a failed lower element leaves you with a small reserve of genuinely hot water at the top. The tank is full of water; only the top is hot. Nothing about the tank’s size has changed.

Cross-connections masquerade as heater failure because the heater is upstream of the fault. Hot water leaving the tank is fine — it is being diluted at a fixture or lost into the cold line. Testing the heater in isolation, by shutting the cold inlet and opening a hot tap, separates the two possibilities in minutes.

Thermal limits trip for a reason. A high-limit switch that trips once is protecting against a transient overheat. One that trips repeatedly is reporting a failed element or thermostat. Resetting it a third time is not troubleshooting, it is disabling a safety device.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I wait before calling a plumber?

If you have confirmed power and gas are on, and the unit is silent, and you are not comfortable opening panels — call. If you've done Steps 2 and 3 and found nothing, the remaining causes (dip tube, crossover, flue) need either tools or a license. Two hours of your own diagnosis is reasonable; a full day is not.

Why do I get hot water for two minutes and then nothing?

Almost always a failed lower heating element on an electric tank, or a broken dip tube. Both produce a small pocket of hot water followed by cold.

Can I relight the pilot myself?

Yes, if the unit has a standing pilot and the instructions are on the label. Follow them exactly, including the hold time. If the unit has electronic ignition, there is no pilot to light.

Is it safe to reset the high-temperature switch?

Once, yes. If it trips again, no — it means the heater is overheating and something is wrong with the element or thermostat.

Why is the water hot in the shower but cold at the kitchen sink?

A failed cartridge in one fixture, or a crossover. Test by shutting the heater's cold inlet and opening a hot tap; if water still runs, hot and cold are connected.

Will a new water heater really save money?

Only marginally on energy — the difference between a 12-year-old tank and a new one is usually 10–20% of the water-heating bill, which is itself only part of the total. The real reason to replace is reliability, not savings.

What's the rotten-egg smell?

Bacteria reacting with the magnesium or aluminium anode rod, common in well water. Replacing the anode with a zinc or aluminium-zinc type often solves it.

Should I shut the heater off if I'm going away?

Set it to "vacation" rather than off — that keeps the tank from growing bacteria and avoids a full cold start. Full guidance is in the vacation checklist for your climate.
