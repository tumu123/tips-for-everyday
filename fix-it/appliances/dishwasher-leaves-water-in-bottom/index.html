Why Your Dishwasher Leaves Water in the Bottom

Fix It Appliances Published October 24, 2026 10 min read

Some water in the bottom of a dishwasher is normal. Every dishwasher leaves a small amount in the sump after the final drain — typically a quarter inch, sometimes a little more. That water sits above the drain pump’s intake and keeps the pump seal from drying out. If you’ve just run a cycle and there’s a shallow, clean layer of water covering the sump floor, nothing is wrong.

What is not normal: water deep enough to cover the filter, water that smells, water that’s still there days later, or water that reappears after you’ve mopped it out.

The distinction matters because the two situations have completely different causes, and most people who call a repair service for “water in the bottom” have the first one.

Step 1: Decide Whether It Is Actually a Fault

What you seeNormal?What it means
Shallow clear water over the sump floor, after a cycleYesDesigned retention above the pump intake
Water covering the filter or reaching the doorNoFailed drain
Water appears hours after the cycleNoBackflow from the sink drain
Water is grey, cloudy, or smellsNoLong-standing water, not a fresh drain failure
Water only after certain cyclesNoCycle-specific drain fault, often a partial clog
Water with food debris floatingNoFilter blocked, or drain pump partially obstructed

The mop-and-wait test settles it in five minutes. Soak up the water with a towel, then leave the dishwasher alone for two hours without running it.

  • Water returns → backflow from the drain line. The problem is downstream of the machine.
  • Water stays away → the machine isn’t draining during the cycle. The problem is in the machine.

That single test separates the whole diagnosis into two halves, and it’s free.

Step 2: The Filter — Still the Most Common Cause

The filter sits at the bottom of the tub, under the lower spray arm. It catches food so it doesn’t reach the pump. When it’s blocked, water can’t get to the pump intake fast enough, and the drain phase ends with water still in the sump.

Cleaning it takes ten minutes:

  1. Remove the bottom rack.
  2. Twist and lift the cylindrical filter — most models have a quarter-turn release.
  3. Lift out the flat filter screen underneath.
  4. Rinse both under hot water, scrubbing with a brush. Pay attention to the fine mesh, which traps grease as well as particles.
  5. Reach into the sump and check for debris around the pump intake — glass shards, a piece of cutlery, a bottle cap.
  6. Reassemble and run a rinse cycle.

How often: the filter should be rinsed monthly and deep-cleaned every three months. Most households never do it, which is why this is the single highest-yield check. Cleaning a dishwasher filter covers the deeper cleaning, including the spray arms and the door gasket, and the smell that comes from skipping it.

If the filter looks clean but the water is greasy or cloudy, the issue is downstream — the drain path, not the filter.

Step 3: The Drain Pump — Jammed or Failed

Dishwasher drain pumps are small, and they fail in two ways: mechanically jammed or electrically dead.

Jammed is more common. Glass shards, fruit pits, a piece of bone, or a broken piece of dishware get past the filter and lodge in the impeller. The pump hums but doesn’t move water, or it runs and the water level doesn’t drop.

Failed shows as silence. No hum at all during the drain phase.

How to check: the drain pump is usually accessible from inside the tub, under the sump cover, or from the front by removing the lower kick panel. With power off:

  1. Bail out the standing water with a cup and sponge.
  2. Remove the sump cover if your model has one.
  3. Reach into the pump intake and feel for debris. This is where glass and seeds collect.
  4. Turn the impeller by hand — it should rotate with slight resistance. Gritty or immovable means debris or a failed bearing.

If the impeller turns freely but the pump doesn’t run during a cycle, the motor or the control has failed. Pumps are $40–$90 and replacing one is a moderate DIY job on most models.

Safety: dishwashers combine water and mains voltage, and the junction box under the machine is live even with the door open. Turn off the breaker, not just the control panel.

Step 4: The Drain Hose and the High Loop

If the machine’s pump works and the filter is clean, the restriction is in the hose.

Check the hose path. From the drain pump, the hose should rise to a high point — ideally attached to the underside of the countertop or the side of the cabinet — before descending to the sink drain or garbage disposal. This high loop is what prevents dirty sink water from siphoning back into the dishwasher.

A missing or fallen high loop produces exactly the symptom of water reappearing hours after a cycle: the sink drains, the water in the trap siphons backwards through the hose, and it ends up in the dishwasher sump. If your mop-and-wait test showed water returning, the high loop is the first thing to check.

Some installations use an air gap on the countertop instead — a chrome fitting with two hoses. An air gap that’s clogged with debris blocks drainage entirely and often causes water to spit out of it. They can be disassembled and cleaned.

Check the hose for kinks and grease. The corrugated section behind the cabinet collects grease and food over years. A hose that drains slowly is often a hose that’s half-blocked. Replacement is inexpensive and more reliable than cleaning.

Step 5: The Garbage Disposal Connection

If the dishwasher drains into a garbage disposal — very common in North America — there are two specific failure modes that produce standing water.

The plug was never removed. New garbage disposals ship with a plastic plug in the dishwasher inlet port, which must be knocked out before connecting the dishwasher hose. Installers forget this regularly. The result: the dishwasher drains into a sealed port and the water comes straight back. This is the answer far more often than anyone expects on a recently installed disposal or dishwasher.

The disposal’s drain is blocked. If the disposal itself is clogged downstream, the dishwasher’s discharge has nowhere to go. Fixing a garbage disposal covers that diagnosis, and it’s worth ruling out because the two appliances share a drain path.

A shared drain that’s slow will back water into whichever appliance is lower — usually the dishwasher.

Step 6: The Drain Valve and the Check Valve

Some dishwashers use a motorised diverter valve rather than a separate drain pump. When the valve fails, the machine either won’t drain or drains continuously during the wash.

Symptoms of a failed diverter valve:

  • Water in the bottom after every cycle, with a working pump
  • The machine fills and drains at the same time (you hear water running continuously)
  • Poor wash performance because water isn’t being directed to the spray arms

The check valve — a small flap in the sump or the pump housing — prevents drained water from flowing back into the tub. A piece of debris holding it open lets water return after the drain completes. This is a cheap part and a common cause of “water comes back.”

Step 7: When the Water Is Dirty or Smelly

Standing water that has been there for days is a different problem from water that fails to drain. It means the drain has been failing slowly for a while, and the residue has had time to grow.

The smell comes from biofilm on the filter, the sump, and the door gasket. Removing kitchen odours covers the general approach; for a dishwasher specifically:

  1. Clear the water and fix the drain first. No amount of cleaning helps while water sits.
  2. Clean the filter and sump thoroughly with hot soapy water.
  3. Run a hot cycle with a cup of white vinegar in a bowl on the top rack.
  4. Then a cycle with baking soda to neutralise.
  5. Wipe the door gasket folds — this is where most of the smell lives.

Why This Works

The drain cycle is time-limited, not level-sensed. Unlike a washing machine, most dishwashers don’t measure water level during drain. The control runs the pump for a fixed period — typically 60 to 120 seconds. If flow is restricted, the pump runs out of time before the tub is empty, and the remaining water stays. A partially blocked filter therefore produces a partial drain, every cycle, indefinitely.

The high loop works on the same principle as a sink trap. Water seeking its own level will flow from the higher sink trap into the lower dishwasher sump if there is an uninterrupted path. The high loop breaks that path by putting a peak in the hose above the sink trap’s water line. Remove the peak and the two become connected vessels.

The disposal plug is a hard stop, not a restriction. A sealed port means zero flow. The dishwasher’s drain pump pushes against a dead end and the water returns through the pump’s own check valve. It presents identically to a failed pump, which is why it gets misdiagnosed on new installations.

The retained water above the pump intake is deliberate. It keeps the pump seal lubricated and prevents the pump from running dry. That’s why “some water” is normal and “water up to the filter” is not — the design intent is a shallow layer, not a pool.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it normal to have water in the bottom of the dishwasher?

A shallow layer over the sump floor, yes — that's designed retention. Water deep enough to cover the filter, or water that smells, is not normal.

Why does the water come back after I mop it out?

Backflow from the sink drain. The drain hose is missing its high loop, or the check valve is held open by debris.

How do I know if my drain pump has failed?

If the filter is clean, the hose is clear, and the water still doesn't drop, listen during the drain phase. Silence means a dead pump; a hum with no water movement means a jammed impeller.

Can I use a plunger on a dishwasher?

No. It can damage the pump and the check valve, and it won't clear a blocked filter.

Why does my dishwasher leave water only sometimes?

A partial blockage — often the filter screen — drains adequately on light cycles and fails on heavy ones. It's a clog, not an intermittent fault.

My dishwasher is new and won't drain. What's most likely?

The plug in the garbage disposal's dishwasher inlet was never removed. It's extremely common on new installations.

How often should the filter be cleaned?

Rinsed monthly, deep-cleaned every three months. Households that pre-rinse less need it more often.

Should I repair or replace?

Pump, filter, valve, and hose are all inexpensive and worth repairing on a machine under 10 years old. A control board or a tub leak on an older machine usually isn't.
