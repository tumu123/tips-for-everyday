Why Your Dryer Won't Heat (and the Vent Question)

Fix It Appliances Published October 24, 2026 11 min read

A dryer that tumbles but doesn’t heat is the most common dryer failure, and it divides cleanly by heat source. Electric dryers, gas dryers, and heat pump dryers have almost nothing in common mechanically, and checking one with the logic of another is how people end up replacing parts that were never broken.

There is also a second reason this deserves attention: a restricted dryer vent is a leading cause of house fires, not just slow drying. Lint is highly flammable, and it accumulates in exactly the place where the air is hottest. Any diagnosis of “not heating” should include the vent, even when the vent turns out not to be the cause.

Start with the vent. It costs nothing and it is the one part of the system where the risk is not just inconvenience.

Step 1: Confirm It Is Actually Not Heating

Before diagnosing, confirm the symptom precisely. Three complaints get confused:

ComplaintWhat’s happeningWhere to look
Tumbles, clothes stay cold and wetNo heat at allHeating element, igniter, thermal fuse
Warm at first, then coldHeat cuts out mid-cycleThermal fuse, high-limit thermostat, vent
Gets hot but clothes take two cyclesHeat works, airflow doesn’tVent, blower, lint filter
Heats, drum doesn’t turnDifferent problem — belt or motorBelt, idler, motor
Shuts off after a few minutesOverheat protection trippingVent restriction, thermostat

The third row is the one people misdiagnose. A dryer that heats but takes two hours is not failing to heat — it’s failing to move air. The heat is there; the moisture has nowhere to go. That is a vent problem 90% of the time, and it is also the fire-risk scenario.

The fifth row is a warning, not a nuisance. A dryer that shuts off early is protecting itself from overheating. Forcing it to run — by resetting, by taping over a sensor, by running it repeatedly — defeats a safety device on an appliance that is already the most fire-prone in the house.

Step 2: The Vent — Always First

Disconnect the dryer from the wall and pull it out. Then check four things in order.

The lint filter. Obvious, and still the most common cause. A filter with a film of fabric softener residue passes air poorly even when it looks clean. Hold it up to a light — if you can’t see through it clearly, wash it in warm soapy water and dry it fully.

The vent hose. Disconnect it at both ends. Look inside with a flashlight. Lint accumulates at the bends and at the transition from the dryer’s blower housing to the hose. A hose that is crushed behind the machine — a very common result of pushing the dryer back against the wall — is worse than a dirty one, because it restricts airflow permanently.

The transition duct. This is the short run between the dryer outlet and the wall. Rigid metal or semi-rigid metal is correct; flexible vinyl or foil accordion duct is not. Vinyl duct traps lint in its ridges, can sag and collect condensation, and is prohibited by most building codes for this reason. If yours is vinyl, replace it — it’s a $15 part and a genuine safety improvement.

The exterior vent hood. Go outside and look at where the vent terminates. The flap should open freely and close. Birds nest in vent hoods, lint builds up behind the flap, and a hood that is painted shut or blocked by landscaping stops airflow entirely. This is the check nobody does, and it produces exactly the symptom of a dryer that won’t heat.

How often: lint filter every load, vent hose and exterior hood annually, full duct cleaning every 2–3 years depending on volume.

If the vent run is long, has multiple elbows, or you cannot reach the far end, a vent cleaning kit with a flexible brush on a drill is the practical approach. If the run is inaccessible, an HVAC or dryer vent service will clean it for a modest fee — considerably less than a fire.

Step 3: Electric Dryers — Element, Fuse, Thermostats

An electric dryer’s heat comes from a resistance element, usually 4,000–5,600 watts at 240 volts. The circuit is protected by a thermal fuse and two thermostats: an operating thermostat that cycles the element to hold temperature, and a high-limit thermostat that cuts power if the machine overheats.

The thermal fuse is the most common failure and it fails for a reason. It is a one-shot device on the blower housing — it blows when the dryer overheats, which overwhelmingly means restricted airflow. Replacing the fuse without clearing the vent means the new fuse blows again.

Diagnostic order for an electric dryer:

  1. Check the breaker. A dryer uses a double-pole 240 V breaker. If one pole trips, the dryer tumbles but doesn’t heat — the motor runs on 120 V, the element gets nothing. This is the single most misdiagnosed dryer symptom, and it’s free to check.
  2. Test the thermal fuse with a multimeter on continuity, power off. No continuity = blown.
  3. Test the heating element for continuity. Infinite resistance = broken element. Also check for a short to the housing.
  4. Test both thermostats for continuity at room temperature. The high-limit should be closed when cold.
  5. Check the element’s terminals for scorching or a burned wire — a loose terminal on a 240 V element is a common failure and cheap to fix.
Electric symptomLikely partCost of part
Tumbles, no heat at allThermal fuse (after vent check)$10–$25
Tumbles, no heat, fuse goodHeating element$30–$80
Heats then cuts outHigh-limit thermostat$15–$30
Heats weaklyElement partially failed, or 240 V issue$30–$80
Breaker trips when heat startsElement shorted to ground$30–$80

Safety note: a dryer is one of the few appliances with 240 V and high current behind a panel you can open with a screwdriver. Unplug it — don’t just switch it off — before opening anything. Capacitors and 240 V terminals don’t care that the dial is off.

Step 4: Gas Dryers — Igniter, Coils, Sensor

A gas dryer’s heat comes from a burner lit by a hot-surface igniter, controlled by gas valve coils, and proven by a flame sensor. The failure sequence is different from electric, and the symptoms are more specific.

Listen and watch. Remove the front lower panel (on most models this exposes the burner) and start a cycle. You should see the igniter glow orange within 30–60 seconds, then hear a soft whoosh as the gas ignites.

What you seeWhat it means
Igniter never glowsFailed igniter, or no power to the burner circuit
Igniter glows, no ignition, then goes outGas valve coils failed, or no gas supply
Ignites, burns a few seconds, goes outFlame sensor not proving flame, or air in the gas line
Ignites once, never againCoils failing when hot — classic symptom
No glow and no clickThermal fuse or control

The gas valve coils are the classic gas-dryer failure. They are two small solenoids on top of the gas valve. When they heat up, the winding opens and the valve won’t open again — so the dryer lights once and then never relights in the same cycle. Replacing both coils is a $20–$40 part and a genuinely easy job.

The igniter is ceramic and fragile. Don’t touch the element itself — skin oils and mechanical stress crack it. If it doesn’t glow and tests open, replace it.

If you smell gas at any point during this, stop. Shut the gas valve off, ventilate, and call. A gas dryer’s burner is a real combustion appliance, and the checks that follow a smell of gas are not DIY checks.

Step 5: Heat Pump Dryers — The Different Beast

Heat pump dryers don’t have an element or a burner. They move heat with a refrigerant loop, like a dehumidifier. They’re far more efficient and they fail differently.

The most common cause of “not heating” is a clogged lint filter or condenser. These machines run at much lower temperatures, so airflow restriction shows up as a dryer that runs forever rather than one that overheats. Clean the filter every load, and clean the condenser fins per the manual — often monthly.

A heat pump dryer that runs but produces no heat and no error code is usually a refrigerant or compressor fault, and that is a service call, not a DIY repair. Refrigerant handling requires certification.

Low ambient temperature matters too. Heat pump dryers work poorly in a cold garage or unheated basement — below about 45 °F, performance drops sharply. An electric dryer in the same location is unaffected, which is why a heat pump dryer that “worked all summer” can seem broken in January.

Step 6: The Moisture Sensor — Why Your Dryer “Stops Too Soon”

Not a heating failure, but it gets diagnosed as one constantly. Modern dryers use two metal strips inside the drum to sense moisture. When lint coats those strips, the machine reads “dry” while clothes are still damp, and the cycle ends early. Users conclude the heat has failed.

The tell: the timed-dry cycle works fine, only the sensor cycles end early.

The fix: clean the sensor bars with a cloth and rubbing alcohol. Also clean the lint filter and check that fabric softener sheets aren’t leaving a film — dryer sheets coat the sensors, and this is the main argument against using them every load.

While you’re at it: heat sets stains. Anything with a food, grease, or protein stain should be checked before it goes in the dryer, because a stain that survives the wash will be permanent after a hot cycle. Removing laundry stains is worth reading before drying anything questionable.

Step 7: When It Is the Vent, Not the Dryer

If the vent was restricted and the thermal fuse blew, both need attention. But the vent is the part that caused it.

Signs your vent is the real problem:

  • The dryer is hot to the touch on top or at the door
  • The laundry room gets humid during a cycle
  • Clothes are hot but still damp at the end
  • Drying takes more than about 60 minutes for a normal load
  • The exterior flap barely moves when the dryer runs

How to test airflow properly: disconnect the vent from the back of the dryer, run the dryer on air-only for five minutes, and feel the output. Then reconnect and feel the exterior hood. A large difference between the two means the duct is restricted.

Why this is worth the effort: dryer fires are almost always caused by lint accumulating near the heat source and igniting, and a restricted vent is what causes lint to accumulate there. Cleaning the vent once a year and cleaning the exterior hood is the entire prevention. It is also the one item on the fall maintenance checklist that most homeowners skip.

Why This Works

Heat is only half of drying. The other half is carrying moisture away. A dryer works by heating air, passing it through tumbling fabric, and exhausting the moisture-laden air outside. Restrict the exhaust and the air becomes saturated, at which point no further evaporation happens regardless of how hot the element gets. That’s why “won’t heat” and “takes two hours” are often the same fault.

The thermal fuse is a deliberate one-shot device, and its failure is information. It exists because a dryer that overheats is a fire hazard. It’s placed on the blower housing — downstream of the drum — so it senses exhaust temperature. When it blows, it is reporting that exhaust temperature exceeded a safe limit. Replacing it without fixing the cause is replacing the smoke alarm because it went off.

A double-pole breaker explains the most confusing electric symptom. The drum motor runs on 120 V from one pole; the heating element needs 240 V from both. One tripped pole leaves the motor running and the element dead. The dryer looks “on,” behaves normally except for heat, and the breaker looks fine at a glance because it isn’t fully off.

Igniter-then-coils is a thermal failure sequence. Gas valve coils fail when hot, not when cold. Cold coils test fine; hot coils open. That is why a gas dryer lights on the first attempt and then won’t relight — and why testing the coils cold can give a false pass.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my dryer tumble but produce no heat?

On electric: check the breaker first (one pole tripped), then the thermal fuse, then the element. On gas: watch the igniter — no glow means a failed igniter, glow with no ignition means the valve coils.

Can a clogged vent stop the dryer from heating?

Yes. It causes overheating, which blows the thermal fuse. The dryer then tumbles cold. Clear the vent before replacing anything.

How often should a dryer vent be cleaned?

The full duct every 1–2 years for a typical household, annually if you dry heavy loads or have pets. The lint filter every single load.

Is it safe to use a dryer whose vent I can't reach?

Not ideal. An inaccessible duct accumulates lint unseen. Have it professionally cleaned, or reroute it to rigid metal with fewer elbows.

How long should a dryer take?

A normal load should dry in 45–60 minutes. More than 75 minutes consistently means restricted airflow, not a weak heating element.

My gas dryer ignites and then the flame goes out. What is it?

Almost always the gas valve coils. They fail when hot, so the dryer lights once and then won't relight. Both coils should be replaced together.

Why does my dryer stop before clothes are dry?

The moisture sensors are coated with lint or fabric softener residue. Clean them with rubbing alcohol, and use fewer dryer sheets.

Is a dryer repair worth it?

Dryers are among the most repairable appliances — the parts are cheap and accessible. Under 10 years old, repair almost always wins. Past 12, weigh it against a new machine's efficiency.
