Why Your Washer Won't Spin (or Walks Across the Floor)

Fix It Appliances Published October 24, 2026 10 min read

A washer that won’t spin is usually not broken. Modern machines are built to refuse the spin cycle when conditions are unsafe or when the load cannot be balanced — and that refusal looks exactly like a fault. If the machine fills, washes, drains, and then stops without spinning, it has almost certainly made a decision rather than failed.

That distinction saves money. A no-spin complaint is one of the most common service calls, and a large share of them end with “the load was tangled” or “the machine wasn’t level” — both free to fix.

The genuine failures are rarer and follow a pattern: suspension components wear out on a schedule, and the age of the machine tells you which one is likely gone.

Step 1: Separate the Three Symptoms

“Won’t spin” describes three different problems. Which one you have determines everything.

SymptomWhat’s actually happeningUsual cause
Drum doesn’t turn at all, water still insideCycle never reaches spinDrain not complete, lid switch, or pump
Water drains, drum doesn’t turnSpin phase blockedLoad imbalance, belt, motor coupling, control
Drum turns slowly, clothes still wetSpinning weaklyWorn belt, failing clutch, overloaded drum
Drum spins fine, machine walksBalance or levelling problemLoad distribution, feet, suspension
Loud banging in spinSomething is hitting the drumSuspension rods, shock absorbers, foreign object

The first row is not a spin problem at all. A washer will not spin with water in the drum — it’s a mechanical interlock on most designs, because spinning a full drum would destroy the bearings. So “won’t spin” often means “won’t drain.” Check that first: if there’s standing water, go solve drainage and the spin problem usually disappears with it. Diagnosing a washer that won’t drain covers that chain.

Step 2: The Load — Responsible for Most No-Spin Calls

Front-loaders are far more sensitive to load balance than top-loaders, because the drum spins on a horizontal axis and an unbalanced load produces a violent oscillation rather than a gentle wobble. The machine’s control board measures that oscillation and aborts the spin if it exceeds a threshold. It will then try to redistribute — tumble, pause, retry — and if three attempts fail, it stops with an error code.

The three load patterns that reliably defeat a front-loader:

  1. One heavy item. A single bath mat, a duvet cover, or a pair of heavy jeans. There is nothing to counterbalance it, so it plasters itself to one side of the drum and the machine cannot distribute it.
  2. A tangled mass. Sheets and long-sleeved items wrap into a rope. The rope is heavy and sits in one place.
  3. Too full. Clothes have no room to tumble and redistribute. The load compresses into a single lump.

The test: take out half the load. Run a drain-and-spin cycle. If it spins, the machine is fine and the original load was the problem.

The fix for the heavy single item: add two or three towels to the load. Counterweight is the entire mechanism — the towels distribute to the opposite side and the drum balances.

The fix for sheets: wash them separately, and close the duvet cover or fold the sheet into the drum rather than letting it ball up. Some machines have a “sheets” or “bulky” cycle that uses a different tumbling rhythm specifically for this.

The fix for overloading: fill the drum no more than three-quarters full for a front-loader, loosely. A hand’s width of space at the top is the practical guide.

Step 3: Levelling and the Feet

A machine that walks across the floor is transferring vibration into the floor, which means it is either unbalanced or not sitting flat.

Check level front-to-back and side-to-side with a spirit level on top of the machine. Both axes matter; a machine level side-to-side but tilted forward will still walk.

Check all four feet are touching the floor. This is the actual test, more reliable than the level. Push down on each corner in turn. If the machine rocks, one foot is off the floor. Adjust the levelling feet — they screw in and out — and lock them with the locknut if the machine has one.

Check what’s under the machine. A washer on a suspended wood floor transmits vibration into the joists and can walk no matter how level it is. A rigid anti-vibration mat or a sheet of ¾-inch plywood spanning two joists distributes the load. Rubber pads under the feet reduce walking on tile and concrete.

Check the shipping bolts if the machine is new or recently moved. Front-loaders ship with transit bolts locking the drum to the cabinet. Leave them in and the machine will shake violently and can damage itself. They must be removed — four bolts at the back, usually with spacers.

Step 4: The Belt (Top-Loaders and Older Front-Loaders)

If the drum doesn’t turn at all but the motor runs — you can hear it — the belt between the motor and the drum has likely slipped off or snapped.

Check: unplug, remove the back or front panel depending on the model, and look at the belt. It should be taut around both pulleys. A belt sitting loose at the bottom of the cabinet has come off; a belt with frayed edges or a glazed, shiny inner surface is worn and slipping.

Replace rather than reinstall a worn belt. Belts are cheap — often under $20 — and a glazed belt will slip again within weeks. Note the routing before removing the old one; some machines use a spring-loaded idler that the belt must pass around.

Direct-drive machines have no belt. If your washer has no belt, the motor is coupled directly to the drum, and a no-spin condition points to the motor coupling, the control board, or the brake/clutch assembly.

Step 5: The Lid Switch and the Door Lock

Both machine types gate the spin cycle on a safety interlock. The spin is the dangerous part of the cycle, so the machine refuses to run it unless it knows the opening is closed and locked.

Top-loader lid switch: a small switch under the top panel, actuated by a plastic plunger when the lid closes. The plunger snaps off, or the switch contacts fail. Symptom: the machine washes and drains but never spins, and if you press down on the lid it sometimes spins. That’s a failed switch.

Front-loader door lock: an electrically actuated lock that must engage before the cycle starts and stay engaged through the spin. A failed lock produces a machine that fills and washes but will not spin, often with a door-lock error code. Unlike the lid switch, the door lock is not bypassable and should not be — replace it.

Both parts are inexpensive and moderately easy to replace, though the door lock on a front-loader varies a lot by brand.

Step 6: Suspension — When the Machine Is Old Enough to Have Worn Out

Top-loaders hang the drum from four suspension rods with springs. Front-loaders mount the drum on two or four shock absorbers, plus springs at the top. Both systems wear out.

Suspension rods (top-load): when the springs weaken or the rod dampers wear, the drum sags and bounces. Symptom: violent shaking in spin, worse with a heavy load, and the drum visibly sitting low or off-centre. Test by pressing down firmly on each corner of the drum — a healthy machine resists and returns; a worn one bounces freely and the drum moves more on one side.

Shock absorbers (front-load): worn shocks let the drum oscillate without damping. Symptom: loud banging in the spin cycle even with a balanced load, and the machine walks despite being level. Test with the machine off: push the drum down and release. It should return smoothly and settle. If it bounces two or three times, the shocks are gone.

Bearings: a rumbling or grinding noise that increases with spin speed, plus play in the drum when you push the top of it. Bearing replacement means splitting the drum — on most machines this is a several-hour job and often not worth it on a machine past ten years.

Under 3 yearsWarranty — call the manufacturer
3–7 yearsRods (top-load) or shocks (front-load)

Step 7: The Drain Pump Filter, Again

It’s worth repeating here because it causes more no-spin complaints than any mechanical part: a machine that cannot drain cannot spin. The filter clogs, the water stays, the cycle never advances.

If you haven’t checked it, check it before anything in Steps 4 through 6. It takes ten minutes and costs nothing. Cleaning a washing machine covers the filter, the gasket, and the drum, which is the same maintenance visit in a different order.

A machine that trips the breaker when the spin starts is a separate fault — the motor drawing locked-rotor current, or a short in the wiring. Resetting a tripped breaker explains why a breaker that trips repeatedly should never be reset repeatedly.

Step 8: The Control Board — Last, Not First

A control board failure can prevent the spin: the machine completes wash, drains, and then either does nothing or repeats the drain phase indefinitely. Symptoms that point to the board rather than a component:

  • Multiple unrelated functions fail at once
  • The machine behaves differently cycle to cycle
  • The display shows nonsense or won’t respond
  • Relays click but nothing happens

Boards are expensive — often $150–$300 for the part alone — and on a machine over eight years old, they usually justify replacement over repair. Before condemning one, check the simpler things: a failed lid switch or door lock reads to the board as “unsafe to spin,” and it will behave exactly as if the board had failed.

Why This Works

Spin is gated on three independent conditions, and any one of them stops it. The machine needs: water out (drain complete), opening secured (interlock closed), and load balanced (vibration under threshold). Three gates, three different failure modes, and the same visible symptom. That is why the diagnosis has to be sequential rather than a guess.

Balance is measured, not assumed. Front-loaders calculate load distribution from the motor’s current draw and from accelerometers. An unbalanced load produces a measurable oscillation, and the control board aborts before the spin reaches full speed because a full-speed unbalanced spin can destroy the drum bearings and walk the machine into a wall.

Vibration travels through rigid contact, so the feet are the entire interface. A machine transfers energy to the floor only through its feet. Four feet in solid contact with a rigid floor means the energy has nowhere to go but into the drum’s suspension, where it is damped. One foot off the floor means the machine pivots on the opposite corner — and pivoting is walking.

Suspension parts have a design life measured in cycles, not years. Rod springs and shock absorbers are consumables. A household running five loads a week will wear them out well before a household running two, which is why the same model can fail at five years in one home and twelve in another.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my washer spin sometimes and not others?

Load-dependent. Small balanced loads spin, large or tangled ones don't. That pattern almost always means the machine is fine and the suspension is marginal or the loads are too big.

My washer fills and drains but won't spin. Where do I start?

The lid switch or door lock. That's the most common cause of "everything works except spin," and it's a cheap part.

Why does my machine walk across the floor only with big loads?

Vibration amplitude scales with load imbalance. Small loads don't generate enough force to overcome friction. Check the feet and the floor first, then the shock absorbers.

Do anti-vibration pads actually work?

For walking on hard floors, yes — they add friction and absorb some high-frequency vibration. They do nothing for an unbalanced load or worn suspension. Fix the cause before adding accessories.

How do I know if my drum bearings are gone?

Rumbling or grinding that gets louder with spin speed, plus vertical play in the drum when you lift the top edge. Bearing replacement is a drum-splitting job and rarely worth it past eight years.

I removed the shipping bolts — is the noise normal now?

Some noise is normal for a front-loader. Banging that shakes the floor is not. If it persists after removing the bolts and levelling, check the shocks.

Can an overloaded washer be damaged permanently?

Yes. Repeated overloading accelerates bearing and suspension wear, and the extra weight stresses the drum spider. It's the most common cause of early failure in machines that are otherwise well maintained.

Should I repair or replace?

Under five years, repair. Five to eight, repair if the part is under half the price of a new machine. Past ten, replace — even a successful repair leaves you with a machine that will need another one soon.
