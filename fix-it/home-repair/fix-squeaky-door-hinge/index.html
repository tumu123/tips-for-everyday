How to Fix a Squeaky Door Hinge (Three Ways)

Fix It Home Repair Published October 14, 2026 6 min read

A squeaky hinge is metal-on-metal friction, and it is one of the few home repairs with a genuinely five-minute solution. The complication is that most people reach for the wrong lubricant and make the problem worse.

Start with the diagnosis, because there are three different causes:

  1. Dry hinge — the pin and knuckle have lost their lubricant. This is the common case, and lubrication fixes it.
  2. Bent or worn pin — the pin is deformed or the hinge plate is loose, so the metal binds mechanically. Lubrication quiets it briefly and then it returns.
  3. Loose hinge screws — the hinge plate moves in the jamb, producing a creak or groan rather than a squeak. Tightening fixes this, not lubricant.

Hinge action sounds different depending on the cause. A dry hinge gives a high-pitched squeak on a consistent motion. A loose plate gives a lower creak at a specific point in the swing. A binding pin gives a scrape.

Method 1: Lubricate the hinge (start here)

This is the fix for the common case, and it takes about five minutes.

  1. Prop the door open with a wedge or blocks so the hinge is under load and the pin is accessible. This matters — a door that swings free moves while you work.
  2. Clean the hinge first. Wipe the hinge area with a rag to remove dust, hair, and old lubricant. Lubricating over grit makes an abrasive paste.
  3. Apply the lubricant to the top of the pin and let it work down. Gravity is your friend here, so apply at the top of the knuckle.
  4. Open and close the door ten to fifteen times to distribute the lubricant through the knuckle.
  5. Wipe off the excess with a paper towel. Leftover lubricant attracts dust, which eventually brings the squeak back.

For stubborn hinges: remove the pin, coat it fully, and reinsert. See Method 2.

The reason WD-40 disappoints here: it is a water-displacer and light solvent, not a long-term lubricant. It quiets the hinge immediately and then evaporates, leaving the hinge drier than before. If you use it, follow up with grease.

Method 2: Remove the pin and grease it properly

When lubrication from the top does not hold, take the pin out. This is the thorough version and it lasts far longer.

  1. Prop the door open and have a helper support it, or wedge it so it cannot fall.
  2. Tap the pin up from the bottom using a nail set or a thin drift punch and a hammer. Work some designs by lifting under the pin head with a flathead screwdriver instead.
  3. Most pins lift out from the top once the head clears — but some hinges have a non-removable pin, and some have a pin that only comes out one way. Look at the pin head: a rounded dome usually means not removable.
  4. Clean the pin with a rag. Remove old hardened grease and any rust with fine steel wool.
  5. Coat the pin fully with grease — white lithium or petroleum jelly.
  6. Reinsert the pin and tap it home with the hammer. It should seat flush.
  7. Work the door open and shut several times, then wipe the excess.

Warning: a door that is not supported while the pin is out can fall or swing, and a hinged door is heavy. Either wedge it firmly or get help. This is the one step where people get hurt.

Method 3: Fix the real problem (loose screws or a bent pin)

If the squeak returns within days, lubrication is not the issue.

Loose hinge screws

  1. Tighten all screws on all three hinges. Test the door. This alone resolves a surprising share of creaks.
  2. If a screw spins freely, the wood has stripped. Do not just use a bigger screw. The durable fix:
    • Dip a wooden matchstick or toothpick in wood glue and tap it into the hole.
    • Snap it off flush with the jamb surface.
    • Reinstall the screw. It now has fresh wood to bite into.
    • Alternative: a golf tee or a wooden dowel plug, following the same method.
  3. For a severely stripped hole, drill it out, glue in a dowel plug, let it cure, and re-drill a pilot hole.

A bent or worn pin

A pin that has been bent by a slammed door will never run smoothly in the knuckle. Replace it.

  • Hinge pins are sold individually and cost very little. Take the old pin to the store to match the diameter.
  • If the pin is not removable, or the knuckle itself is deformed, replace the whole hinge.

Replacing a hinge

  1. Support the door or remove it and lay it on its edge.
  2. Unscrew the old hinge from both the door and the jamb. Note the orientation.
  3. Match the hinge — same size, same corner radius as the existing mortise.
  4. Reuse the existing mortise if the new hinge matches, which avoids chiseling. Most standard hinges match.
  5. Install with the longest screws that fit to maximize grip in the jamb.

Doors that sag and drawers that rub are usually the same problem — tightening loose cabinet hinges and drawer fronts covers the screw, anchor, and adjustment fixes.

Squeaks are not the only door complaint, and the fix differs.

The latch deserves a mention: a latch bolt sliding against a strike plate squeaks in a way that sounds exactly like a hinge. Grease the latch bolt and the strike plate opening with a little petroleum jelly. If the door still squeaks with the door held stationary, the source is the latch, not the hinge.

Step 5: Maintain it

Once fixed, a hinge stays quiet for a long time with minimal attention.

  • Reapply grease about once a year on heavily used doors, especially exterior doors.
  • Tighten the hinge screws during your annual walkthrough. Loose screws are the cause of most recurring noise.
  • Do not slam doors. Impact bends pins and loosens screws, and it is the reason a fixed hinge squeaks again.

Why This Works

Hinge noise is caused by friction, and friction has two sources: the absence of a lubricant film, or mechanical interference between metal surfaces.

Lubrication works by inserting a film between the pin and the knuckle so the metals never touch. It only works if the surface is clean — lubricant applied over grit simply suspends the grit and creates an abrasive slurry that accelerates wear. That is why cleaning before lubricating matters as much as the lubricant itself.

Loose screws change the geometry. The hinge plate lifts and shifts slightly under load, and the resulting movement produces a lower-frequency creak rather than the high-frequency squeak of dry metal. No lubricant can fix it, which is why so many people grease a hinge repeatedly with no lasting result.

Pin replacement addresses the case where the metal is physically deformed. Momentary improvement from grease is the tell — it quiets the noise for hours or days and then returns, because the underlying interference remains.

Lubricant choice follows the same logic. A long-lasting film is required, so grease beats oil for hinges under load. Solvent-based products such as WD-40 sit in the temporary category by design: they displace moisture and free stuck parts, but they are not formulated to remain as a load-bearing film.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best lubricant for door hinges?

White lithium grease is the best general choice because it stays put and handles load. Silicone spray and petroleum jelly are good alternatives. Avoid WD-40 as a long-term solution and avoid cooking spray entirely.

Why does WD-40 not fix a squeaky hinge permanently?

WD-40 is a water displacer and light solvent, not a heavy lubricant. It works immediately because it is thin enough to penetrate, then it evaporates, leaving the hinge drier than before. Follow it with grease if you use it at all.

Can I use olive oil or cooking oil on a hinge?

Not recommended. Cooking oils are sticky and oxidize over time, collecting dust and eventually gumming up the hinge. The result is usually a worse noise a few months later.

My hinge squeaks even after greasing — why?

Either the hinge screws are loose and the noise is coming from plate movement, or the pin is bent or worn. Tighten every screw on every hinge first, and if the noise persists, replace the pin.

How do I fix a hinge screw hole that is stripped?

Dip a wooden matchstick or toothpick in wood glue, tap it into the hole, snap it off flush, and reinstall the screw. For badly damaged holes, drill out, glue in a dowel plug, and re-drill a pilot hole.

Is it safe to remove a hinge pin myself?

Yes, if the door is supported. A hinged door is heavy and will fall or swing when a pin comes out. Wedge it firmly or have someone hold it.

How do I stop a door from creaking rather than squeaking?

A creak usually means loose hinge screws rather than dry metal. Tighten every screw on all hinges, and plug any stripped holes. Lubrication alone will not fix a creak.

Should I grease the latch too?

Yes. A latch bolt rubbing on the strike plate produces a noise that sounds identical to a hinge squeak. A small amount of petroleum jelly on the latch bolt and the strike plate opening quiets it.
