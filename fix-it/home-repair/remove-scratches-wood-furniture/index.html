How to Remove Scratches From Wood Furniture

Fix It Home Repair Published October 15, 2026 10 min read
Advertisement

The single most important distinction in furniture scratch repair is whether the damage is in the finish or in the wood. They look similar and need completely different treatment — and confusing them is how people turn a five-minute fix into a refinishing job.

Do the fingernail test first. Drag a fingernail gently across the scratch. If it passes over smoothly, the damage is in the finish and can usually be made nearly invisible. If your nail catches, the wood itself is cut and you’re filling, not polishing.

Step 0: Identify What You’re Working With

Depth:

Surface type — and this one matters a lot:

SurfaceSafe to sand or steel-wool?
Solid woodYes, carefully
Wood veneerNo. Veneer is a thin layer; sanding goes straight through it
Laminate / melamineNo repairs beyond colouring; sanding destroys it
Painted woodTreat as paint, not as wood

How to tell veneer from solid: look at an edge or the underside. On veneer, you can see a thin surface layer over a different substrate, and the grain pattern often repeats across panels. On solid wood, the grain continues through the thickness at edges. Our guide to checking used furniture covers this in more detail.

If it’s veneer, skip anything abrasive. Repairs are limited to colouring and filling — which is still enough for most scratches.

Step 1: Clean Before Repairing

This is skipped constantly, and it’s why repairs look muddy.

Wipe the area with mineral spirits on a soft cloth. This removes wax, polish, and oils that would otherwise stop a repair product from bonding or blending. Let it dry fully.

You may find that some “scratches” are actually residue on the surface and disappear at this step.

Step 2: Light Surface Scratches

These are white or pale lines sitting in the clear finish. The wood beneath is fine; you’re dealing with a mark in the topcoat.

Method A — Furniture repair crayon or marker (most reliable). Choose a shade slightly lighter than the furniture; you can always darken, but you can’t lighten. Fill along the scratch, wipe away the excess immediately with a soft cloth, and buff gently.

Method B — Walnut (works, with caveats). Rub the meat of a walnut half along the scratch. The oils and fine abrasion darken the mark so it blends with the surrounding wood.

The honest caveat: this works best on unfinished or oil-finished wood, where oil can actually penetrate. On a modern polyurethane or lacquer finish, it does little, because the oil can’t get through the topcoat. It’s worth trying because it costs nothing, but don’t expect it to fix a scratch on a sealed surface.

Method C — Paste wax. For very fine marks, a good paste wax fills micro-scratches and evens the sheen. Apply, let it haze, buff. This improves the overall appearance even where it doesn’t remove a specific scratch.

Finish by buffing the whole panel, not just the scratch — a locally treated spot often ends up with a different sheen than the rest of the surface, which makes it more visible than the original mark.

Step 3: Dents and Compressions (Not Cuts)

A dent isn’t missing material — the wood fibres are compressed. That means it can often be raised back, which is far better than filling.

The steam method:

  1. Lay a clean damp cotton cloth over the dent (damp, not soaking)
  2. Press a warm iron onto the cloth for a few seconds at a time
  3. Lift and check repeatedly — do not leave the iron in contact
  4. The fibres swell with the moisture and heat and the dent rises
  5. Repeat until the surface is level, then let it dry
  6. Apply paste wax to protect the now-exposed or dried area

This genuinely works on solid wood and it’s the most satisfying fix in furniture repair, because there’s no filler and no colour matching. It works less well on veneer, and not at all on laminate.

Use a hair dryer instead of an iron if you’re worried about heat damage. Slower, but safer.

Critical caution: keep the iron moving, use a low-to-medium setting with no steam from the iron itself, and check constantly. Excessive heat blisters finish and can discolor wood. The goal is warm and moist, not hot.

Step 4: Deep Scratches Into the Wood

When the nail catches, you’re filling.

For narrow deep scratches:

  1. Clean the scratch with mineral spirits and a swab
  2. Fill with a repair crayon or colour-matched wood filler, pressing it in rather than smearing it over
  3. Level it with a plastic card or a soft cloth while still workable
  4. Let it cure per the product instructions
  5. Buff the area
  6. Protect with paste wax or a matching topcoat

For wider gouges: use wood filler rather than a crayon, since you need body, not just colour. Overfill very slightly, let it cure, then level carefully. On solid wood you can use 0000 steel wool; on veneer, use only a cloth and very light pressure.

Colour matching tip: test the product on an inconspicuous area first — underside of the table, inside a leg, the back panel. Daylight and indoor light show colours differently, so check in the room where the furniture lives.

Then seal it. A filled scratch absorbs finish differently from surrounding wood. A light coat of wax, or a matching topcoat on solid wood, evens that out.

Step 5: White Rings and Water Marks

These are moisture trapped in the finish, and they’re a different problem from scratches — though they often get treated the same way.

What they are: a white or cloudy ring means moisture has penetrated the topcoat. A dark ring means it has reached the wood and the stain.

For white rings (moisture in the finish):

  • Gentle heat — a hair dryer on low, moved constantly, can drive the moisture out
  • Paste wax or petroleum jelly left on overnight, then buffed — this can draw the moisture out
  • A paste of baking soda and water or non-gel toothpaste, rubbed very gently, then wiped — mildly abrasive, so use light pressure

For dark rings (moisture in the wood): these need the finish to be removed in that area and the wood treated, or the piece refinished. That’s often a professional job.

The prevention is trivially simple and worth more than the cure: use coasters, and wipe up spills quickly. Water damage to a wood tabletop is one of the most common and most avoidable furniture problems.

Why This Works

Finish damage and wood damage are physically different. A scratch confined to the topcoat is a surface-texture and light-reflection problem, so anything that evens the surface or fills the micro-groove restores the appearance. A scratch into the wood has removed stained material, so colour has to be added back — no amount of polishing will restore it.

Compressed wood fibres can be re-expanded; removed wood cannot. Steam works on dents because it’s swelling existing material back into shape. Filling works on cuts because material is genuinely gone. Distinguishing the two determines whether you’re restoring or substituting.

Veneer has no depth to sand into. The decorative layer is a fraction of a millimetre thick, so any abrasive treatment removes the pattern permanently. This is why surface type constrains every subsequent choice — and why identifying it first matters more than any technique.

Cleaning before repairing is what makes products bond. Polishes and silicones leave a residue that prevents fillers and colourants from adhering or blending, which is why a repair applied over furniture polish often looks blotchy and wears off quickly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a scratch is deep? Drag a fingernail across it. If it catches, the wood is cut and needs filling. If it passes smoothly, it’s in the finish.

Does the walnut trick really work? On unfinished or oil-finished wood, yes. On modern polyurethane or lacquer finishes, it does little because the oil can’t penetrate the topcoat.

Can I fix a dent without filler? Often yes, with steam. Damp cloth plus gentle heat swells the compressed fibres back up. It works best on solid wood.

How do I remove white water rings? Try gentle heat from a hair dryer, or leave paste wax or petroleum jelly on overnight. These work when moisture is in the finish.

Is it safe to sand a scratched table? Only if it’s solid wood. Sanding veneer destroys the decorative layer permanently.

What colour repair product should I buy? Slightly lighter than the surrounding wood. You can darken in layers, but you can’t lighten.

When should I call a professional? For antiques, large-area damage, lifting veneer, structural problems, or dark stains that have reached the wood.

How do I prevent scratches? Felt pads under anything that moves, coasters under anything wet, and lifting rather than dragging objects across the surface.
