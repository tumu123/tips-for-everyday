How to Fix Scratches in Hardwood Floors

Fix It Home Repair Published October 15, 2026 10 min read

Floor scratches are harder to hide than furniture scratches, for one specific reason: a floor is a large continuous surface viewed in flat light, so any local repair that changes sheen or colour shows up as a patch. A repair that would pass on a table leg is obvious on a hallway floor.

That constraint shapes everything. The goal is usually to make a scratch less noticeable, not invisible — and knowing which repairs stay invisible is what separates a good result from an obvious patch.

Start by identifying your finish type. It determines what’s possible.

Step 0: What Finish Do You Have?

This is the question that decides the whole approach, and it’s answerable in a minute.

Finish typeHow to recognize itWhat it allows
Polyurethane (surface film)Visible sheen (satin, semi-gloss, or gloss). Water beads on the surface and stays there for several minutesLocal colour repair; local sanding leaves a visible patch; whole-room recoat is the real option for widespread wear
Penetrating oil / hardwax oilMatte or very low sheen. Wood grain is tactile. A drop of water slowly darkens the woodSpot repair works well — you can re-oil a small area and blend it
Wax (older floors)Soft, low sheen; marks easily; can be buffed to a glowSpot repair and buffing; needs regular re-waxing
Aluminum oxide (factory prefinished)Very hard, uniform factory finish on prefinished boardsLimited spot repair; boards are usually replaced rather than refinished

The water test: place a few drops of water on an inconspicuous spot. If they bead and sit for several minutes, you have a surface film. If the wood darkens as it soaks in, you have a penetrating finish.

Why this matters so much: on a polyurethane floor, sanding one spot and applying finish produces a shiny patch that’s more visible than the scratch — because the new finish sits on top of the old finish rather than merging with it. On an oil floor, the new oil soaks into the same wood and blends naturally.

Step 1: Identify the Scratch Depth

Same test as furniture, same logic:

TestDepthApproach
Fingernail passes over smoothlyIn the finish only — a scuff or hazeClean and polish; colour if needed
Fingernail catchesInto the woodFill and colour
Pale raw wood visibleThrough finish and stainFill, colour, seal
Dark lineStain or moisture in exposed woodMore involved; may need board-level work

Also distinguish scratches from wear. High-traffic paths develop a dull, uniformly abraded area rather than discrete scratches. That’s finish wear, and it’s addressed at the whole-area level, not spot by spot.

Step 2: Light Surface Scratches and Scuffs

These are the most common and the most fixable.

  1. Vacuum or sweep the area thoroughly — grit makes everything worse
  2. Clean with a floor cleaner appropriate to your finish. Do not use general-purpose cleaners on wood; many leave residue or dull the finish
  3. Assess after cleaning. A surprising number of “scratches” are scuff marks from shoe soles and disappear with proper cleaning
  4. For remaining light marks, use a colour-matched hardwood floor repair pen or crayon. Apply along the scratch, wipe the excess immediately
  5. Buff the surrounding area — not just the scratch — so the sheen stays uniform

For wax floors, buffing alone often resolves light scratches, since you’re redistributing the wax rather than adding anything.

Step 3: Deeper Scratches Into the Wood

  1. Clean the scratch thoroughly and let it dry
  2. Fill with colour-matched wood filler or a dedicated floor repair kit. Press it in rather than smearing it across
  3. Level it with a plastic scraper or putty knife held nearly flat
  4. Wipe away excess immediately, along the grain direction
  5. Let it cure per the product instructions
  6. Apply the sealer included in most floor repair kits — this is what makes the repair durable enough to walk on
  7. Buff the wider area

Be realistic about the result. A filled deep scratch on a polyurethane floor will be visible on close inspection in flat light. The goal is a repair you notice only when looking for it — which is a substantial improvement over a pale raw-wood line.

Colour matching: test on an area under a rug or inside a closet. Floor colour varies across a room, and the same product can look right in one spot and wrong three feet away.

Step 4: Widespread Wear — The Middle Option Most People Don’t Know About

If your floor has many scratches rather than a few, spot repair is the wrong approach. But full refinishing is expensive and often unnecessary.

There’s a middle option: screen and recoat.

OptionWhat it involvesWhen it’s right
Spot repairFill and colour individual scratchesA handful of discrete scratches
Screen and recoatLightly abrade the existing finish with a screening pad, then apply one new coat of finish over the whole floorWear confined to the finish, wood itself is undamaged
Full refinishSand down to bare wood, then stain and apply multiple coatsDeep damage into the wood, or discolouration

Screen and recoat is dramatically cheaper and faster than full refinishing, because it doesn’t remove wood — it just refreshes the topcoat. It’s the right answer for a floor that’s dull and scuffed but not gouged. Most households should consider this before refinishing.

When it’s not enough: if scratches have gone through the finish into the wood, recoating seals the damage in without removing it. That’s when you need a full refinish.

Step 5: Prevention (Worth More Than Any Repair)

Floor damage is far easier to prevent than to fix, and the causes are a short list.

CausePrevention
Furniture being draggedFelt pads under every leg, replaced when worn or when grit gets embedded
Grit tracked in from outsideDoormats inside and outside every entrance; a no-shoes policy if your household will accept it
Pet clawsRegular nail trimming; rugs in the paths pets run
Rolling office chairsA chair mat, or a rug
High heels with damaged tipsRepair the tips; they concentrate enormous force
Area rugs shiftingNon-slip underlay, and clean underneath periodically
Furniture legs with no protectionCheck regularly — pads wear through and fall off

The highest-leverage habit is keeping grit off the floor. Abrasive particles underfoot are what turn a floor from satin to dull over a few years. Mats at every entrance and regular sweeping do more for a floor’s appearance than any product.

Replace felt pads when they get dirty, not just when they fall off. A grit-embedded pad becomes sandpaper.

When to Call a Professional

  • Deep gouges across many boards
  • Water damage — cupping, buckling, or black staining
  • Boards that are split or splintered
  • A prefinished floor needing board replacement — this requires matching and careful installation
  • Any whole-floor refinishing job — the equipment and technique matter, and mistakes are very expensive to correct
  • You’re unsure of the finish type and considering a whole-floor treatment — a professional can identify it quickly

Why This Works

Repair options are constrained by finish chemistry, not by the scratch. A surface film finish sits as a distinct layer, so a new patch of it sits as a visible patch. A penetrating finish becomes part of the wood, so a new application blends invisibly into the surrounding area. The same scratch is easy on one floor and nearly impossible on the other.

Depth determines whether you’re colouring or rebuilding. Scuffs in the clear coat are a light-reflection problem. Cuts into stained wood have removed material, so colour and body must be added back. Confusing the two leads to either futile polishing or unnecessary filling.

Distributed wear is a coating problem, not a spot problem. When abrasion covers a whole traffic path, no number of individual repairs will restore uniformity. Recoating addresses the actual mechanism — the finish layer has thinned — at a fraction of refinishing cost.

Prevention targets the abrasive, not the floor. Most floor wear is caused by mineral grit and hard objects dragged under pressure. Removing the abrasive from the system prevents nearly all of it, which is why entrance mats and furniture pads outperform every repair product available.

MistakeWhat happensDo this instead

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sand out a floor scratch?

On a polyurethane floor, no — a local patch of new finish will look different from the surrounding area. On an oil-finished floor, light sanding and re-oiling a small area blends well.

How do I know what finish my floor has?

Put a few drops of water on an inconspicuous spot. If they bead and sit on the surface, it's a film finish. If the wood darkens, it's penetrating.

What's the difference between screen and recoat and refinishing?

Screen and recoat abrades the existing finish lightly and adds one new coat — no wood removed, much cheaper. Refinishing sands down to bare wood.

When do I actually need a full refinish?

When damage has gone through the finish into the wood, or when there's discolouration or water damage.

Does a floor repair kit work?

For individual scratches on a film-finish floor, yes — it reduces visibility substantially. It won't make a deep scratch invisible in flat light.

How do I prevent scratches most effectively?

Keep grit off the floor with entrance mats, and keep felt pads under every furniture leg, replacing them when they wear or trap grit.

Can I use a steam mop on hardwood?

No. Steam forces moisture into wood and can cause cupping and finish failure.

Are pet scratches fixable?

Individually, yes. Widespread claw damage usually means refinishing, plus nail trimming and rugs in the paths pets use.
