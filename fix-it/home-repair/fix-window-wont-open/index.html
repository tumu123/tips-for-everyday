How to Fix a Window That Won't Open

Fix It Home Repair Published October 15, 2026 9 min read

Two problems get confused under “my window won’t open”:

  • The window is stuck — it doesn’t move when you push it
  • The window moves but won’t stay — on a double-hung window, the sash slides back down

These have almost nothing in common. The first is friction or obstruction; the second is a failed balance mechanism. Identifying which one you have determines whether you’re cleaning a track or replacing hardware.

Safety note first: never use force that could break glass or cause you to lose balance. If the window is on an upper floor and you’d need to lean out, get help or call a professional.

Step 0: Identify Your Window Type

TypeHow it movesMost likely cause when stuck
Double-hungTwo sashes slide verticallyPaint seal, swelled wood, failed balance, dirty channel

CasementCranks outward on a hingeCrank mechanism failure, corroded hinge
Awning / hopperCranks out at top or bottomOperator mechanism, corrosion

The treatment is completely different for each. Determine which you have before touching anything.

Step 1: Check for a Paint Seal (Most Common on Older Windows)

If the window hasn’t been opened in years, and especially if it’s painted wood, the paint is almost certainly holding it shut. Paint bridges the gap between the sash and the frame and glues them together.

The fix:

  1. Run a sharp utility knife along every joint where the sash meets the frame — both sides, the top, and the bottom
  2. Cut firmly and repeatedly until the blade passes through freely with no resistance
  3. Do the same on the outside if you can safely reach it — exterior paint seals are usually the tighter ones
  4. Tap gently with a putty knife along the seam to break any remaining bond
  5. Try the window with steady, moderate pressure — not a hard shove

Use a fresh blade. A dull blade tears rather than cuts and leaves a ragged paint bridge.

This resolves the majority of genuinely stuck windows in homes over about twenty years old. It’s also the cheapest fix on this page.

Step 2: For Double-Hung Windows

If the sash won’t move at all:

  1. Clear the channels — vacuum the side channels where the sash slides. Debris and insect nests accumulate there
  2. Check for swelled wood. If the sash fits tightly all around and it’s a humid season, the wood has expanded
  3. Clean the channels with a cloth and a mild cleaner, then dry thoroughly
  4. Apply dry lubricant to the channels — PTFE or silicone spray. Do not use oil, which collects dirt and will worsen the problem within months
  5. Work the sash gently up and down to distribute the lubricant

If the sash slides up but falls back down:

This is not a sticking problem. The counterbalancing mechanism has failed.

MechanismHow to recognize itThe fix
Sash cord and weightOlder windows. A cord runs over a pulley to a weight inside the frameRe-cording — a fiddly but very doable job
Sash chainSimilar to cord, but chainReplace the chain
Spiral balanceA metal spiral rod in the channelReplace the balance tube
Block and tackle balanceA spring-loaded unit in the channelReplace the balance unit

Replacing a balance is a hardware swap, not a repair. You remove the sash, take out the failed unit, and install a matched replacement — balances are specified by length and strength, so take the old one with you when buying.

If one side lifts higher than the other, only one balance has failed. Replace both anyway; the other is the same age and will follow shortly.

Step 3: For Sliding Windows

Clean the track first. Sliding window tracks collect grit, and grit is the most common cause of a hard-sliding window.

  1. Vacuum the track with a crevice tool
  2. Scrub with a brush and a mild cleaner
  3. Check the track for bends or dents — a deformed track will never slide smoothly
  4. Clean the rollers on the bottom of the sash if you can reach them
  5. Apply dry lubricant to the track
  6. Slide it back and forth several times

If cleaning doesn’t help, the rollers have likely worn out or seized. Rollers are replaceable — usually by removing the sash from the frame and swapping the assemblies. Take the old roller to match it.

If the track itself is bent, that’s a frame issue. Minor bends can sometimes be straightened carefully with pliers, but significant deformation means the window is near the end of its service life.

Rollers, track, and alignment fail in that order — getting a sliding patio door to glide again has the test for each.

Step 4: For Casement and Awning Windows

These use a crank operator, so the failure is usually mechanical.

  1. Check whether the crank turns at all. If it spins freely with no movement, the operator’s gear or the linkage has failed
  2. If the crank is stiff, the mechanism needs cleaning and lubrication — remove the cover if possible, clean old hardened grease, and apply fresh
  3. Check the locking mechanism. A casement window that won’t open may simply still be locked, or the lock may be jammed
  4. Inspect the hinge for corrosion, especially on coastal properties
  5. Lubricate the hinge and operator with a dry or light lithium lubricant

Crank operators are replaceable units. If the gear is stripped, replacing the operator is straightforward and the parts are standardised by size and handing (left or right).

Step 5: Dealing With Swelled Wood

Wood absorbs moisture and expands. A window that works fine in winter may bind in summer.

Confirm it’s swelling if the sash is tight all around rather than at one point, and if the problem tracks with the weather.

Options:

  1. Wait for drier weather — sometimes the only right answer
  2. Use a hair dryer on the sash edges to drive moisture out, then try again. This can free a marginally swollen sash
  3. Once dry, sand the binding edge lightly and apply lubricant or a sealer to slow future absorption
  4. Seal the raw wood — bare wood absorbs moisture fastest. Paint or seal any exposed edges

Only sand when the wood is dry. Sanding swollen wood means when it later shrinks, you’ve created a loose rattle — the opposite problem.

Prevention and Maintenance

TaskFrequency
Vacuum tracks and channelsTwice a year
Lubricate moving parts with dry lubricantAnnually
Operate every window fullySeasonally — windows that sit unused seize
Check and replace weatherstrippingEvery few years
Keep weep holes at the bottom of the frame clearAnnually — blocked weep holes cause water accumulation and rot
Repaint or reseal exposed woodAs the finish degrades

Operate every window seasonally. This is the single most effective preventive measure. Windows that sit for years develop paint seals, corrosion, and seized hardware — all of which a two-minute opening would have prevented.

Clear the weep holes. Small slots or holes at the bottom exterior of the frame let water escape. When they clog with paint or debris, water sits in the frame and causes rot — which eventually jams the window permanently.

When to Call a Professional

  • Upper-floor windows requiring anything beyond reaching from inside
  • Broken glass — never attempt to move a sash with cracked glass
  • Failed sealed units (condensation between panes) — the glass unit needs replacing, not the frame
  • Rot in the frame — this is carpentry, and the extent is often worse than it appears
  • Structural movement — if several windows in the house are binding, the frame may be shifting
  • Vinyl window with a failed internal mechanism — often requires partial disassembly best left to someone experienced

Why This Works

Paint forms the most common mechanical bond, and cutting it is the only way to break it. Paint bridges the gap between sash and frame and cures into a rigid connection. No amount of pushing will overcome it, and pushing risks breaking glass. Cutting the film at the joint restores the gap.

A sash that falls is a force-balance failure, not a friction problem. Double-hung windows use a counterweight or spring to offset the sash weight. When that fails, the sash falls regardless of how clean or lubricated the channel is. Lubricating a window with a failed balance produces no improvement at all — which is why distinguishing the two symptoms matters.

Grit is the primary cause of hard sliding. Tracks are horizontal surfaces exposed to dust, and rollers running over grit both resist and wear. Cleaning removes the abrasive; lubrication reduces friction but cannot compensate for grit.

Dry lubricants stay effective where oils do not. Window tracks are exposed to dust by design. Oil makes a surface tacky, and within months a well-oiled track collects enough grit to become worse than before.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my window suddenly hard to open?

Most often dirt in the track, or wood swelling from humidity. On older windows, a paint seal is the most common cause.

How do I free a painted-shut window?

Run a sharp utility knife along every seam where the sash meets the frame, several times, until the blade passes freely. Then try again with steady pressure.

Why won't my window stay open?

The balance mechanism has failed — a cord, chain, spring, or spiral balance. This is a hardware replacement, not a lubrication issue.

Can I use WD-40 on a window track?

It's better than nothing for freeing a seized part, but it attracts dirt and degrades. Use a dry PTFE or silicone lubricant for lasting results.

My window only sticks in summer. Why?

Wood absorbs humidity and swells. Dry the area, and seal any bare wood to slow future absorption.

How often should I open every window?

Seasonally. Windows left closed for years develop paint seals, corrosion, and seized hardware.

What are weep holes?

Small openings at the bottom exterior of the frame that let water drain out. If they clog, water collects and rots the frame.

When should I just replace the window?

When the frame has rotted, the track is deformed, or sealed glass units have failed. Isolated hardware failures are usually worth fixing.
