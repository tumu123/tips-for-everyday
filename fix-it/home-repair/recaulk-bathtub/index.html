How to Recaulk a Bathtub So It Actually Lasts

Fix It Home Repair Published October 15, 2026 9 min read

Two things determine whether a recaulking job lasts for years or fails within months, and both are routinely skipped:

  1. Removing every trace of the old caulk. New caulk does not bond to old caulk. Applying a fresh bead over a partially cleaned seam is the single most common reason this job fails.
  2. Filling the tub with water before you caulk. A bathtub drops slightly under the weight of water and a person. Caulk applied to an empty tub gets stretched the first time someone baths — and tears away from one side.

Get those two right and the rest is straightforward technique.

Step 0: Repair or Full Replacement?

ConditionDo this
One short section has lifted or crackedRemove and redo that section, blending the ends into the existing bead
Mildew staining but the bead is intactTry cleaning first — see how to remove mold from bathroom caulk
Caulk is peeling, cracked, or shrinking in multiple placesFull removal and replacement
Caulk is soft, sticky, or crumblingFull replacement — it has chemically failed
Gaps between tub and wall with visible openingFull replacement, and check for water damage behind
Black staining through the full thickness of the beadFull replacement — surface cleaning won’t reach it

Rule of thumb: if more than about a third of the bead is compromised, redo the whole thing. Patching produces visible joints, and those joints fail first.

Once grout is cracked rather than stained, no amount of cleaning holds it — see regrouting cracked grout for the regrouting sequence.

Step 1: Choose the Right Product

This matters more than people expect, because the wrong product fails quickly in a wet area.

ProductUse in a bathtub?Notes
100% siliconeYes — the right choiceWaterproof, flexible, long-lasting. Look for mildew-resistant bathroom formulations
Siliconized acrylic / latexAcceptable, shorter lifeEasier to work with and paintable, but less durable in constant wetness
Pure acrylic latex caulkNoNot suitable for continuous water exposure; cracks and shrinks
Paintable caulkOnly for trim outside the wet zoneNot for the tub-to-wall joint

Choose 100% silicone for the tub. It’s harder to apply smoothly than acrylic, but it’s the only common product that holds up to daily water exposure.

Colour: white and clear are standard. Clear works where the surrounding surfaces differ in colour; white matches white tubs and tile. Almond and grey are available for non-white fixtures.

Step 2: Remove the Old Caulk Completely

Budget most of your time here. This is the step that determines success.

  1. Apply caulk remover gel along the old bead and let it sit per the product instructions — typically several hours. This softens silicone substantially and saves a lot of scraping
  2. Score along both edges of the bead with a utility knife, so the bead releases cleanly rather than tearing
  3. Pull or scrape out the bead with a removal tool, putty knife, or pliers for long strips
  4. Remove the residue. This is the part people skip. Scrape, then scrub with a stiff brush. Any remaining film will prevent the new caulk from bonding to the surface
  5. Wipe with isopropyl alcohol and let it dry completely

The test: run your fingernail along the seam. If you can feel any rubbery residue, keep cleaning. New silicone will not stick to it.

Check for damage behind the caulk once it’s out. Soft or darkened drywall, crumbling backer board, or a visibly loose tub means water has been getting through — and that needs addressing before you seal it back up.

Step 3: Fill the Tub With Water

This is the step almost everyone misses, and it’s why so much recaulking fails early.

A bathtub flexes under load. When you fill it with water and get in, the tub sinks slightly relative to the wall, widening the joint. If you caulked with the tub empty, the first bath stretches the new bead — and it separates from the tub or the wall.

The procedure:

  1. Fill the tub with water to normal bathing level
  2. Leave it filled while you caulk and while the caulk skins over
  3. Leave it filled for the full curing time recommended on the product — typically 24 hours
  4. Drain it only after the caulk has fully cured

This means the caulk cures in the position the joint will actually be in when the tub is in use. The bead ends up slightly compressed in normal use rather than stretched.

Step 4: Tape, Prime, and Apply

Tape both edges of the joint with painter’s tape, leaving only the gap you intend to fill. This is what produces a clean straight line, and it’s the difference between a professional-looking bead and a wobbly one.

Then apply the caulk:

  1. Cut the tube tip at 45 degrees, and cut the opening small — you can always enlarge it, but an oversized opening makes a mess
  2. Puncture the inner seal inside the tube
  3. Load the gun and squeeze until caulk flows, then release pressure before positioning
  4. Hold the gun at roughly 45 degrees to the surface
  5. Move in one continuous motion along the joint, squeezing steadily. Maintain consistent speed — inconsistent speed produces an uneven bead
  6. Fill the joint completely. Don’t leave a thin surface-only bead; it needs body in the gap
  7. Release the trigger at the end and pull away

Work in one pass per side. Stopping and starting creates lumps. If you must stop, restart from where you left off and blend.

Step 5: Tool the Bead and Remove the Tape

Smooth the bead within a few minutes of applying — silicone skins over quickly.

Use a caulk smoothing tool, a spoon, or a gloved finger. Two methods work well:

  • A caulk finishing tool gives the most consistent result
  • A finger dipped in soapy water (not plain water) works well — the soap prevents sticking. Use a disposable glove

Some people prefer to skip tooling on silicone and just remove the tape carefully, since silicone is harder to smooth than acrylic. Either is fine; consistency matters more than method.

Remove the tape immediately after tooling, pulling it away at a low angle while the caulk is still wet. If you let the caulk skin over first, the tape will pull the edge up with it.

Step 6: Cure Fully Before Use

StageTime
Skin-over10–30 minutes
Water-readyUsually 24 hours — check your product
Full cureUp to several days for thick beads

Do not use the shower or bath until the caulk is water-ready. Products differ, so read the tube. Running water over caulk that hasn’t cured washes it out and ruins the job.

Ventilate well. Silicone gives off acetic acid while curing — that vinegar smell is normal. Open a window and run the extractor fan.

Making It Last

  • Run the extractor fan during and after every shower — the biggest factor in preventing mildew
  • Squeegee or wipe the tub surround after use. Caulk that stays dry lasts dramatically longer
  • Check the bead annually and repair small sections before they become full failures
  • Don’t let shampoo or soap sit on the caulk line — residue feeds mildew

Why This Works

Silicone bonds to clean substrate, not to itself. Cured silicone has a low-energy surface that new silicone adheres to poorly. Any remaining film acts as a release layer, so the new bead lifts away as a continuous strip. Full removal isn’t fussiness — it’s the mechanism.

The joint changes dimension under load, so the cure condition must match the use condition. A tub moves when filled. Caulk that cures in the expanded position is compressed in service, and caulk handles compression far better than tension. Curing in the empty position puts the bead in tension every time someone baths, which tears the bond.

The tape defines the line, because freehand beads are impossible to keep straight. Silicone is too viscous to self-level, so whatever shape comes out of the nozzle is what stays. Tape converts a difficult freehand motion into a simple filling task, and removing it wet leaves a clean edge.

Ventilation determines mildew more than caulk formulation. Mildew-resistant products slow growth, but persistent moisture defeats them. A caulk line that dries between uses stays clean regardless of which product was used.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why fill the tub with water before caulking?

A tub sinks slightly under the weight of water. Caulking with it empty means the bead gets stretched the first time it's used and tears away.

How long does caulk need before I can use the shower?

Usually 24 hours, but check the specific product — formulations differ. Full cure can take several days.

Can I put new caulk over old caulk?

No. It won't bond. Remove the old bead completely, including the residue film, and wipe with alcohol before applying.

What type of caulk should I use?

100% silicone, mildew-resistant, bathroom grade. Acrylic latex is not suitable for constant water exposure.

How do I get a straight bead?

Tape both edges of the joint with painter's tape, apply in one continuous motion, tool it, then remove the tape while still wet.

Why does my new caulk keep getting mold?

Usually persistent moisture and poor ventilation. Use a mildew-resistant product, run the extractor fan, and dry the surround after each use.

What's the vinegar smell?

Normal — silicone releases acetic acid as it cures. Ventilate well; it dissipates.

Should I remove caulk between the tub and the floor?

Yes, and replace it if it has failed. That joint also keeps water out of the subfloor.
