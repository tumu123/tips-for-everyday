How to Stop a Door from Slamming

Fix It Home Repair Published October 17, 2026 6 min read

Most slamming doors get a doorstop, and most of them still slam. The reason is that there are two different problems, and only one is solved by a stopper.

Problem A: the door moves on its own. Open it halfway, let go, and it swings shut by itself. That is not a slamming problem — it is a level problem. The frame or the floor is out of square, so gravity pulls the door closed. A stopper only catches it at the end; the door still travels and still hits.

Problem B: the door stays put, but gets pushed or caught by a draft. Here a stopper or a soft-close device is the right answer.

Test it first: open the door to about halfway and let go. If it moves at all on its own, you have Problem A and Step 2 comes before any stopper.

Step 0: Identify the Cause Before Buying Anything

  1. Do the halfway test. Open the door halfway and release it. Note whether it moves, and which direction.
  2. Try it at several angles — 15 degrees, 45 degrees, 90 degrees. A door that only moves near the closed position has a different issue than one that swings from anywhere.
  3. Check for a draft. Open a window and see whether the door moves. Wind pressure through the house is a very common cause.
  4. Check when heating or cooling is running. Air-handling systems create pressure differences that move doors.
  5. Note who slams it. If it is children or a habit, the fix is partly behavioural — a soft-close device helps, but so does a conversation.

Step 1: Look at the Hinges for Wear or Looseness

  1. Open the door and watch the hinges. If the door drops as it opens, a hinge is loose or worn.
  2. Check each hinge screw and tighten any that have worked loose. A loose hinge lets the door sag, which changes how it swings.
  3. If a screw spins without gripping, the hole is stripped — move to Step 2’s shim approach or use a longer screw that reaches the framing.
  4. Check whether the hinge pin is bent, which is the adjustment used in Step 2.
  5. Squeaking or binding means the hinge needs attention as well.

Step 2: Fix a Door That Swings on Its Own

If the halfway test showed movement, correct the lean before anything else.

  1. Determine the direction. The door swings toward the low side.
  2. Small correction — bend the hinge pin. Remove the top hinge pin, lay it on a hard surface, and tap it gently with a hammer to introduce a very slight bend. Reinsert it. The bend creates friction and slight resistance.
  3. Work in tiny increments. A small bend goes a long way; test after each adjustment.
  4. Larger correction — shim the hinge. Loosen the hinge, slip a thin cardboard or purpose-made shim behind the hinge leaf on the side the door leans toward, and retighten. This changes the hinge angle and therefore the door’s resting position.
  5. Check the frame for plumb with a level if the lean is large. A frame that is significantly out of square may need the jamb adjusted, which is a bigger job.
  6. Re-run the halfway test after each change.

Why this comes before a stopper: a stopper on a self-swinging door just relocates the bang. Fixing the lean removes the energy that causes the slam in the first place.

Step 3: Add Bumpers for the Simplest Noise Fix

If the door does not swing on its own and the issue is impact noise, start here. This is the cheapest and least invasive option.

  1. Clean the door frame where the door makes contact.
  2. Stick adhesive felt or rubber bumpers to the door stop — the strip the door closes against.
  3. Use two or three, spaced along the stop, at the height where the door contacts.
  4. Close the door slowly and check where it touches, then position the bumpers to meet that point.
  5. Choose rubber for a firm door and felt for a lighter one. Rubber absorbs more energy; felt is gentler on finishes.
  6. Check the latch still works. Bumpers that are too thick prevent the door from latching.

This solves the noise, not the travel. If the door still closes hard, combine it with Step 4 or 5.

Step 4: Choose the Right Door Stop

There are four common types, and they suit different situations.

  1. Wall-mounted stop — a fixed post on the wall. Robust, but it needs a stud or an anchor, and it puts a hole in the wall.
  2. Baseboard-mounted stop — screws into the skirting board. Less robust but avoids wall damage in some layouts.
  3. Floor-mounted stop — a dome or post on the floor. Very robust and good for heavy doors, but it is a trip hazard and it obstructs cleaning.
  4. Hinge-pin stop — clamps onto a hinge and blocks the door’s swing at a set angle. No drilling, adjustable, and it works well on hollow-core doors.

Avoid spring-mounted stops for this purpose. A spring stop bounces the door back, which adds motion rather than removing it, and it makes a noise of its own.

Position it correctly: the stop should catch the door before the handle hits the wall, and it should contact the door’s rail — the solid horizontal frame — not the middle of a hollow panel.

Step 5: Fit a Hydraulic Door Closer for a Real Fix

If you want controlled closing every time, this is the most effective option.

  1. Choose a closer rated for your door’s weight. Residential doors usually use a light-to-medium rating; check the door’s weight and width.
  2. Mount it according to the template supplied — usually on the door face and the frame, or on the frame and the door’s top rail depending on the type.
  3. Understand the two adjustments. Most closers have separate controls for sweep speed (most of the travel) and latch speed (the final few inches). Setting the latch speed slower is what prevents slamming.
  4. Adjust the latch speed first. This is the control that stops the bang.
  5. Test and adjust in small increments, and do not overtighten the valve screws.
  6. Note that a closer makes the door close every time — if you sometimes want it held open, use a hold-open function or a separate stop.

Step 6: Deal With Draft-Driven Slams

If wind or the HVAC system is pushing the door:

  1. Identify the pressure source. A window open on the windward side, or an extractor fan running, creates pressure differences.
  2. Close or adjust the window on the opposite side of the house to balance pressure.
  3. Consider whether an extractor fan is running when the door slams — bathroom and kitchen fans move a surprising amount of air.
  4. Seal the gaps around the door if drafts are the issue; that reduces the force available to move it.
  5. Combine with a stop or closer so the door has a controlled endpoint regardless of pressure.

Step 7: Add Finger Protection if There Are Children

A slamming door is also a pinch hazard.

  1. Fit a finger pinch guard on the hinge side — a strip that covers the gap when the door is open.
  2. Use a foam or rubber door wedge to hold a door open when children are around.
  3. Check the closing speed — a soft-close or a closer set slowly dramatically reduces the risk.
  4. Do not rely on a spring stop, which adds unpredictable motion.

Step 8: Maintain It

  1. Check bumpers every few months — adhesive ones dry out and fall off.
  2. Re-check the halfway test seasonally. Houses move with humidity and temperature, and a door that was level in summer may lean in winter.
  3. Tighten hinge screws annually.
  4. Re-adjust a door closer if the closing speed changes, which happens with temperature.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my door slam shut by itself?

Because the frame or floor is out of level, so gravity pulls it closed. Test it by opening the door halfway and letting go — if it moves on its own, fix the lean by bending the top hinge pin slightly or shimming the hinge. A doorstop alone will not fix it.

How do I stop a door from slamming without damaging the wall?

Use a hinge-pin stop, which clamps onto an existing hinge and needs no drilling. Alternatively, stick adhesive rubber bumpers to the door stop to absorb the impact noise.

Are felt or rubber bumpers better?

Rubber absorbs more energy and suits a heavier door; felt is gentler on finishes and suits a light door. Both work on the impact noise. Make sure they are not so thick that the door stops latching.

Will a door closer stop slamming?

Yes, and it is the most effective option. The key is the latch-speed adjustment, which controls the final few inches of travel — that is where slamming happens. Set the sweep speed for the main travel and the latch speed slower.

Why does my door slam only when the wind blows?

Air pressure differences move it. An open window on one side of the house, or a running extractor fan, creates the pressure. Balance the pressure by adjusting windows, and fit a stop or closer so the door has a controlled endpoint.

What is the best doorstop for a hollow-core door?

A hinge-pin stop. It clamps to the hinge, requires no fasteners in the door itself, and is adjustable. Avoid mounting a wall or floor stop that loads the door's hollow panel.

Can I stop a door slamming temporarily?

Yes — a rubber door wedge holds it open, and adhesive felt bumpers reduce the noise immediately. Both are reversible and take under a minute.

How do I protect children's fingers?

Fit a finger pinch guard on the hinge side, use a wedge to hold the door open, and slow the closing speed with bumpers or a closer. Do not rely on a spring stop, which adds unpredictable motion.

How often should I check a door that slams?

Seasonally. Houses move with humidity and temperature, so a door that was level in summer can lean in winter. Re-run the halfway test each season and tighten hinge screws annually.
