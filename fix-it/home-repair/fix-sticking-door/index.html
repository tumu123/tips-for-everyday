How to Fix a Sticking Door (Not by Sanding)

Fix It Home Repair Published October 14, 2026 8 min read
Advertisement

Sanding a sticking door is the most common advice and the last thing you should try. Sanding removes material permanently, and it usually treats a symptom — the door is rarely genuinely too big.

The order that matters: diagnose, then adjust, then sand only as a final step.

Doors stick for five reasons, and they produce different symptoms:

CauseSymptomFix
Sagging from loose top hingeRubs at the top of the latch side or the bottom of the hinge sideReplace the top hinge screws with long screws
Humidity swellingSticks in summer, frees in winter, rubs evenly along an edgeAdjust the strike plate or wait it out; seal the edge
Loose hinge screws (stripped)Door drops or shifts, uneven gapPlug and re-screw
Paint buildup on the jamb or edgeRubs at a specific band, worst after repaintingSand only the painted edge, then seal
Swollen or warped slabRubbing along a long section, door is visibly bowedAssess whether the slab needs replacing

The diagnostic test that identifies the exact contact point: close the door until it just touches, then slide a sheet of paper or a strip of card around the perimeter. Where the paper will not pass, that is where the door rubs. A flashlight aimed at the gap from one side also shows light where the gap exists and shadow where it does not.

Step 1: Tighten and upgrade the hinge screws

This fixes the majority of sticking doors, and it takes ten minutes.

A door hangs from its hinges, and the top hinge carries the most load. Over years, its screws work loose or the wood around them fails, the door sags forward and down, and the top corner of the latch side rubs.

  1. Tighten every screw on all three hinges. Test the door.
  2. If screws spin freely, the holes are stripped. Do not use a bigger screw — plug the holes:
    • Dip a wooden matchstick or toothpick in wood glue.
    • Tap one or more into the hole until it is snug.
    • Snap it off flush with the jamb.
    • Reinstall the original screw into fresh wood.
  3. For the top hinge specifically, upgrade to a 3-inch screw driven into the wall stud behind the jamb. This is the single most effective fix for a sagging door, because it transfers the door’s weight from the thin jamb casing into the framing. Most jamb screws are only an inch long and grip nothing structural.
  4. Check the gap with the paper test. If the gap is now even, done.

Why this works so well: a sag at the top hinge translates to a large lateral movement at the far edge of the door. A small amount of sag at the hinge becomes a significant rub at the latch side, which is why people sand the latch edge and cannot fix it. The problem is at the hinge.

Step 2: Rule out humidity swelling

Interior doors are usually made of wood or a wood composite, and both absorb moisture from humid air. A door that sticks from June to September and frees in October is a humidity problem, not a size problem.

How to identify it: the rub appears evenly along an edge rather than at one corner; the door was fine in winter; the sticking started during a humid stretch or after a weather change.

What to do about it:

  • Wait if you can. A seasonal door often frees itself when the humidity drops. Sanding a seasonal door guarantees it will rattle in winter.
  • Reduce indoor humidity. A dehumidifier or better ventilation addresses the actual cause.
  • Seal the door edges. Paint or varnish the top, bottom, and side edges of the door — particularly the bottom edge, which is almost always left unfinished by builders. Sealing slows moisture absorption and is the durable fix for a chronically swelling door.
  • Adjust the strike plate so the door latches without needing force, if the rub is minor. Move the plate slightly outward by filing the screw holes or by filing the plate opening, rather than shaving the door.

Never sand a seasonal door to fit in one season. You will remove the material needed for the other season, and the door will be undersized permanently.

Step 3: Adjust the strike plate (for a door that will not latch)

If the door closes but the latch will not catch, or you have to push hard to make it latch, the strike plate is misaligned rather than the door being oversized. This is a different problem from rubbing and needs no sanding.

  1. Check whether the latch is hitting above or below the strike plate opening by closing the door slowly and looking at the marks it leaves.
  2. Tighten the strike plate screws first. A loose plate shifts.
  3. If the plate is slightly high or low, move it: fill the old screw holes with glued matchsticks, then re-drill at the new position a few millimeters up or down.
  4. If the latch is barely caught, filing the top or bottom of the strike plate opening outward with a metal file is enough. File in small increments and test frequently.
  5. For a deeper offset, carefully enlarge the opening in the jamb with a chisel — or use an adjustable strike plate, which has a larger opening and accommodates minor misalignment without chiseling.

The bowing-the-hinge trick: bending a hinge pin slightly is a traditional technique for adjusting a door’s position. It works and it is reversible, but bending too far kinks the pin and creates a new problem. It is worth knowing and easy to overdo.

Step 4: Sand, but only as a last resort

If the door genuinely is too large — usually because of paint buildup or a slab that was cut slightly oversized — sanding is correct. Do it carefully, and only after Steps 1–3 have been ruled out.

  1. Identify the exact rub line with the paper test. Mark it with a pencil.
  2. Sand only the rub line, and only as much as needed. Use 220 grit on a sanding block, working along the grain.
  3. Check frequently. Sand, test the door, and repeat. It is easy to remove too much and impossible to add it back.
  4. Never sand the hinge side unless you have no alternative. Removing material there changes how the door sits in the frame and can create a gap that lets light and air through.
  5. Seal the sanded edge immediately with paint, primer, or varnish. Bare wood absorbs moisture and swells, which brings back the sticking — and now the door is both smaller and still swelling.
  6. For a large correction, use a block plane on the edge for a controlled cut rather than sanding, then seal.

The bottom edge deserves care. Doors are often cut to fit and left unfinished on the bottom. That unfinished edge is a moisture sponge, and it is a common cause of doors that swell and drag on the threshold.

Step 5: Handle the specific cases

SituationFix
Door rubs the top of the frameTop hinge sag — long screws first, then plug stripped holes
Door rubs the bottom of the frameCheck for a loose bottom hinge, and check whether the threshold or floor is high
Door rubs the latch sideSag or humidity; hinge fix first
Door rubs the hinge sideRare; usually a warped jamb or a hinge set too deep
Door closes but will not latchStrike plate alignment, not door size
Door rattles when closedWeatherstripping or a loose latch plate
Door rubs only in summerHumidity swelling — seal the edges, do not sand
Door rubs after paintingPaint buildup — sand the painted edge lightly, then seal
Door drags on carpetCarpet height or a sagging door — adjust hinges, or trim the bottom

Why This Works

A door is a lever hanging from hinges. Because the latch side is far from the hinge axis, a tiny displacement at the hinge becomes a large displacement at the edge. This geometry is why the fix for a sticking door is usually at the hinge, even when the symptom is at the opposite corner — and why sanding the rubbing edge so often fails.

Stripped screw holes are a wood-strength issue, not a screw-size issue. A larger screw forces the surrounding wood apart and holds poorly. A glued wooden plug restores solid material and lets the original screw hold again. A 3-inch screw into the stud bypasses the jamb entirely and reaches structural framing, which is why it resolves sag that no amount of jamb-screw tightening will.

Humidity swelling is a moisture-content problem. Wood expands as it takes on moisture, and a door edge that was cut to fit at a low moisture content will not fit at a higher one. Sealing the edges reduces the rate of moisture exchange, but sanding a seasonal door is a mistake because you would be removing material that is only needed in one season.

Paint buildup is a thickness problem, and it is genuinely one of the few cases where removing material is the fix. Even then, sealing the sanded surface is essential because bare wood will absorb moisture and undo the correction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my door stick only in summer? Wood swells as it absorbs moisture from humid air. A door that sticks from June to September and frees in autumn has a humidity problem, not a size problem. Seal the door edges, especially the bottom, and consider reducing indoor humidity. Do not sand it.

What is the fastest fix for a sticking door? Tighten every hinge screw, then replace the top hinge’s screws with 3-inch screws driven into the stud. That resolves a large share of sticking doors in about ten minutes and is fully reversible.

How do I find exactly where the door rubs? Close the door until it just touches, then slide a sheet of paper or card around the perimeter. Where it will not pass is the contact point. A flashlight aimed at the gap from either side also shows the location clearly.

Should I sand the door or the frame? Sand the door, not the frame, because the jamb usually has more structural importance and paint buildup is typically on the door edge. Whichever you sand, seal it immediately afterward.

Why does my door not latch even though it closes? That is a strike plate alignment problem, not a door size problem. Tighten the plate screws first, then reposition or file the strike plate opening. Never sand a door to fix a latch that will not catch.

How do I fix a stripped hinge screw hole? Dip a wooden matchstick or toothpick in wood glue, tap it into the hole, snap it flush, and reinstall the screw. For badly damaged holes, drill out and glue in a dowel plug, then re-drill a pilot hole.

Can I just plane the bottom of a door? Yes, and a block plane gives more control than sanding. Take off material in small passes from the bottom edge only if the door drags on the floor or carpet, and seal the cut edge immediately afterward.

Will a sticking door fix itself? Sometimes, if the cause is seasonal humidity. If the door stuck evenly along an edge and the weather has since dried out, it may free on its own. If it sticks at one corner, that is a hinge sag and it will not resolve itself.
