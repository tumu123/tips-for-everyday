House Centipedes: The Case for Not Killing the Thing on Your Wall

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 11 min read

It is on the bathroom wall at 2 a.m., and it moves like something that should not be able to move that fast indoors. Fifteen pairs of legs, a body about an inch and a half long, a fringe of striped legs that seem to ripple rather than step, and the ability to cross a wall and disappear behind a towel in under two seconds.

Almost everyone kills it. That reaction is understandable, and it is also the wrong move, for a reason that has nothing to do with sentimentality.

The house centipede, Scutigera coleoptrata, is a predator. It eats the pests you actually do not want: cockroaches and their nymphs, silverfish, firebrats, clothes moth larvae, carpet beetle larvae, earwigs, spiders, and termites. It is one of the few animals in an American house that hunts other household pests as its full-time occupation, and it does so without damaging anything, without breeding in your food, and without leaving a residue.

Killing it removes a control agent and leaves its prey. That is the whole argument, and the rest of this guide is about what to do instead — which is to change the conditions that let it thrive, so that it stops finding the house worth living in. What It Actually Is

A house centipede is not an insect. It is a centipede, in the class Chilopoda, and the difference matters for one practical reason: centipedes are predators by body design, with venom glands and modified front legs that function as fangs. Insects in your house are mostly detritivores, fabric eaters, or blood feeders. A centipede is a hunter.

The fifteen pairs of legs are a feature of the adult. Immatures hatch with four pairs and add a pair with each molt, up to fifteen, which takes several months. An adult can live three to seven years, which is a long time for an arthropod and part of why a population feels persistent.

Two behaviors explain most sightings. It is strictly nocturnal, spending the day in cracks, under baseboards, behind a water heater, in a floor drain, or in the gap between a cabinet and a wall. And it is fast — among the fastest arthropods, capable of well over a foot per second, which is why it appears to teleport. It hunts by speed and by touch, not by sight.

It also has a defensive trick: when seized, it can shed legs, and the detached legs continue to twitch, which distracts a predator while the centipede escapes. A centipede missing several legs is not sick; it is a survivor.

The Risk, Stated Plainly

The house centipede can bite, and it does have venom, but the practical picture is reassuring. Its fangs are weak and are usually unable to penetrate human skin. When a bite does occur — typically when one is trapped against skin inside clothing or bedding — the reaction is comparable to a mild bee sting: local pain, redness, slight swelling, resolving within hours. There is no record of serious envenomation from this species, and it is not a disease vector.

It causes no structural damage. It does not eat wood, fabric, paper, or food. It does not nest. It does not infest stored goods. It does not leave droppings that stain. Its entire footprint in your house is that it exists and that it is startling to look at.

Compare that to what it eats. A silverfish consumes the starch in book bindings, wallpaper paste, and cotton. A cockroach contaminates food and triggers asthma in children. Termites consume the building. The centipede is, in household terms, on your side of that ledger.

Why They Are in the House: Two Conditions

Centipedes need two things, and if either is missing they cannot persist.

Moisture. A house centipede loses water rapidly through its cuticle and has no way to conserve it. In a house below about 50 percent relative humidity, they die within days. This is why they appear in bathrooms, basements, laundry rooms, kitchen sinks, and crawlspaces, and why they are almost never found in a dry, well-sealed, air-conditioned bedroom. They cannot drink; they need ambient humidity.

Prey. A centipede eats one to several small insects a day when it can, and it will not stay where there is nothing to hunt. Its presence is direct evidence that something else is there.

Both conditions are fixable, and neither involves a pesticide. That is the useful conclusion: the presence of a centipede is a diagnostic result, and the treatment is aimed at what the diagnosis found.

When a Centipede Population Means Something Else

One centipede every few weeks is a wandering individual. Several per week, or a size range from small to full-grown, is a resident population — and a resident population requires a food supply.

If you are seeing them regularly, check for the prey before doing anything about the predator:

What to Do Instead of Killing It

Dry the room. This is the single most effective measure and it works without touching the animal. A dehumidifier set to 50 percent in a basement or crawlspace, a working bathroom exhaust fan run for twenty minutes after each shower, a floor that dries, and a fix for any weephose or trap leak will reduce the population to near zero over a season. A house centipede cannot survive a dry environment; you do not have to kill it.

Remove the prey. Glue monitors placed along baseboards will tell you what is living in the room. If they catch silverfish or roach nymphs, treat that problem, and the centipede food supply goes with it.

Reduce the harbor points. Seal the wall-floor joint with a bead of caulk, close the gaps around plumbing penetrations under sinks, and clear the clutter under the water heater and behind the washer. A centipede needs a dark, humid crack to spend the day in. Fewer cracks, fewer centipedes.

Keep the space ventilated. A crawlspace without a vapor barrier holds humidity at 70 percent or more year-round, which is a permanent centipede habitat and also a permanent rot risk. Encapsulation or a vapor barrier plus ventilation is the durable fix.

Remove one and release it. If a particular individual is intolerable, invert a glass over it, slide a card underneath, and put it outside in leaf litter. It will not find its way back and it will not be replaced any faster than the environment allows.

The Counterproductive Part: Spraying

Broad-spectrum residual spraying against centipedes is self-defeating, and it is worth understanding exactly why.

A pyrethroid spray applied along baseboards kills the centipedes that walk over it, and it also kills the spiders, the ground beetles, and the other generalist predators that were doing the same job. It does not kill the cockroach eggs inside a cabinet, the silverfish in a box of books, or the termites in the sill plate. The result, over months, is fewer predators and the same prey — a house that is easier for the pests to occupy than it was before.

There is also a resistance effect. Cockroaches and silverfish in treated buildings develop tolerance faster than their predators do, because they have shorter generations and larger populations. Repeated spraying in multifamily buildings is a well-documented route to a harder-to-control roach problem.

The exception is when a specific prey species has reached infestation level, in which case, treat that species with a targeted method — gel bait for roaches, a growth regulator for fabric pests — and leave the predator alone. If You Cannot Live With Them

Some people cannot share a house with a fast-moving fifteen-legged arthropod, and that is a legitimate position rather than an irrational one. The path that actually works, in order:

  1. Dry the building first. Dehumidifier in the basement, exhaust fans in the wet rooms, fix every leak. This alone often ends the problem within a season, because the animal cannot survive dry air.
  2. Treat the prey. Monitors tell you what is there; targeted treatment removes the food supply.
  3. Seal the harborage. Caulk the wall-floor joint, close plumbing gaps, and get the clutter off the floor in the basement and under the sinks.
  4. Use sticky monitors to track progress rather than to kill — placed along baseboards in the wet rooms, they show whether the population is falling.
  5. Only then consider a labeled indoor product, applied to cracks and voids rather than broadcast over surfaces, and accept that it will reduce numbers rather than eliminate them.

If the house has a chronic damp basement and the centipedes are constant, the real project is the basement. That is a moisture job, not a pest job, and the diagnostic sequence for it is in how to find moisture problems early.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are house centipedes dangerous to humans or pets?

They can bite if trapped against skin, and the reaction is comparable to a mild bee sting that resolves in hours. Their fangs usually cannot penetrate human skin. They are not venomous in any medically significant way, do not transmit disease, and are not a threat to dogs or cats.

What do house centipedes eat?

Cockroaches and their nymphs, silverfish, firebrats, clothes moth larvae, carpet beetle larvae, earwigs, spiders, and termites. They are generalist predators and will take almost any small invertebrate they can catch. That diet is why they are considered beneficial indoors.

Why do I only see them at night?

They are strictly nocturnal and highly sensitive to light and vibration. During the day they hide in cracks, behind baseboards, under appliances, and in floor drains. A sighting usually means the animal was moving between harborages and was disturbed.

How do I get rid of house centipedes permanently?

By removing moisture and prey rather than killing the centipedes. A dehumidifier keeping the basement at 50 percent, working exhaust fans in bathrooms, sealed wall-floor joints, and treatment of any silverfish or cockroach population will end the conditions they need. In a dry house, they cannot persist.

Does finding one mean I have cockroaches?

Not necessarily, but it means something is available to eat. One centipede can be a wanderer from a damp crawlspace. A regular population means a resident food supply, and glue monitors along the baseboards will tell you what it is.

Do house centipedes come up through drains?

They are found in floor drains and basement sinks because those are the most humid points available, and they can travel through plumbing in some cases. A floor drain that stays wet is a permanent harbor. A dry trap or a drain cover reduces it.

Will an exterminator get rid of them?

A professional can reduce the population with crack-and-crevice treatment and can identify the prey species, but no treatment is durable if the moisture remains. In a chronically damp basement, the effective order is moisture control first, pest control second. If a company proposes broadcast spraying for centipedes alone without addressing humidity, the result will be temporary.
