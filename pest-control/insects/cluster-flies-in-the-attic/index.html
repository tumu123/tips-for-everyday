Cluster Flies in the Attic: Why They Come Inside and Why Spraying Makes It Worse

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 12 min read

On a warm afternoon in early October, the south-facing upstairs windows are covered in flies. Not one or two — dozens, crawling slowly, buzzing against the glass, falling onto the sill and buzzing again. There is no garbage nearby, no rotting food, no dead animal. The house is clean. And the same thing happened last autumn.

These are cluster flies, Pollenia rudis, and almost everything people assume about a fly in the house is wrong for this species. It does not breed in garbage. It does not lay eggs in food. Its larva is a parasite of earthworms, living in the soil of your lawn, and the adult that is crawling on your window will never reproduce indoors at all. It came in to spend the winter asleep.

That changes the entire response. A housefly problem is a sanitation problem. A cluster fly problem is a building-envelope problem with a narrow seasonal window, and the only durable fix is closing the gaps before the flight period ends. This is the second of the two autumn entry patterns — for the other one, the sun-tracking beetle that gathers on exterior walls, see why boxelder bugs gather on one wall.

Confirm the Identification First

This matters because the response differs completely. Cluster flies and houseflies look similar at a glance and are treated in opposite ways.

SizeSlightly larger, 6–10 mm, stouter5–7 mm, slimmer
AppearanceDense golden hairs on the thorax, wings held overlapping at restGrey thorax with dark stripes, wings held apart
MovementSlow, sluggish, crawls rather than darts; clusters in one placeFast, erratic, hard to swat
Season indoorsAutumn and winter, plus early springSummer, whenever windows are open
Where they gatherAttic, wall voids, upper-floor windows, roof trussesKitchen, trash, anywhere with food
Breeding site in the houseNone — neverGarbage, manure, decaying matter

The behavioral difference is the easiest test. A fly that sits still and lets you approach it, then crawls a few inches when disturbed, is a cluster fly. A housefly accelerates away the moment you move.

Cluster flies also show up in numbers, in one place, all at once. That is the behavior behind the name — they aggregate into a cluster to overwinter, and in a wall void or an attic corner they will pack together in a dense mass.

Why Your Lawn Is the Reason

The cluster fly life cycle runs entirely outside the building, and it runs through the earthworm population under your grass.

Females lay eggs in the soil in summer. The larvae seek out earthworms, bore into them, and develop inside the worm before pupating in the soil. There are typically two to four generations per season. The adults that emerge in late summer and early autumn are the ones that will overwinter, and their trigger is shortening day length and falling temperature.

This has a consequence that is genuinely counterintuitive: cluster fly populations correlate with earthworm density, so a lawn with a healthy, well-watered, organically amended soil profile can support a larger cluster fly population than a poor one. A thick layer of thatch, heavy irrigation, and annual top-dressing all favor earthworms. This is not a reason to degrade your lawn — the earthworm population is doing exactly what it should — but it explains why a well-kept yard occasionally produces more flies than a neglected one, and it explains why you cannot eliminate cluster flies at the source.

You cannot treat the earthworms. What you can do is prevent the adults from getting into the building during the four to six weeks when they are looking for a way in.

Why Indoor Spraying Is the Wrong Move

Spraying the flies you can see produces two problems and solves none.

The first is that the visible flies are a small fraction of the population. Most are already inside the wall voids, the attic, and the soffits, where no surface spray reaches. The ones on the window are the ones that emerged early or got pushed out of the cluster.

The second is that the dead ones stay in the wall. A spray that kills a large group inside a void leaves hundreds of desiccated corpses in a place you cannot clean, and those corpses become food for dermestid beetles — carpet beetles and larder beetles — which then move into the living space and start eating wool, silk, and stored food. This is a documented and common secondary infestation: the fly problem ends and a fabric pest problem begins.

For what that follow-on problem looks like, see how to identify carpet beetles indoors. The short version is that a dead cluster fly in a wall is worth more attention than a live one on a window.

Step 1: Do the Work in the Right Month

Timing is the whole strategy, and the window is narrow.

Cluster flies begin moving toward buildings when nights drop below about 50°F, typically from late August through early October depending on latitude. They enter through the same small gaps that admit other overwintering insects and, once inside, work into wall voids and attics and become dormant.

If you seal in November, you are sealing after the population is in. If you seal in June, the sealant has months to weather before it matters, but you have time to do it properly. The effective window is late August through mid-September — and the second-best time is now, before the flight ends.

Mark it on a calendar: seal by the first week of September, and inspect again in the first warm days of March when the overwintered adults try to leave.

Step 2: Seal From the Outside, Top to Bottom

Cluster flies enter high and low, and the high entries are the ones that matter most because they lead directly to the attic.

Start at the roof line and work down:

  • Soffit and fascia gaps. The joint between the soffit panel and the fascia board is a continuous thin opening along the entire eave, and it is where most overwintering insects enter an attic. Caulk it or fill it with a backer rod and sealant.
  • Attic vents. Screen every gable vent, ridge vent, and static vent with fine mesh. A quarter-inch mesh stops cluster flies; standard insect screen is better.
  • Roof-to-wall junctions and chimney flashing. Any gap in the flashing line is an entrance.
  • Weep holes in brick veneer. These must stay open for drainage, but you can fit them with perforated weep hole inserts that admit water and exclude insects.
  • Exterior utility penetrations — electric service, cable, gas, AC line sets, dryer vents. Fill the annulus with expandable foam, not with a smear of caulk over the top.
  • Window and door frames, especially on the south and west faces, which are the warmest surfaces.
  • The attic hatch or access panel. Weatherstrip it so it closes against a gasket. This is the one interior-facing gap that matters, because a hatch that does not seal lets everything that got into the attic into the house.

The full list of building gaps and the materials that hold up is in sealing gaps around doors and windows. For cluster flies, prioritize the top third of the building.

Step 3: Attend to the Attic Itself

The attic is the primary overwintering site, and it is also where an existing population will be concentrated. If you can access it safely:

  • Inspect in September, before the flight ends, with a flashlight along the gable ends, under the ridge, and in the corners where the roof meets the floor. Cluster flies gather in the warmest, brightest spots, often near a vent.
  • Look for the cluster itself. A dense patch of flies in a corner is the overwintering mass. In cold weather they are motionless and can be vacuumed directly.
  • Screen the vents you cannot seal. An attic needs ventilation; it does not need an open path for insects. Screened vents keep the airflow and stop the entry.
  • Do not fog the attic. Same corpse problem, larger scale, plus a fire risk in a space full of dry wood.

Where the attic has already been used by wildlife, the entry work overlaps with a bigger job. For that, see squirrels in the attic. A soffit gap that admits a squirrel also admits a few thousand flies.

Step 4: Manage the Light

Cluster flies are attracted to warmth and light, and an exterior light left on after dark pulls them toward the building and toward whatever gap is nearest.

  • Switch exterior lights to warm-white or yellow, or better, to a motion-activated fixture. Cool-white and mercury-vapor lamps attract far more insects.
  • Aim exterior fixtures away from the wall rather than washing the siding.
  • Keep a porch light off during the flight period unless it is needed. This is a small inconvenience with a measurable effect on how many flies find your soffit.
  • Interior lights with unsealed windows are an invitation into the room. Draw the blinds in the evening during September.

Step 5: Handle What Is Already Inside

You cannot seal your way out of a population that is already in the walls. For the ones that appear indoors:

  • Vacuum, do not spray. A vacuum with a hose and a sealed bag removes the visible flies without leaving corpses in the wall. Empty the bag outdoors immediately. Flies in a vacuum can survive for a while and will emerge.
  • Sticky ribbons near upper-floor windows during the autumn emergence and again in early spring. Ugly but effective, and they give you a count to judge the population.
  • Do not squash them on walls. Cluster flies leave a greasy mark that is harder to remove than housefly spots, and a crushed fly attracts dermestids.
  • Deal with the bodies. Any window sill or floor area where dead flies accumulate must be vacuumed regularly through the winter. The corpses are the actual risk.

Step 6: The Spring Exit Is Not a New Infestation

In March, on the first warm days, the overwintered flies wake up and try to get out. They head for light, which means windows, and they appear in numbers on the same upper-floor sills.

This is the exit, not an invasion. Two things follow:

  • Open the windows on the warm side of the house for an hour on those first mild afternoons and let them leave. It costs nothing and reduces the population that will try again.
  • Do not treat the spring emergence as a failure of last autumn’s sealing. The flies you are seeing were already inside before you sealed. What you are measuring is the result of last year, not this one.

The test of whether the sealing worked comes the following September, when the new generation should not be able to get in.

What NOT to Do

Do not use a bug bomb in the attic or a wall void. It kills a population you cannot remove, and the corpses feed carpet beetles for months. This converts a nuisance into a fabric pest infestation.

Do not spray the exterior wall as a substitute for sealing. A residual on the siding kills flies that rest on it, and it is a reasonable supplementary measure during the flight period if applied before the flies arrive. It is not a substitute, because the gaps remain open.

Do not hunt for a breeding site in the house. There isn’t one. Cluster fly larvae develop inside earthworms in the lawn. Every hour spent looking for a source indoors is wasted, and every product applied indoors is unnecessary.

Do not assume the house is dirty. Cluster flies have no relationship to household sanitation. A spotless house with an unsealed soffit will fill up; a messy house with a tight envelope will not.

Do not try to treat the lawn for earthworms. Earthworms are beneficial, and the cluster fly larvae are a small fraction of them. There is no approved or sensible lawn treatment for this, and destroying the worm population damages the soil.

When to Consider Professional Help

Most cluster fly problems are solved by sealing. Consider professional service when:

  • The attic has a very large overwintering population that you cannot safely access or remove.
  • The building has wall voids or a knee-wall space that cannot be opened for cleaning, and dead flies are producing a persistent dermestid problem indoors.
  • The building is large or multi-unit and the entry points cannot be identified.
  • You want an exterior residual application timed to the flight period, which is genuinely more effective when applied with proper equipment just before the flies arrive.

A professional will also tell you, honestly, that sealing is the durable fix. If a company proposes recurring indoor fogging for cluster flies, that is a treatment for a symptom that will recur every September.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are cluster flies dangerous or do they spread disease?

They are not considered significant disease vectors. Unlike houseflies, they do not breed in filth and do not move between manure and food. They do not bite. The practical problems are the nuisance of the numbers, the spots they leave, and the secondary pests their corpses attract.

Why are there so many flies on my windows in the fall?

You are seeing the overwintering generation that got inside before the building was sealed, plus individuals that emerged from wall voids into the living space. They head for light and warmth, which means south- and west-facing windows on upper floors.

Do cluster flies lay eggs in the house?

No. The larvae are parasites of earthworms and develop in soil. There is no indoor breeding site for this species, which is why searching for one indoors is pointless and why sanitation changes have no effect.

Why does my neighbor not have them and I do?

The population depends on the earthworm density in the surrounding soil and on the tightness of the building envelope. A house with an unsealed soffit line next to a well-watered lawn will collect them; a tightly built house on the same street may not.

Will they go away on their own?

The ones inside will die over the winter or leave in spring. The population returns each autumn from the lawn, which is why the sealing work has to be done before the flight period rather than after.

How long do cluster flies live indoors?

Overwintering adults can survive several months in a dormant state and emerge in spring. Indoors, without food, most die within weeks, and the accumulation of bodies is the main cleanup task.

Does an exterminator get rid of cluster flies permanently?

No treatment is permanent, because the population is renewed from the lawn every year. A professional can reduce an existing indoor population and can apply a timed exterior residual, but the durable fix is closing the building gaps. Expect seasonal recurrence in an unsealed building no matter what is applied.
