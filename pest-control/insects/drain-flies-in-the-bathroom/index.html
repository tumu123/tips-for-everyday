Drain Flies Are Not Fruit Flies: Where They Really Come From

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 12 min read

The flies on the bathroom wall are shaped wrong. They are fuzzy, they hold their wings out at an angle like a tent, and when they move they lurch in short hops rather than flying in circles. Most people look at that and call it a fruit fly problem, buy a vinegar trap, and wait. Nothing happens, because the trap is in the wrong room for the wrong animal.

Drain flies — also called moth flies or sewer flies — do not fly into your house from outside. They complete their entire larval life inside the thin film of organic slime that coats the inside of a drain pipe, then emerge as adults through the grate. The pipe is not the route they took to get in. The pipe is where they were born.

That single fact changes the whole method. You are not excluding an invader, you are removing a habitat. The general sequence of identifying a pest before treating it and excluding before spraying is set out in the DIY pest control method; this guide is the drain fly version, and the version for a different small fly is covered in pantry moths versus fruit flies.

Why Pouring Water Down the Drain Does Nothing

The standard advice is to pour boiling water down the drain, or bleach, or a kettle of both. Here is what actually happens to that water.

A 1.5-inch drain pipe holds about a cup of water per foot of run. A kettle holds eight cups. When you pour it in, the water travels down the center of the pipe as a narrow stream and reaches the trap, where it sits. The biofilm that drain flies feed on is a gel layer perhaps a millimeter thick, adhered to the pipe wall, above and around the water line. A stream of hot water passes over it and leaves most of it intact. The parts it does soften re-form within days, because the same soap scum, hair, skin cells, and grease keep arriving.

Bleach is worse as a treatment. It kills the surface layer it touches, which temporarily reduces the food supply, and it also suppresses the bacteria that would otherwise break the film down. You get a few quiet days and then a rebound. Meanwhile the bleach has been diluted to a few parts per million by the time it reaches the parts that matter.

The mechanical removal of that film, by contrast, works immediately and permanently — which is the same principle behind clearing a slow drain in general, described in how to unclog a slow drain.

A drain supports more than one species. For the one that appears on the floor beside it, see the other insect living in the same biofilm.

Step 1: Confirm the Species Before You Treat Anything

Do this part in the evening; drain flies are most active then, and a bathroom light left on for twenty minutes will concentrate them on the mirror and the wall above the sink.

Three checks separate a drain fly from a fruit fly:

FeatureDrain flyFruit fly
BodyFuzzy, dense hair, looks like a tiny mothSmooth, tan and black striped abdomen
Wings at restHeld out in a shallow roof or heart shapeFolded flat over the back
FlightShort hops, snake-like, lands abruptlyCircles, hovers, lands and takes off fast
Where it landsWall above a drain, shower curtain, ceilingFruit bowl, bin, glass, bottle
Bait that attracts itNothing sweet; it does not feed as an adultVinegar, wine, overripe fruit

The bait column is the decisive one. Adult drain flies do not eat. They live a few days, mate, and lay eggs, and they are not interested in anything in your kitchen. A vinegar trap in a bathroom catching nothing is not evidence that the trap failed. It is evidence that you have drain flies.

If you still are not certain, tape a clear plastic cup upside down over a drain grate overnight. Flies that emerge through the grate collect inside the cup. That confirms the source directly and also tells you which drain is producing them.

Step 2: Inventory Every Drain, Not Just the Obvious One

Drain flies rarely occupy only the drain you notice. Count the wet points in the room and check each one, because the population you see on the wall is the output of all of them combined.

  • The shower or tub drain. First suspect if the trap is slow or the stopper has never been removed.
  • The sink drain and the overflow hole. The overflow is the most overlooked. It is a hollow channel behind the basin that stays wet, receives soap and skin cells, and cannot be reached by water poured down the main drain.
  • The toilet base. If the wax ring has failed, seepage under the flange feeds a population that appears to come from nowhere.
  • The floor drain, if there is one. A floor drain in a rarely used room may have a trap that has dried out entirely, letting sewer gas and flies up.
  • The washing machine standpipe and its drain pan.
  • A mop sink or utility sink, especially one that receives dirty water and is never scrubbed.
  • The condensate line from an air handler, if it discharges into a floor drain nearby.

Write the list on paper with a column for each. You will use it twice — once to clean, once to verify — and the verification pass is the one that decides whether the job is finished.

Step 3: Brush the Pipe, Do Not Rinse It

This is the step that resolves the problem, and it is unpleasant enough that most people skip it.

Remove the stopper or grate. Most pop-up stoppers lift out after you unscrew the pivot rod behind the drain, or simply pull straight up if it is a push-button type. Underneath you will find a surface that is not metal but slime.

Use a stiff nylon brush — a bottle brush, a dedicated drain brush, or an old toothbrush for the overflow — and scrub the visible pipe wall in a circular motion, working down as far as the brush reaches. Pull the brush out, wipe it on a paper towel, and go again. Keep going until the brush comes out clean rather than grey.

For the overflow hole, work a small brush into the slot horizontally while running a cup of water into the basin, and catch the runoff. Repeat until what comes back is clear rather than grey. Do not fill the basin and let it drain through the overflow — that rinses the surface without touching the film on the channel wall.

For a tub or shower drain where the film extends past arm’s reach, a wet-dry vacuum is the practical tool. With the machine on, seal the hose over the drain opening with a rag and let it pull for thirty seconds. The negative pressure lifts loose film and standing water out of the trap. Then brush what you can reach and vacuum again.

Do the same work on every drain on your list, on the same day. Cleaning one drain and leaving three is how a population persists for months.

Step 4: Treat the Film Chemically After You Have Broken It Up

Mechanical removal takes off the bulk. What remains is the thin layer re-adhering to the pipe wall, and that is where an enzyme product earns its place.

Enzyme drain maintainers contain bacterial cultures and enzymes that digest the organic fraction of the film — the proteins, fats, and starches — over a period of hours to days rather than instantly. They are not caustic, they do not damage seals, and they keep working after the water has passed because the bacteria colonize the surface.

Use them in the evening, when the drain will not be used for eight or more hours. Follow the dose on the label; more is not better, because a heavy dose of a product that needs time is just money down the drain. Apply to every drain on your list, not only the worst one.

Three cautions:

  1. Do not follow an enzyme treatment with bleach. Bleach kills the cultures you just paid for. If you use bleach for another reason, wait a week.
  2. Do not use enzyme products in a drain you have just treated with a caustic clog remover. The chemistry conflicts and the caustic has already done whatever it was going to do.
  3. Keep treating weekly for a month. The film regrows from residual material within about ten days at normal bathroom temperatures of 68 to 75°F. A single application interrupts one generation; a month of weekly applications clears the niche.

Step 5: Close the Other Ways In

Adult flies that emerge in one bathroom can move to another. More importantly, a dry trap is an open door for both flies and sewer gas.

  • Fill every floor drain trap. Pour a quart of water into any floor drain you do not use, once a month. If a drain is in a space that stays dry and unused, fit a sealing plug or a membrane trap guard.
  • Replace a failed wax ring. If the toilet rocks, if the floor around the base stays damp, or if there is staining on the ceiling below, the ring has failed. This is a plumber job and it is also a rot problem, not just a fly problem.
  • Seal the gap where the drain pipe meets the floor or wall. A bead of silicone around the escutcheon closes a path that flies use and that also leaks air.
  • Check the caulk line at the tub and shower. Failed caulk holds a wet organic layer behind it, which is a larval habitat in its own right; the repair sequence is set out in removing mold from bathroom caulk.

Step 6: Verify With a Count, Not a Feeling

Drain flies are easy to declare solved because they are small and you stop noticing them. Count instead.

Tape a strip of paper near the worst drain and mark a tally each evening for two weeks, at the same time of day with the light on. The number should fall to zero and stay there. A count that drops from fifteen to two and holds at two is not a success — two flies a day is a breeding population, and it will return to fifteen within a month.

If the count plateaus rather than falling, the remaining source is somewhere you have not cleaned. The usual candidates, in order: the overflow hole, a second bathroom’s sink, a floor drain under an appliance, and a failed wax ring.

When Drain Flies Mean a Broken Pipe

A persistent population that survives thorough cleaning of every drain is a diagnostic signal worth taking seriously.

Drain flies require wet organic material. In a drain that is being cleaned weekly, the film should not have time to establish. If it does anyway, there is a continuous supply of moisture and nutrients reaching it from somewhere else. The common causes:

  • A drain line with a crack or a failed joint under the floor, seeping into the slab or the subfloor
  • A slow leak at a tub overflow gasket or a drain flange
  • A leaking supply line in the same wall cavity, keeping the framing damp
  • A sewage ejector pit or a basement floor drain with a broken trap

The tell is usually not the flies themselves but a damp patch, a musty smell, or staining on a ceiling below. If you see any of those alongside a fly population that will not clear, stop treating and start looking for water. That is the point where the flies have done you a favor.

What NOT to Do

Do not install a drain cover and call it solved. A mesh cover stops adults emerging into the room. It does not stop the larvae, and it hides the evidence while the film thickens. The population behind the cover gets worse.

Do not pour bleach weekly as maintenance. It suppresses the problem and damages rubber seals and grout over time. It also kills the bacteria that would otherwise break down the film naturally.

Do not spray the walls. The flies on the wall are already at the end of their life. Killing them has no effect on the next generation, and aerosol in a bathroom is a poor trade for the exposure.

Do not use a sweet trap. It catches nothing relevant and, sitting in a warm bathroom, becomes its own breeding site within a few days.

Do not treat one drain and stop. The population is the sum of all wet points in the room. Partial treatment produces a plateau that looks like resistance and is actually an untreated source.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do drain flies look like?

Small — about one-eighth of an inch — fuzzy, and dark grey or black. They hold their wings out at a shallow angle rather than folding them flat, which makes them look like tiny moths. They land abruptly and hop rather than fly smoothly.

Are drain flies harmful?

They do not bite and they do not carry disease in the way mosquitoes do, but they develop in sewage-adjacent organic film and they land on toothbrushes, towels, and counters. The practical concern is contamination and the fact that a sustained population usually signals a moisture problem behind a wall or under a floor.

Why do they come back after I clean the drain?

Because the film regrows from residual material in about ten days, and because cleaning one drain leaves the others. A single clean interrupts one generation. Weekly enzyme treatment for a month, across every drain in the room, is what closes the niche.

Does boiling water kill drain flies?

It kills larvae and eggs in the top inch or two of the drain and it softens the surface film. It does not reach the film on the pipe walls above the water line or the material further down. Used alone, it produces a few quiet days and then a return.

How long does it take to get rid of them?

Two to three weeks with thorough work. Larvae take roughly eight to fifteen days to develop at room temperature, so adults will continue to emerge from sites you cleaned until that cohort is exhausted. Expect the count to fall steadily rather than stop overnight.

Will they go away on their own?

Sometimes, in a bathroom that dries out completely for weeks. In any drain that stays wet and receives soap, the population persists indefinitely at a low level. Low level is not solved — it is a reservoir.

Do I need an exterminator for drain flies?

No. No spray reaches inside a pipe. The treatment is mechanical cleaning plus enzyme maintenance, which is a homeowner task. If a professional is involved, the work they do is the same scrubbing you can do yourself.
