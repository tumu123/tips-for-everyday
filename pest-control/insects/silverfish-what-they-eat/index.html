Silverfish: What They Eat and When It Actually Matters

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 11 min read

A silverfish is a small, wingless, teardrop-shaped insect that moves in a way that is hard to describe without using the word “glide.” It is grey to silver, about half an inch long, with three tail bristles and a habit of appearing in a bathtub at night and vanishing when the light comes on.

Most of what is written about silverfish treats them as a pest to be eliminated. That framing overstates the damage and understates the useful information. A silverfish does not bite, does not carry disease, does not damage structure, and in a dry house will not establish at all. What it does do is live only in conditions that are dark, undisturbed, and humid — which makes it a reasonably reliable indicator that a space has a moisture problem worth finding.

The right response depends on which of those two things you care about. If the concern is the occasional insect in the bathroom, the answer is that it is close to harmless. If the concern is that something in the house is damp enough to support a breeding population, the insect is the least of it.

What Silverfish Actually Eat

Silverfish are starch and polysaccharide feeders. Their diet is unusually broad because their digestive system handles several classes of material that most insects cannot use.

High-starch foods. Flour, cereal, pasta, dry pet food, and bookbinding paste. A silverfish in a pantry is eating the same things a pantry moth would.

Paper and its coatings. Wallpaper, the glue behind wallpaper, book pages, the sizing in paper, envelopes, cardboard boxes, and the adhesive on labels and stamps. This is why archives and libraries treat them as a pest of record rather than a household nuisance.

Natural fibres. Cotton, linen, silk, and rayon. They will feed on the surface of fabric and on sizing left in it. They do not generally eat wool, which is protein rather than carbohydrate, and they do not eat synthetic fibres at all.

Dead skin, hair, and dander. Which is part of why bathrooms and bedrooms are favoured rooms — the food supply renews itself continuously and requires no spill.

Glue and paste generally. Book spines, the back of wallpaper, the adhesive under a floor covering, the paste behind a picture frame.

What this list has in common is that none of it is fresh food. Silverfish are scavengers of dry, stored, starchy material, which is why their damage shows up in the things people keep rather than the things people eat day to day.

The Damage They Do, and the Damage They Do Not

A realistic assessment matters here, because the two failure modes are opposite: panicking over an insect that cannot hurt you, or ignoring an infestation that is quietly destroying something you wanted to keep.

What they do not do:

  • They do not bite or sting.
  • They do not transmit disease. They walk over surfaces, but they are not a recognised vector.
  • They do not damage structure, insulation, or wiring. They are not rodents and they do not gnaw through anything to get in.
  • They do not eat wool, and they do not eat synthetic fabric.
  • They do not spread fast. A silverfish population grows slowly compared with ants or flies.

What they can do:

  • Damage paper. Books, photographs, certificates, letters, sheet music, and cardboard-stored documents. The damage is cosmetic at first — small irregular holes and thinned edges — and cumulative over years.
  • Damage fabric. Linen and cotton stored in a damp basement or a closet against an exterior wall will show irregular holes and thinning along folds.
  • Stain. Their droppings are small dark specks, and moulted skins accumulate in corners.
  • Signal moisture. This is the one that matters most often.

The damage timeline is what makes the “should I care” question answerable. Silverfish live two to eight years, and a female lays roughly 100 eggs over that span, a few at a time. That is a slow population growth curve. A box of photographs in a damp basement for a decade can be meaningfully damaged; a silverfish seen once in a bathroom is not a crisis.

Why Humidity Is the Whole Story

Silverfish cannot thrive in dry air. They need high relative humidity — evidence suggests roughly 70 to 90 percent — and they lose water readily through their cuticle, so a dry environment is functionally lethal to them over time. Preferred temperatures are in the 70 to 85°F range, which is to say normal indoor temperatures.

This single fact reorganises the entire problem. You are not treating a silverfish infestation; you are treating a humidity condition that happens to have silverfish in it. Kill every silverfish in a room and, if the humidity is unchanged, the room remains suitable and the population returns from eggs and from adjoining spaces.

The humid rooms in a house are predictable:

SpaceWhy it stays humidWhat to check first
BathroomShower and bath steam, poor exhaustWhether the exhaust fan actually moves air; run time after showers
BasementGround moisture, poor ventilation, cool surfacesA hygrometer reading; a dehumidifier; whether stored goods sit on the floor

A cheap hygrometer — the kind sold for a nursery or a terrarium — settles the question in a day. Put it in the room where you see the insects and read it at the same time each day for a week. If the room sits above 60 percent relative humidity for much of the day, the silverfish are a symptom and the humidity is the problem.

That also links the insect to a much larger set of problems. Sustained indoor humidity above roughly 60 percent is the condition that supports mould growth on cool surfaces, musty odours in stored textiles, and the gradual deterioration of anything paper or fabric. The diagnostic approach — finding where the moisture is coming from rather than treating the room — is set out in how to find moisture problems early, and it is a more valuable exercise than anything you can do about the insect directly.

A food supply indoors attracts more than the thing eating it. For the arthropod that arrives once the silverfish are established, see the hunter that follows a silverfish colony.

How to Tell Silverfish From Firebrats

There are two closely related insects, and they indicate different conditions. Confusing them sends you after the wrong fix.

FeatureSilverfishFirebrat
ColourSilver-grey, slightly metallicMottled grey-brown, faintly banded
Preferred temperature70 to 85°F90°F and above
Preferred humidityHigh — 70 to 90 percentLower than silverfish; heat matters more
Where foundBathrooms, basements, closets, kitchensNear ovens, water heaters, boilers, furnace rooms, around warm pipes
MovementGliding, stops and startsFaster, more erratic

If you see them near the water heater, the boiler, or the back of the oven, that is the firebrat, and a dehumidifier will not help. The fix is to reduce the food supply and seal the seams around the appliance — high heat is not something you can remove from a working appliance.

If you see them in a bathroom, a basement, or a closet against an outside wall, that is the silverfish, and the humidity diagnosis applies.

What Works: Dry the Room, Starve the Colony, Seal the Gaps

The effective sequence is short, and none of it involves insecticide.

1. Bring the humidity down. This is the only measure that changes the fundamental condition. A dehumidifier sized for the room, run until the hygrometer reads below 50 percent, will make the space unsuitable. In a bathroom, the practical version is mechanical: run the exhaust fan during and for 20 minutes after every shower, and confirm that it is actually moving air — hold a piece of tissue against the grille; if it does not stay, the fan is not working, and the fix may be as simple as cleaning the blade and the grille.

2. Get stored goods off the floor and away from walls. Cardboard boxes directly on a basement floor wick moisture and provide food and shelter in one object. Metal shelving with the bottom shelf at least six inches off the floor, and a gap of a couple of inches between stored items and the wall, is the standard that works. The storage method that keeps contents dry is in how to store things in a basement without ruining them.

3. Remove the food supply. Cardboard boxes are a food source in themselves. Replace them with plastic totes for long-term storage. Remove old newspapers, paper bags, and any box that has been sitting for years. In a closet, anything stored on the floor against an exterior wall should move.

4. Seal the seams they use. Silverfish travel along baseboards, through gaps at pipe penetrations, and under the edge of flooring. Caulk the line where the baseboard meets the floor in a humid room, and seal around pipes under the sink. This is not a barrier that keeps them out of the house — it interrupts movement between the humid space and the dry one, which reduces sightings elsewhere. The baseboard line is also where dust and debris collect, and the order to clean it in is set out in how to clean baseboards fast.

5. Vacuum the corners. Moulted skins and droppings accumulate in the same corners the insects use. A vacuum with a crevice tool, run along the baseboards and into the corners of a closet or bathroom, removes food debris and the insects themselves.

6. Reduce clutter in the humid room. A crowded, undisturbed space is the habitat. The mechanism is simple: fewer objects means fewer places to hide, more airflow, and surfaces you can actually see and clean.

What Does Not Work

Insecticide sprays and foggers. Silverfish live in wall voids, under flooring, and behind baseboards. A surface spray contacts the few that cross it and does not reach the population. The same objection that applies to fogging for ants applies here, plus the residue; the general case for exclusion over spraying is in the DIY pest control method.

Cedar, lavender, and mothball sachets. Cedar oil has some repellent effect in a sealed container, which is why cedar chests work as storage rather than as treatment. In an open room, the effect is negligible. Mothballs are a pesticide with a real toxicity profile and should not be scattered in living space.

Diatomaceous earth in a damp room. Diatomaceous earth works by desiccation and stops working when it absorbs moisture. In the humid spaces where silverfish live, it clogs and becomes inert within days.

Killing the ones you see. Silverfish are nocturnal and hide in crevices; the ones visible on a wall at night are a small fraction. Removing them changes nothing about the conditions.

Dehumidifying without finding the source. A dehumidifier that runs continuously to hold a basement at 55 percent is treating a symptom, and it will run forever. If a dehumidifier needs to run constantly in a space with no obvious water source, the source exists and has not been found.

When to Stop Worrying

Not every silverfish warrants a project. The judgement call is reasonable in three situations.

One insect, one sighting, in a bathroom. This is common and unremarkable — they come up through the drain or the gaps around pipes from a humid void. Increase ventilation, and note whether it recurs.

An old house with a stone or dirt-floored cellar. Silverfish are part of the fauna of an old damp cellar. Unless something is stored there that matters, the response is to store nothing valuable there and accept the insect.

A dry house with an occasional sighting in summer. Humidity fluctuates seasonally. If the hygrometer reads under 50 percent, there is no breeding population, and the insects are coming in from outside or from a nearby void.

The situation that does warrant action is different: repeated sightings plus a hygrometer reading above 60 percent, or damage appearing on stored paper or fabric. At that point the insect has done its job as an indicator and the work is about the moisture.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are silverfish harmful to humans?

No. They do not bite, sting, or transmit disease, and they are not a recognised allergen source. The harm they do is to stored paper and natural fabric, and the useful information they provide about humidity.

What attracts silverfish to a bathroom?

Moisture, dark crevices, and a food supply of dead skin cells and hair. Bathrooms combine all three, and the gaps around pipes and under the floor give them a route in. Running the exhaust fan properly is the single most effective change.

Do silverfish eat clothes?

They eat cotton, linen, silk, and rayon, and they favour fabric that has been stored undisturbed in a damp place. They do not eat wool or synthetics. Damage appears as irregular holes and thinned folds rather than the clean-cut holes associated with clothes moths.

How do I get rid of silverfish permanently?

By removing the humidity that supports them. Lower the room below 50 percent relative humidity, get stored goods off the floor, replace cardboard with plastic totes, seal the baseboard and pipe gaps, and reduce clutter. Insecticide does not achieve a permanent result because the habitat remains.

Are silverfish a sign of a bigger problem?

Often, yes — not structural damage, but excess moisture. A room that supports silverfish is a room where mould can grow and where stored fabric will develop a musty odour. It is worth reading the humidity and finding out why it is high.

How long do silverfish live?

Two to eight years, which is long for an insect of this size. A female lays roughly 100 eggs over her life, a few at a time, so populations build slowly. That slow timeline is also why damage to stored paper accumulates gradually rather than appearing at once.

Do dehumidifiers get rid of silverfish?

They remove the condition the insects need, which is the closest thing to a permanent fix. Expect the population to decline over weeks rather than days, and expect reinvasion if the dehumidifier is switched off and the room returns to its previous humidity.
