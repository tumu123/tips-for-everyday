Carpet Beetles: The Larvae Do the Damage, Not the Beetle

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 12 min read

In late spring, small oval beetles appear on the windowsill, on the curtain, and on the outside of the window screen. They are mottled brown, white, and orange, about the size of a sesame seed, and they are slow enough to catch with a finger. People kill them, feel a small satisfaction, and consider the matter closed.

It is not closed, but the beetle is not the problem either. Adult carpet beetles feed on pollen and nectar. They come indoors to lay eggs near a food source, and the food source is not your carpet in the sense you imagine — it is the protein-rich dust that accumulates along the edges of it. The larvae that hatch are what eat wool, silk, fur, feathers, felt, and the dried protein in a bag of dog food, and they do it out of sight for months.

This is one of the few household pests where the visible bug and the damaging bug are effectively two different animals, and where the treatment has almost nothing to do with killing anything. The general identify-before-you-treat principle is in the DIY pest control method; this is the carpet beetle version of it, and the garment-eating moth that produces similar damage is covered in clothes moths versus pantry moths.

The Adult Is Not the Problem

Three species account for nearly all indoor cases:

  • Varied carpet beetle — the mottled one on the windowsill. The most common indoors, and the larvae eat almost anything of animal origin.
  • Furniture carpet beetle — more uniform in color, similar behavior, often found in upholstered furniture and under rugs.
  • Black carpet beetle — larger and darker, longer larval stage, and the one most often found in stored grain as well as textiles.

The adults live a few weeks, do no fiber damage, and are not a health risk. They are also the reason people believe the problem is superficial. A windowsill full of adults means a female laid eggs somewhere indoors a month ago; the adults you see now are her offspring emerging.

The larvae are the whole problem. They are about a quarter inch long, carrot-shaped, brownish and banded, and covered in bristles. Some species have a tuft of hair at the tail that can cause skin irritation on contact — a small itchy patch that people mistake for a rash of unknown origin.

Step 1: Confirm You Have Larvae, Not Just Adults

Before doing anything, establish whether there is an active breeding population. This changes the entire scope of the work.

Look for three things:

  1. Larvae. Live ones, moving slowly, usually under something.
  2. Shed skins. The empty husks of previous molts. They look like pale bristly grubs, they do not move, and people frequently mistake them for live insects and then over-treat. Each larva sheds several times, so a few larvae produce a lot of husks.
  3. Frass. Fine brownish grit, like coarse sand, in the same places as the skins.

If you have adults on the windowsill and nothing else — no larvae, no husks, no grit — you probably have adults that flew in and found no suitable place to breed. Vacuum the windowsill and the area below it and stop. Treating a house for one beetle is an unnecessary exposure.

If you find larvae or husks, continue. Note where you found them, because that is your inspection axis for the next two months.

Where to look, in order of how often it turns out to be the answer:

Along the edge of a wool or wool-blend rug, under the baseboard overhangDust, hair, and fiber accumulate in a warm, dark, undisturbed line
Under furniture legs and under the sofaThe same dust line, protected from the vacuum

A rash attributed to bites is often neither a bite nor a bug you can spray for. For the identification that depends on physical evidence, see whether the marks are bites or bristles.

Step 2: Inspect the Five Reservoirs Properly

The inspection is the job. Do it with a flashlight and pull things out rather than looking around them.

The carpet edge. Get down and lift the edge of the rug where it meets the wall. Run a finger along the gap between the carpet and the baseboard, then look at what is on your finger. Grey fluff with grit in it is the habitat. Follow the entire perimeter of every carpeted room, not just the visible middle.

Under the furniture. Move every piece. Lift the legs, look at the pad underneath, and check the recess where a leg meets the carpet. A sofa that has not moved in three years is the most common single site in a living room.

The stored textiles. Blankets, out-of-season clothing, a wool rug rolled in a closet, a felt table pad, a feather pillow, a fur or leather item. Open each container and look at the bottom, not the top.

The dry goods. Less obvious but real. Larvae eat grain, nuts, and dry pet food as readily as wool. Check opened bags of flour, rice, cereal, birdseed, and kibble, and check the shelf underneath for grit.

The dead-insect and hair accumulations. Accumulated pet hair behind a door, a wool sweater in a bag with a dead moth in it, a forgotten wool sock under a radiator. Any concentration of animal protein will do.

Photograph what you find and where. You will repeat this exact route in six weeks, and memory is unreliable about which corner had the husks.

Step 3: Steam First, Vacuum Second

The order matters more than the products.

Steam the surfaces where larvae and eggs are — carpet edges, under-furniture areas, the seams of upholstery, the inside of empty drawers. Steam at 200°F and above kills all stages on contact. Move slowly, about an inch per second, and let the head dwell. It also lifts the grime that the larvae are feeding in, which the vacuum can then remove.

Then vacuum with a HEPA machine and a crevice tool, and go over the same surfaces twice — once with the brush to agitate the fiber and lift what is embedded, once with the crevice tool to pull it out. Overlap your passes. Do the edges, the baseboard line, the furniture undersides, the closet floors, and the inside of every drawer and bin.

Two rules about the vacuum:

  • Empty the canister outdoors, into a sealed bag. A canister sitting in a closet with larvae and eggs in it is a breeding container.
  • Use a HEPA filter, or a machine with a sealed bag. A cheap bagless vacuum with no filtration blows the fine dust — the actual food — back into the room air and settles it forty minutes later, sometimes on the same carpet.

Repeat the vacuum every two weeks for two months. Larvae hatch in cycles and one pass leaves the next cohort.

Step 4: Handle the Food Sources Nobody Expects

Larvae do not need wool. They need keratin and chitin — hair, skin cells, feathers, insect remains, and dried animal protein. That is why a house with no wool carpet at all can still have a population.

  • Dry pet food and birdseed. Store in a hard container with a gasket, not the bag. Check the bag for grit before you transfer it.
  • Natural-fiber brushes. A boar-bristle hairbrush, a wool duster, a shaving brush. Rinse and dry them, or replace them.
  • Wool felt pads under furniture and inside a piano. Check the felt; replace if it shows grit or thinning.
  • Down and feather bedding. A comforter with a small tear is a larval habitat. Repair or replace.
  • Silk flower arrangements and dried wreaths. Some contain natural material.
  • The dust itself. After a renovation or a long period without deep cleaning, the accumulated dust along a wall is enough. This is why the vacuum step is not optional and why it needs repeating.
  • Dead insects. Accumulated dead cluster flies or boxelder bugs behind a window frame supply chitin for months.

Step 5: Dry the Space Down

Carpet beetles develop faster and survive better in humid air. In a basement or a crawlspace that sits above 60 percent relative humidity, a population persists no matter how well you clean.

Put a hygrometer in the room. If it reads above 55 percent, run a dehumidifier and find the moisture source — a downspout discharging against the foundation, a failed vapor barrier, a dryer venting indoors. The storage conditions that make a basement safe for textiles in the first place are set out in basement storage without ruining your things.

Dry air does two things: it slows the larval cycle and it prevents the mold that would otherwise become the next problem after you have removed the insects.

Step 6: Re-Run the Same Inspection in Six Weeks

Six weeks is roughly one larval cycle at room temperature. Go back along the same route, in the same order, with the flashlight. Same corners, same furniture lifted, same bins opened.

The result is a binary:

  • No larvae, no fresh husks, no grit. You are done. Repeat the crevice vacuum at each season change, and keep the wool sealed.
  • Fresh larvae or new grit. A reservoir is still active and it is somewhere outside the route you covered — an attic, a crawlspace, a closet in another room, a bag of birdseed in the garage, a wool rug under a bed you did not move.

Do not escalate to insecticide at that point. Escalate the search instead. A spray reaches the surface of a carpet and does nothing to the dust beneath a furniture leg, which is where the colony actually is.

Why Spraying Usually Fails

Three structural reasons.

The target is inaccessible. Larvae live in the dust layer beneath and behind objects. A surface spray over a carpet wets the tips of the pile. The larvae are at the base of the pile and under the pad.

The food supply is the real driver. Killing the larvae you can reach leaves the dust. The next female lays on the same surface, and the population returns within a cycle. Removing the dust removes the reason for the population.

The adults fly. Adults on the windowsill are not controllable by indoor spraying, and they arrive from outside in spring regardless of what you do inside. The window of control is eggs and larvae, not adults.

There are residual products labeled for carpet beetles, and a professional may apply them to the perimeter of a room. They are not the treatment; they are a supplement to cleaning, and on their own they do not resolve the problem.

When You Do Not Need to Do Anything

Not every sighting justifies a deep clean.

  • Adults on the outside of a window, in spring. Normal. They are feeding on the blossoms of whatever is flowering nearby.
  • One adult inside, no larvae, no husks anywhere. A stray. Vacuum it up.
  • A single husk in a drawer that holds only cotton. Probably a wandering larva that did not establish. Clean the drawer, keep an eye on it.
  • Larvae in a bag of flour you are going to throw out anyway. Fix the storage, not the house.

The threshold for real work is larvae or repeated finds in the same place.

What NOT to Do

Do not fog the house. A fogger kills exposed adults and nothing else, and it leaves a residue on every surface in the kitchen for a problem that lives under the furniture.

Do not spray clothing or bedding. Residues against skin, for an insect you can wash and freeze out of textiles.

Do not skip the vacuum because you steamed. Steam kills what it touches; the vacuum removes the habitat. They do different jobs and you need both.

Do not treat a house because of window adults alone. Establish larvae or husks first.

Do not assume a hole in wool means moths. Carpet beetle damage has no webbing or cases; look for grit and husks. Treating for the wrong insect is a month lost.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are carpet beetles harmful to humans?

The adults are harmless and do not bite or sting. The larvae can cause a contact irritation — an itchy patch where the bristles touch skin — but they do not burrow in and do not transmit disease. The real cost is damage to textiles and stored food.

What do carpet beetle larvae look like?

About a quarter inch long, carrot-shaped, brown with lighter bands, covered in bristles. Some species have a tail tuft of hairs. They move slowly and curl up when disturbed. The shed skins are the same shape, pale and hollow, and are often mistaken for live insects.

How do carpet beetles get in?

They fly in through open windows and doors in spring, and they arrive on cut flowers, on secondhand furniture, and in dry pet food or birdseed. Adults are strong enough fliers to enter from a nearby nest or a bird nest under an eave.

Do carpet beetles bite?

No. There is no biting stage. An itchy patch after handling stored wool is bristle irritation from a larva, not a bite.

Are they the same as clothes moths?

No. Both eat protein fiber and both produce holes, but clothes moths leave webbing and silken cases while carpet beetles leave bristly husks and sand-like grit. Carpet beetles also eat dry food, hair, and feather fillings that moths rarely touch.

How long does it take to get rid of them?

One full larval cycle — about six to twelve weeks depending on temperature — with cleaning repeated every two weeks. A single cleaning interrupts one generation; the population is only closed out when a six-week follow-up inspection finds no fresh husks or grit.

Does one beetle mean an infestation?

No. A single adult on a windowsill is common in spring and does not imply breeding indoors. Look for larvae, shed skins, or grit before treating. The presence of husks is a much stronger signal than the presence of an adult.
