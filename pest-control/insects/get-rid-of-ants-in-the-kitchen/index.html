How to Get Rid of Ants in the Kitchen Without Fogging the House

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 13 min read

The ants on your counter are a small fraction of the colony, and they are not the part you need to kill. What you see is the foraging workforce — a few percent of the total — sent out to find food and report back. Killing them with a spray removes the messengers and leaves the nest, the queen, and the brood entirely intact.

Worse, the spray is usually a repellent rather than a contact killer. It lays down a chemical barrier that the ants detect and avoid, which does not eliminate the colony but does split its traffic. Many of the species that invade kitchens respond to a disrupted trail by budding: a portion of the workers and a queen leave and start a second nest. You started with one colony and finished with three.

The method that actually works is slower and less satisfying to watch, which is why it is underused. You put out a bait the ants carry back themselves, at a concentration that kills them after they have delivered it.

Why Spraying Makes Ants Worse

There are two distinct mechanisms, and both are well documented.

Repellency. Most consumer ant sprays use pyrethroids — permethrin, bifenthrin, deltamethrin — which are repellent at the concentrations applied. The ants do not walk through the treated band. They reroute, often into the cabinets or along a new path, and the nest continues.

Budding. In species with multiple queens per nest — Argentine ants and pharaoh ants are the classic examples — a disturbance triggers colony fission. A group of workers carrying brood and a queen walks away and establishes a satellite nest. Each nest is then smaller but the total population is unchanged or larger, and the new nests are in less accessible places. This is the mechanism behind the familiar experience of spraying a kitchen and finding ants in a bedroom two weeks later.

There is a third, practical problem: a spray applied to a countertop or inside a cabinet leaves a residue on surfaces where food is prepared. That is a cost with no benefit, because the treatment was not reaching the colony anyway. Warm weather is when the traffic starts and stops, so the seasonal side of the same work — excluding rather than killing — is in how to keep bugs out of the house in summer.

Repellency is the same trap with ants, and it fails the same way. For the species that splits its colony when sprayed, and the bait that reaches the cavity, see why spraying scatters cockroaches.

Step 1: Identify the Species Before You Buy Anything

This step is not academic. In ants, species determines diet preference, and diet preference determines which bait works. Getting this wrong is the single most common reason people conclude that “bait doesn’t work.”

For most kitchen invasions the field narrows to pharaoh, Argentine, and odorous house ants, all of which are sweet-feeders for at least part of the year. The species that changes your plan is the carpenter ant, and it changes it completely — see the section below on when baiting will not work.

A phone photo taken through a magnifying glass is enough to distinguish these, mostly on colour and size. Count the ants in a 10-second window along the trail and note it down; you will want the same number to compare against in a week.

Step 2: Match the Bait to What They Are Eating This Week

Ant colonies change their nutritional target over the season. In spring and early summer, when the brood is growing, they want protein. Later, when the colony is storing energy for winter, they want sugar and fat. This is why a bait that worked in June can fail in September, and why the advice “just use sugar water” is unreliable.

The efficient approach is a bait buffet: place three or four small offerings at once, a foot or so apart, and see which one the ants recruit to. Whichever they swarm is the one you scale up.

The three base baits:

  • Sweet. A teaspoon of jam, honey, or a sugar syrup (1 part sugar to 4 parts water by volume) with boric acid added.
  • Protein. Peanut butter thinned with a little oil, with boric acid added.
  • Fat. Cooking oil or a dab of shortening, with boric acid added.

The concentration is the part that decides success, and it is counterintuitive. Boric acid works as a stomach poison that also disrupts the gut lining, and it needs to be delivered at a low dose — roughly 0.5 to 2 percent by weight of the finished bait.

Above about 2 percent, the workers die before they get home. They pick up the bait, walk a few inches, and stop. The colony never receives the dose, the queen keeps laying, and you have simply poisoned a few foragers. This is why strong homemade recipes — and some are 5 percent or higher — fail while a weaker one succeeds.

A practical way to hit the range without a scale: 1 level teaspoon of boric acid powder to 1/4 cup (4 tablespoons) of bait base gives roughly 1 to 1.5 percent. Mix it thoroughly. Boric acid is not instantly toxic to humans at these amounts, but it is not a food ingredient either — label the containers and keep them away from children and pets.

Why boric acid and not something faster. A fast-acting poison produces exactly the failure described above: dead ants on the trail, no delivery. A slow-acting one lets the foragers return, share the food through trophallaxis — mouth-to-mouth transfer — and feed the brood and the queen. The colony dies from the inside over one to three weeks.

Step 3: Place the Bait on the Trail, Not at the Nest

It sounds backwards to avoid the nest, but there is a reason. A bait placed directly over an entry hole is mostly ignored, because ants leaving the nest are not yet in foraging mode. A bait placed along the active trail is encountered by ants already carrying food back, and those are the ones that will pick it up.

Place small amounts — a smear on a piece of masking tape, or a bottle cap with a shallow pool — directly on the trail, every few feet. Small amounts matter: a large pool is a hazard for pets and children, and the ants will recruit to it so heavily that it can be exhausted before enough has been carried back.

Masking tape as a substrate has two advantages: it can be lifted and moved if the trail shifts, and the bait does not soak into a porous surface.

Do not place bait where it will be walked through. A bait station — a small plastic container with entry holes — is the right choice in a household with pets, toddlers, or a crawling baby.

Step 4: Do Not Clean the Trail

This is the hardest instruction to follow, because the instinct is to wipe up the ants and the line they are walking on.

The trail is a pheromone path laid by the scouts. It is also your delivery system: it tells the foragers where the bait is. Wipe it and you destroy the route, which is the same as destroying the treatment.

For the two weeks of the baiting period, leave the trail alone. Do not spray it, do not wipe it, do not use a vinegar solution on it. Wipe counters around the bait, and clean up crumbs and grease elsewhere — the pantry is the other half of this, and the layout that removes the standing invitation is in how to organize a pantry — but the strip of counter with the trail stays as it is.

If you must clean for guests, use plain water on a cloth and avoid the trail line. A single wiped trail can set the process back several days.

Step 5: Follow the Line Back to the Entry

While the bait is working, trace the trail backwards. This is the step that prevents a repeat next season.

Ants enter through gaps that are much smaller than they appear. A 1/16-inch gap — the thickness of a credit card — is enough for most kitchen species. The usual entry points:

  • The gap where a countertop meets the wall or backsplash
  • Around pipes entering the wall under the sink
  • The edge of a window frame, especially above a sink
  • Behind a dishwasher or under a fridge, where the gap is never sealed
  • Along the baseboard, at the corner where two walls meet
  • Through the exterior wall at a hose bib or an outdoor outlet
  • Under a sliding door, through the track

Mark each one. Do not seal them yet — sealing during active baiting can trap ants inside and cause them to find a new route. Seal after the trail has been dead for a week.

When you do seal, use caulk for narrow gaps, and copper mesh plus caulk for gaps wide enough to admit a finger. The same materials and the same logic apply to the whole building envelope; the general method is in the DIY pest control guide, and the exterior wall is where most of the work sits.

Step 6: Wait Two Weeks Before Judging

Baiting is not fast, and the timeline is predictable if unpleasant to watch.

DaysWhat you will see
1 to 2More ants than before, clustered on the bait. This is success, not failure — recruitment is working
3 to 7Activity on the bait stays high or rises; some dead ants appear near the nest
7 to 14Bait visits fall off sharply; trail thins
14 to 21No ants on the trail. This is the end point

A well-placed bait at the right concentration typically collapses a kitchen colony in two to three weeks. If the population is unchanged after two weeks of steady bait feeding, the diagnosis was wrong — usually the species is a protein-feeder and the bait was sweet, or the concentration is too high.

Refresh the bait every two to three days. It dries out, and dried bait is not eaten. If the ants stop visiting a bait that they previously swarmed, they have switched nutritional targets; put out the other type.

When Baiting Will Not Work

Two situations require a different response.

Carpenter ants. These nest in damp or damaged wood and are not managed with kitchen baits. Baiting may kill foragers without touching the nest. A carpenter ant sighting indoors is a signal to find moisture — a leaking roof, a wet sill, a condensation problem — because the ants follow water damage rather than causing it. The relevant diagnostics are in how to find moisture problems early, and the ant problem usually resolves when the moisture does.

Ants nesting indoors. Pharaoh ants in particular will nest inside wall voids, behind baseboards, and in appliance housings in heated buildings. Baiting still works, but it takes longer because there are multiple nest sites, and every one of them needs to be fed.

What NOT to Do

Do not spray a trail. It is repellent, it splits colonies of the species most likely to invade a kitchen, and it leaves residue where food is prepared.

Do not use a fogger. It does not penetrate wall voids or cabinets, and it deposits insecticide across every surface in the room.

Do not make the bait stronger to “finish it faster”. Above about 2 percent boric acid, the foragers die before delivering the dose and the colony survives.

Do not wipe the trail during treatment. The pheromone path is the delivery route.

Do not seal entries while ants are still active. You trap them inside and they find another way out.

Do not bait and spray at the same time. The spray makes the ants avoid the area, including the bait.

Do not judge the result after three days. The visible increase in activity in the first 48 hours is the treatment working.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do the ants come back every year?

Because the colony was never eliminated — usually only the foragers were killed, or the entry points were left open. A sealed building and a bait-based treatment remove both the population and the access. If ants return at the same spot each spring, that spot is an entry, not a nest.

Is boric acid safe around pets and children?

At the concentrations used in bait — under 2 percent — it is low in acute toxicity, but it is still a pesticide and should not be accessible. Use closed bait stations in households with pets or small children, and place them where a child cannot reach. Do not use it on a surface where food is prepared.

How long does it take for ant bait to work?

Two to three weeks for a typical kitchen colony. Activity increases for the first few days, then falls off between days 7 and 14. If nothing has changed at two weeks, the bait type or the concentration is wrong.

Why are ants ignoring my bait?

Three usual reasons: the bait type does not match what the colony wants this week, the boric acid concentration is too high so foragers die before recruiting others, or a recent spray has made them avoid the area. Reset by offering three bait types side by side and leaving the trail undisturbed.

Do cucumber peels, cinnamon, or vinegar keep ants away?

They may disrupt a trail briefly, but they do not kill the colony, and disrupting the trail is the mechanism that causes budding. As a barrier in a cupboard they are harmless; as a treatment they are not effective.

How small a gap do ants need?

About 1/16 inch, roughly the thickness of a credit card, for the small species that invade kitchens. Sealing needs to be at that scale — a visual check for "big gaps" misses most of the entry points.

What if I live in an apartment and the ants come from a neighbour?

Baiting still works inside your unit, but reinvasion from an untreated neighbouring unit is likely. Report it to the building manager, and keep entry points sealed. In multi-unit buildings, pharaoh ant infestations are a building-level problem, not a unit-level one.
