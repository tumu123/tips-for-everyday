Should You Kill the Spiders in Your Basement?

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 11 min read

A basement spider is a predator with a fixed territory, no interest in your food, and a diet consisting entirely of the insects you would otherwise have. That is an unusual combination in a household pest: it costs nothing, it does not reproduce in your pantry, and removing it tends to make the situation worse rather than better.

So the honest answer to “should I kill them” is usually no — but “usually” is doing real work in that sentence. In North America there are two species that change the calculation completely, and the decision should be made on identification rather than on discomfort. What follows is the identification, then the four cases that cover almost every basement.

What Is Actually in Your Basement

The spiders found in basements are a small, predictable group. Knowing which one you have tells you most of what you need.

The first four are the ones you will almost always find. The last two are the ones that matter, and both are far less common indoors than the volume of fear around them suggests.

The Two That Change the Answer

The brown recluse. Its range in the United States is specific and worth knowing, because outside that range the identification question mostly resolves itself: it is established in the south-central states, roughly from Kansas and Missouri south through Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Texas, and east into parts of Mississippi, Alabama, and Tennessee. If you live well outside that band, a brown spider in your basement is overwhelmingly likely to be one of a dozen harmless species.

Within the range, the identifying features are precise: a uniformly pale brown body with no mottled pattern on the abdomen, a violin-shaped darker marking on the head region with the neck of the violin pointing backwards, and — the feature that settles it — six eyes arranged in three pairs, rather than the eight eyes in two rows that most spiders have. That last check requires a flashlight and a close look, which is why “I saw a violin shape” is an unreliable identification. Many harmless spiders have a dark line down the head region.

The black widow. Glossy black, globular abdomen, and a red or orange hourglass on the underside. It builds a coarse, strong web in dark corners near the floor — under a workbench, behind stored boxes, in a floor drain recess. It does not seek out people and bites when compressed against skin, which is why the practical risk is reached into a corner without looking.

Both are treated the same way at the decision level: do not handle them, do not reach blindly into corners, and treat an established indoor population as a reason for professional help rather than a project.

Why Killing Spiders Usually Makes the Bug Problem Worse

A basement with spiders has spiders because it has something for them to eat. Silverfish, camel crickets, pillbugs, centipedes, and the occasional fly are the food base. Remove the food and the spider population falls on its own; kill the spiders and the prey population — the one you actually notice and dislike — increases.

There is a second effect that is specific to how spiders work. They are not insects, and the pyrethroid insecticides sold for household use are less effective against them than the label implies: a spider’s body has a small contact area relative to its size, and many species groom their legs after contact with a residue. A surface spray in a basement may reduce the visible population briefly and will not clear the space.

The third effect is the reason not to spray at all if you can avoid it. A basement is a closed, poorly ventilated space. Insecticide applied there persists in air and on surfaces and is being used against predators that were providing a service.

Predators and prey do not rise and fall together. For the insect that is purely a moisture reading, see why removing one pest raises another.

The Decision: Four Cases

Case 1: Cellar spiders and cobwebs in the corners, no other insects, no bites. Leave them. Knock down the webs that are in the way when you clean, and accept the rest. This is the majority of basements.

Case 2: Many spiders of several types, and you also see silverfish, crickets, or pillbugs. The spiders are a symptom. Work on the moisture and the entry points, and the prey population drops; the spiders follow. Fixing the humidity also addresses the conditions that support silverfish, which are usually part of the same food base — what silverfish eat and why humidity decides it explains the mechanism.

Case 3: Wolf spiders or large ground spiders coming in from outside. These are almost always entering at floor level, through gaps at the sill, the garage door, or the foundation. Sealing the building envelope is the fix, and it is the same work that keeps mice out — the general method is in the DIY pest control guide.

Case 4: A spider you believe is a brown recluse or a black widow, inside the house. Stop handling things in that area with bare hands. Photograph it, keep the photograph, and arrange professional identification and treatment. Do not apply a general spray and assume the problem is solved.

What to Do Instead of Spraying

The practical work reduces both the spiders and the reason they are there, and none of it is a pesticide.

Reduce the humidity. A damp basement supports the insects spiders eat. A dehumidifier holding the space under 50 percent relative humidity reduces silverfish, woodlice, and centipedes at the source. How to trace elevated humidity to its cause is in how to find moisture problems early.

Get stored goods off the floor and away from walls. Cardboard boxes stacked against a foundation wall create exactly the dark, humid, undisturbed galleries that both spiders and their prey use. Metal shelving with clearance from the wall, and rigid totes rather than cardboard, is the storage standard that breaks the habitat — the specifics are in how to store things in a basement without ruining them.

Remove the webs you can reach, on a schedule. A vacuum with a crevice tool, run along the ceiling line and the corners once a month, removes webs and egg sacs. This is mechanical control, and it is more effective against cellar spiders than any spray.

Seal the floor-level gaps. Door sweeps on the basement door and the garage door, caulk at the sill plate, and mesh over foundation vents. Most ground spiders and virtually all the prey species come in at this level.

Reduce clutter. A basement with clear floor and organised shelving has dramatically fewer spiders than one with stored clutter, because there are fewer places to build and fewer prey habitats. This is not a tidiness argument; it is a habitat argument.

Check your shoes and stored items if you are in recluse territory. Shake out boots, gloves, and anything stored in a dark corner before putting a hand or foot in it. This single habit accounts for most of the reduction in bite risk that individuals can achieve.

The same reasoning applies to another hunter in the same room. For the fifteen-legged one that eats roaches and silverfish, see the argument against spraying a predator.

How to Identify a Brown Recluse Without Guessing

Because identification drives the decision, the process matters more than the conclusion.

  1. Check whether you are in the range. South-central United States, as described above. Outside it, the probability drops sharply.
  2. Look at the abdomen. A brown recluse has a uniformly coloured abdomen with no mottling, no spots, and no bands. Most similar-looking spiders are patterned.
  3. Look at the eyes with a light. Six eyes in three pairs, arranged in a semicircle. This is the definitive check and requires the spider to be still — a clear container and a flashlight is the safe way.
  4. Look at the legs. Uniformly coloured and without bands or spines, which distinguishes it from wolf spiders and most house spiders.
  5. Note the behaviour. Brown recluses avoid light and vibration, do not build a tidy orb web, and are usually found in undisturbed storage rather than in the open.

A photograph taken from directly above, in good light, with the spider in a clear container, is enough for someone qualified to identify it. Keep the spider if you can do so safely; several extension services will identify a specimen.

When to Call Someone

Professional help is warranted in three situations: a confirmed brown recluse or black widow inside the living space; a bite that occurred indoors and the spider was not identified; and a persistent population that has not responded to moisture control and exclusion. The last one is uncommon — most basement spider populations respond to the habitat work within a couple of months.

For everything else, the cost of a service contract is being spent on a problem that a dehumidifier and a shelving unit address.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are basement spiders dangerous?

The great majority are not. Cellar spiders, house spiders, wolf spiders, and jumping spiders are harmless, and their fangs either cannot pierce human skin or will not do so unless the spider is trapped against it. Two species — the brown recluse and the black widow — are medically significant, and both are far less common indoors than people assume.

Do spiders eat other pests?

Yes, and that is the main argument for leaving them. Cellar spiders and house spiders eat silverfish, flies, mosquitoes, and other small insects. Removing the spiders removes a predator that was working for free, and the prey population typically rises.

Will spraying for spiders work?

Partly, and not for long. Pyrethroid residues are less effective against spiders than against insects, because of the small contact area and grooming behaviour, and spraying does nothing about the food supply or the humidity that brought them in. In a poorly ventilated basement it also leaves persistent residue in the air.

How do I tell a cellar spider from a brown recluse?

A cellar spider has a tiny body with legs several times its body length and hangs upside down in a tangle web near the ceiling. A brown recluse has a body about 1/4 to 1/2 inch long, legs in proportion, a uniformly pale brown abdomen, and six eyes. The leg-to-body ratio settles it at a glance.

Why do I suddenly have a lot of spiders in the basement?

Usually because the prey population increased — a wet season, a humidity problem, or an opening that let crickets or pillbugs in. The spider population follows the food with a lag of a few weeks. Treat it as an indicator and look for what they are eating.

Do dehumidifiers get rid of basement spiders?

They reduce the prey base, which reduces the spiders over a couple of months. They do not remove spiders directly. Combined with clearing cardboard storage and sealing floor-level gaps, the effect is substantial.

Should I kill a spider I find in my bedroom?

Catch it and put it outside if it bothers you; there is no pest-management reason to kill it. The exception is a spider you suspect is a recluse or a widow, which should be treated with caution and identified rather than handled.
