Clothes Moths or Pantry Moths? Identify by Where the Damage Is

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 12 min read

A small pale moth lifts off the closet shelf and the first instinct is to check the flour. That instinct is wrong about half the time, and acting on it wastes a month.

There are two moths that show up inside a house, and they are separated by what they eat rather than by what they look like. Pantry moths — the Indian meal moth and its relatives — breed in grain, nuts, dried fruit, and pet food. Clothes moths — the webbing clothes moth and the case-bearing clothes moth — breed in keratin: wool, cashmere, silk, fur, feathers, and the protein fraction of dust and hair. A pantry moth in a closet is a stray adult that wandered. A clothes moth in a kitchen is the same. The breeding site is in only one of those rooms, and finding which one is the entire diagnostic.

Sorting the two small flies in a kitchen is covered in pantry moths versus fruit flies. This guide takes the closet side, and the general identify-then-exclude sequence is in the DIY pest control method.

The Damage Is the Diagnosis

You usually do not see the moth that caused the problem. You see the hole, weeks later. The hole itself tells you which species you have.

Clothes moth damage appears as irregular holes in a single layer of a wool garment, often along a fold, a seam, or the underside of a collar where the fabric rests against itself. Look closely at the edges and you may find a silken tube, a webbing mat, or a grain of sand-like frass. Case-bearing larvae carry a small flat case made of fiber snippets and drag it with them; the case looks like a fleck of lint that moves.

Pantry moth damage appears in food. You find silk webbing across the surface of flour or cereal, clumped grain, and larvae on the underside of a jar lid. The moths themselves congregate on ceilings and near windows in the evening, and they are strong fliers that cover a room.

That third row matters. Carpet beetles produce the same kind of fiber damage as clothes moths, and spraying for moths does nothing to them. If the damage is in carpet rather than garments, start with the carpet beetle guide instead.

Why Mothballs and Cedar Do Not Solve It

Both are worth understanding before you spend money.

Mothballs contain paradichlorobenzene or naphthalene, which are fumigants. They work only in a sealed, saturated atmosphere — a closed container with the garment and enough vapor to reach a lethal concentration. In an open closet, the vapor disperses and the concentration stays far below the effective level. The balls also do nothing to larvae already feeding in a sweater, and the odor permeates everything nearby. If you use them at all, use them in a sealed bin, never loose on a shelf, and never in a space where a child or a pet can reach them.

Cedar repels some adults at close range when the oil is fresh. It does not kill eggs or larvae, and the repellent effect fades as the wood ages and the surface oils evaporate — roughly a year for a thin cedar plank. A cedar closet that has been in place for a decade is furniture, not protection.

Neither product addresses the actual problem, which is that a female found an unsealed wool surface and laid eggs on it. Exclusion does.

Step 1: Establish Whether You Have a Breeding Population

One moth is not an infestation. Moths that come in on a shopping bag or fly through a window in the evening do not establish indoors without a suitable substrate.

Set two or three sticky pheromone traps — the paper kind that fold into a triangle — in the closet and one in the room. Check them weekly. Pheromone traps attract males only, so they cannot end an infestation on their own, but they are an accurate census. A trap that catches nothing over three weeks means you do not have a breeding population in that space and you can stop.

One caveat: the traps are species-specific. A webbing clothes moth trap will not catch a case-bearing moth or a pantry moth. If you are unsure which you have, buy one of each and let the trap tell you.

Step 2: Empty the Closet Completely

Not the floor, not the shelf — everything. A clothes moth population lives in the places you do not look: the folded garment at the back, the sweater in the bag you have not opened since last winter, the wool blanket in the bottom of the linen closet, the felt hat box.

Take every item out and sort it into three piles on a white sheet in good light:

  • Damaged. Visible holes, webbing, cases, or frass. These are the breeding sites.
  • Undamaged protein fiber. Wool, cashmere, silk, fur, feathers, felt, leather trim. These are at risk and need treatment or protection.
  • Everything else. Cotton, polyester, linen, and blends with low wool content are not a food source. They may harbor larvae wandering between meals, so they still get vacuumed and washed, but they are not the reason the population persists.

Inspect each protein-fiber item against the light, along seams and under collars. Hold it over the white sheet and brush it gently; frass and broken fiber fall out and show up clearly. Pay particular attention to items stored at the back and to anything that has not been worn in a year — those are where a population establishes undisturbed.

Step 3: Treat the Damaged Items Before Anything Else

The pile of damaged items is the source. It leaves the house or it gets treated. There is no middle option where it goes back in the closet.

  • Badly damaged, unwearable. Bag it and put it out. Do not donate infested wool; you would be moving the problem.
  • Wearable with small damage. Wash it, then mend it, then store it sealed. Washing is the treatment; the mending is cosmetic.
  • Dry-clean only. Dry cleaning kills eggs and larvae and is the appropriate route for tailoring, coats, and lined garments.

Washing details matter. Hot water at 120°F or above for thirty minutes kills eggs and larvae. Many wool garments cannot take that, so the alternative is a warm wash at the temperature the care label allows plus a full cycle in a hot dryer — twenty minutes at medium-high is enough to kill all stages, and it is the dryer heat rather than the water that does the work. For garments that can do neither, freezing works: seal the item in a bag, freeze at 0°F for a week, remove and let it come to room temperature, then freeze again for a week. Two cycles catch eggs that survived the first. The washing methods themselves are set out in how to wash wool and silk.

Dry cleaning fluid and heat both kill; what does not kill is airing something on a line for an afternoon.

Step 4: Vacuum the Closet Like a Surface, Not a Floor

Larvae do not need a garment to survive. They eat the protein in dust — skin scales, hair, pet dander, wool fiber — and that dust collects in specific places.

Use the crevice tool along:

  • The junction of the shelf and the wall, all the way around
  • The back corners of each shelf
  • The closet floor perimeter and the corner where the two walls meet
  • The top of the closet, above the shelf and below the header
  • The track of any sliding door
  • Under and behind the dresser or the shoe rack
  • The hem of any wool rug in the room

Then vacuum the room’s carpet edges, the baseboard line, and under the bed. Empty the vacuum canister into an outdoor bin immediately — a canister holding larvae and eggs is a breeding container sitting in your house.

The dust under a furniture leg is the single most common reservoir in a room where the closet looks clean. Pull the furniture out rather than vacuuming around it.

Step 5: Store So That the Next Female Cannot Land

The population is gone once you have treated the damaged items and removed the dust. Keeping it gone is a storage problem with one rule: a female clothes moth must not be able to reach a wool surface.

  • Sealed hard-sided bins with gasketed lids for blankets, out-of-season knitwear, and anything wool. A bin with a snap lid and no gasket is not sealed; the gap is enough for a moth to enter.
  • Zippered garment bags for coats and suits. The zipper must close completely; a bag left open at the top is decoration.
  • Not cardboard boxes. Corrugated board has gaps at the seams, absorbs moisture, and is itself a paper source for other pests.
  • Not vacuum bags or dry-cleaner bags for long-term storage. They trap moisture against the fiber.

Keep the relative humidity in the closet below 50 percent if you can. Clothes moths develop poorly in dry air, and the same condition prevents the mold that would be the next problem anyway.

Storing a wool sweater clean is part of the rule. Body oils and food residues on a worn-but-not-washed garment are an attractant, and the fold lines where soil collects are exactly where holes appear. A closet organization pass that keeps wool visible and separated is described in how to organize a closet, and a basement that stays dry enough for textile storage is covered in basement storage without ruining your things.

Step 6: Confirm With Traps Over Eight Weeks

Two months, one trap in the closet, checked weekly. The count should go to zero and stay at zero through a full season change.

Do not stop at four quiet weeks. Clothes moths have a slow cycle — roughly two months from egg to adult at 70°F, and longer in a cool closet — so a cohort that was in the egg stage when you cleaned will produce adults well after you think you are finished. A trap that catches one moth in week seven is a signal to do the crevice vacuum again, not to panic.

If the traps keep catching after two full passes, look outside the closet. Wool rugs, a felt piano pad, stored wool batting, a feather pillow, an old fur coat in the attic, and a wool carpet under a heavy piece of furniture are all common reservoirs in a house where the closet has been cleared twice.

What NOT to Do

Do not spray insecticide on clothing. Residues on garments that touch skin are a poor trade for a problem that washing solves, and no spray reaches larvae inside a folded sweater.

Do not rely on mothballs in an open closet. At open-closet concentrations they do not kill, and the odor is permanent in wool.

Do not skip the item at the back. The population is in the garment you have not touched in a year. Cleaning everything except that one is the most common reason for a repeat.

Do not assume a hole means moths. Carpet beetles, a snag, a zipper, and normal wear all produce holes. Confirm with webbing, cases, or frass before treating.

Do not put an infested item in the freezer without sealing it first. Moisture condenses on the fiber and you trade an insect problem for a mildew one.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between clothes moths and pantry moths?

Diet. Clothes moths breed in wool, silk, fur, feathers, and protein-rich dust; pantry moths breed in grain, nuts, dried fruit, and pet food. They look similar — both small and pale — so the reliable test is where the damage is, not what the adult looks like.

Do clothes moths fly?

Weakly. The adults flutter rather than fly, prefer to run or hop, and are most active in dim light. They are rarely seen in flight across a room, which is one reason an infestation can go unnoticed for months. Pantry moths, by contrast, fly strongly and gather on ceilings.

Will cedar or lavender sachets get rid of them?

No. Cedar oil and lavender repel some adults at very close range while fresh, but neither kills eggs or larvae, and the effect fades as the wood or sachet dries out. Sealed storage does what these products are marketed to do.

How do moths get into a sealed closet?

They do not, once it is sealed. They arrive on newly purchased wool, on secondhand clothing, on a wool blanket brought down from storage, or through a window in the evening. The source is usually an item that came in recently.

Do I have to throw out everything?

Only items with active larvae, webbing, cases, or frass. Undamaged protein fiber can be washed or frozen and returned to sealed storage. Cotton, linen, and synthetics are not a food source and do not need to be discarded.

How long do clothes moths live?

Adults live roughly one to two weeks and do not feed. The damage is done entirely by the larval stage, which lasts about two months at room temperature. That long larval period is why treatment must include the dust and the hidden items, not just the garment you found.

Are moth holes always from moths?

No. Carpet beetle larvae, normal abrasion at folds, zipper snags, and rodent gnawing all produce holes. Moth damage is distinguished by clean-edged irregular holes plus webbing, silken cases, or sand-like frass nearby.
