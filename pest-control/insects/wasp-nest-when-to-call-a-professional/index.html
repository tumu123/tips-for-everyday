A Wasp Nest in Autumn: Which Ones to Leave Alone

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 12 min read

There is a grey paper nest the size of a fist under the porch eave, and a steady trickle of wasps going in and out. The instinct is to deal with it now, before it gets bigger.

The instinct is right and the timing is wrong. In September a wasp colony is at its yearly peak — the largest number of workers, the most aggressive they will ever be, and the least to lose. Through the summer the workers were provisioning larvae; by autumn the queen has stopped laying, the brood is gone, and the workers are foraging for themselves on sugar and fallen fruit. They have no nest-repair duties left and they are hungry, which is a combination that produces the most stings of the year.

What follows is how to identify which of four common species you have, which nests you can reasonably handle, and the three situations where the correct answer is to call someone. This is a case where the general exclusion-first principle in the DIY pest control method runs up against a hard limit: no amount of preparation makes a nest inside a wall safe to treat from the outside.

Why Autumn Is the Season That Stings

Three things converge.

The colony is as large as it will get. A yellowjacket colony that started from a single queen in April can hold several thousand workers by September. More workers means more defenders when the nest is disturbed.

The workers have nothing to do but eat. With no brood to feed, they switch to sugars and proteins — fallen apples, open cans, the bin, pet food left outdoors. They are also more willing to investigate people, because people carry food.

They are near the end of their lives. Workers do not survive the winter. Only newly mated queens do. A worker in October has nothing to conserve, and defensive behavior reflects that.

The colony collapses on its own after the first hard frost. That single fact governs most of the decisions below.

Identify the Nest Before You Decide Anything

Four species account for nearly all residential encounters, and two of them are much less dangerous than their reputation.

Paper wasps. The umbrella-shaped nest with open hexagonal cells, hanging from an eave, a porch ceiling, a shutter, or a branch. Slender body, long legs, brown or reddish with yellow markings. They are not aggressive: they will watch you from the nest and will sting if you touch it, but they do not pursue. A single wasp hovering near a porch light is usually a paper wasp.

Yellowjackets. Compact, bold yellow and black banding, short-waisted. The nest is enclosed in paper and usually hidden — underground in an old rodent burrow, inside a wall cavity, in a soffit, under a deck, in a compost bin. This is the species that ruins picnics, and it is the aggressive one. If you see wasps entering and leaving a hole in the ground or a gap in siding, that is a yellowjacket nest, and it is not a DIY job.

Bald-faced hornets. Actually a yellowjacket relative, not a true hornet. Black with a white face and white markings on the last segments. The nest is a large grey paper football, sometimes the size of a basketball, suspended in a tree or under a roof overhang. They defend it in numbers and they will chase. A large aerial nest of this species is a professional job.

Mud daubers. Not social, no colony, no workers. A solitary female builds small organ-pipe tubes of mud in a sheltered spot and provisions each with paralyzed spiders. They do not defend a nest and essentially never sting. Removing the dried mud is a scraper and a hand vacuum.

The Decision Table

Work through this before you buy anything.

Call a professional if any of these is true:

  • The nest is inside a wall, a soffit, or under a deck. Treating the entrance does not reach the nest, and blocking the entrance drives the colony into the house. This situation cannot be solved from outside and frequently requires opening the wall.
  • The nest is in the ground and you cannot see the extent of it — an old burrow system can have multiple exits.
  • Anyone in the household has a known systemic reaction to stings. This is absolute. A nest that would be routine becomes an emergency, and no homeowner equipment compensates for anaphylaxis.
  • The nest is above the first floor, or the work requires a ladder and a reach overhead. Falling is the more likely injury than stinging.
  • The nest is large — larger than your open hand — or activity is heavy.
  • It is a bald-faced hornet nest of any size in an area people use.
  • The nest is in a location children or pets use daily, and you cannot keep them away for 24 hours.

You can reasonably handle it yourself if all of these are true:

  • It is a small paper wasp nest, no bigger than your palm, attached to a reachable surface under an eave or on a shutter.
  • You can work at dusk or after dark, when every adult is on the nest.
  • You have a clear retreat route and no ladder is involved, or only a step stool.
  • Nobody in the house is allergic.
  • You are prepared to abandon the attempt if the nest turns out to be larger than you thought or the entrance goes into a cavity.

Anything that does not fit the second list goes to the first.

If You Are Doing It: Dark, Red, and Two Passes

The timing is the whole method.

Go after full dark, two hours or more after sunset. All the adults are on the nest; foragers are back. Wasp vision is poor in low light and they do not fly readily at night. Do not do this at dusk in summer, when foragers are still returning.

Use a red-filtered light if you can. Wasps are relatively insensitive to long-wavelength red light, so a red headlamp lets you see the nest without the same level of disturbance a white light causes. A white light at close range will wake the nest.

Wear more than you think you need. A bee suit is ideal; failing that, a hooded jacket, long sleeves, gloves, boots, and cuffs and trouser legs sealed with tape or elastic. Thin fabric does not stop a sting — the stinger is about 2 millimeters and will go through a T-shirt. A face covering matters most, because stings to the head and neck produce the most swelling.

Use a product labeled for wasps and hornets, and use it from distance. These aerosols are formulated to shoot a jet 15 to 20 feet, which is the point — you want to be outside the defensive radius. Follow the label exactly; the label is the law for pesticide use and it also tells you the distance and the re-entry interval.

Do it in two passes. First, from maximum range, apply to the nest entrance and the surface. Wait the interval on the label — usually several minutes — while staying back. Then approach a step closer and apply again to the same area. A single close approach is how people get stung.

Then leave. Do not stand under the nest to admire the result. Do not poke it with a stick to see whether they are dead. Return the following evening to check, and again two nights later.

Remove the nest in daylight only after two consecutive nights with no activity, and wear gloves. A dead nest still contains larvae and pupae and will attract scavengers; a paper wasp nest removed and discarded is the end of it.

Never stand on the top rung of a ladder, and never position a ladder so your exit route is behind you. The injury statistics in this work are dominated by falls, not stings.

The Two Situations Where You Must Not Intervene

A nest inside a wall. This is the one people get wrong in a way that makes things worse. The visible entrance is a hole in the siding or a gap in a soffit. Spraying that hole kills the guards and does not reach the nest, which is somewhere in the cavity. Blocking the hole is worse: the colony, now unable to leave, follows the light into the living space and starts emerging from outlets, baseboards, and light fixtures.

The correct sequence is a professional one: treat through the entrance with a product designed for it, leave the entrance open for several days so returning foragers are exposed, then seal after activity has stopped. That is a multi-visit job and it is not something to improvise.

Anyone with a history of systemic reactions. If someone in the household has ever had hives beyond the sting site, swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty breathing after a sting, the calculation is not “what are the odds” but “what is the consequence.” A prescribed epinephrine auto-injector is not a substitute for avoiding the encounter; it is a bridge to emergency care. Call a professional and keep the area clear in the meantime.

What Happens to the Nest in Winter

This is the piece of information that dissolves a lot of unnecessary worry and expense.

The colony dies at the first hard frost. Workers, larvae, and the old queen do not survive. Only the newly produced queens leave, mate, and find a sheltered spot to overwinter individually — in a woodpile, under loose bark, in a shed.

The nest is not reused. A paper wasp nest, a yellowjacket nest, and a bald-faced hornet nest are all single-season structures. Leaving one in place over winter costs nothing. You can remove it in winter if it is in a place you want clear — an eave, a porch ceiling, a shutter — and it comes away easily once the paper has weathered, but there is no pest-control reason to do it.

The practical consequences:

  • Do not pay for a nest treatment in December. It was already over in October.
  • Do the removal in winter rather than autumn if the nest is in an awkward spot and nobody is being stung. It is safer in every respect.
  • Expect new nests in spring, in new places. Each spring a queen starts from nothing, builds a small nest, and raises the first workers. That early nest — the size of a walnut — is the easiest one all year to deal with, and the queen is the only adult present.

What NOT to Do

Do not block the entrance to a nest in a wall. The colony will find the interior route and emerge indoors. This is the most common way a manageable exterior problem becomes an indoor emergency.

Do not pour gasoline, kerosene, or lighter fluid into a ground nest and light it. It is a fire hazard, it is illegal in many jurisdictions, and it does not reliably kill the colony.

Do not use a pressure washer on a nest. It breaks the structure without killing the occupants and produces a large number of very agitated wasps with nothing to defend.

Do not knock an aerial nest down with a stick or a broom during the day. You will be standing underneath the fall.

Do not spray a nest and then immediately go closer to inspect. The defensive response peaks in the first minutes.

Do not assume a nest is dead because it is quiet. Activity in autumn is intermittent; a cool morning tells you nothing. Check on two consecutive evenings.

Do not use a trap as a nest treatment. Yellowjacket traps catch foragers and are useful for reducing pressure around a picnic table. They do not affect the colony, and a trap placed near the nest entrance can increase local aggression.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do wasp nests get reused the next year?

No. Paper wasp, yellowjacket, and bald-faced hornet nests are single-season. The colony dies at the first hard frost and only new queens survive, overwintering alone. An old nest left on an eave over winter is inert.

Can I just block the hole where yellowjackets are going into the wall?

No. The nest is inside the cavity, and blocking the entrance forces the colony to find another exit, which is usually into the living space through an outlet, a baseboard, or a light fixture. Treat through the entrance and seal only after activity has stopped, which is a professional job.

What time of day is safest to deal with a nest?

After full dark, at least two hours past sunset. All the adults are on the nest and wasps do not fly readily in the dark. A red-filtered light reduces disturbance. Daylight work on an active nest is the highest-risk option available.

How do I know if it is a yellowjacket or a paper wasp?

Nest shape and body shape. Paper wasps build open-comb umbrella nests you can see the cells in, and have slender bodies with long legs. Yellowjackets nest in enclosed paper structures that are usually hidden in the ground, a wall, or a soffit, and have compact bodies with bold yellow and black bands.

Is one wasp near the porch a nest?

Usually not. Paper wasps forage alone and often patrol a porch or a windowsill. A nest is indicated by repeated traffic to a specific point, with insects entering and leaving.

Are wasp sprays safe to use around plants and pets?

Follow the label, which specifies where the product may be applied and the re-entry interval. Most are not labeled for application to vegetation or to surfaces where food is prepared. Keep pets and children out of the area until the product has dried and the label interval has passed.

What should I do if I am stung and start to feel unwell?

Seek emergency care immediately if there is swelling away from the sting site, hives, nausea, or any difficulty breathing. A systemic reaction is a medical emergency and is not something to monitor at home, regardless of how mild it seems at the outset.
