Earwigs in the House: A Moisture Reading, Not an Infestation

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 12 min read

An earwig on the bathroom floor at seven in the morning produces a reaction out of all proportion to the animal’s actual capability. It is about three-quarters of an inch long, glossy brown, with a pair of curved pincers at the tail end and a habit of running rather than fleeing. People kill it with a shoe and then worry for a week.

Start with the two facts that end the worry. The pincers are used for capturing small prey and for folding wings; they cannot break skin on a human, and an earwig will almost always choose to run rather than pinch. The name comes from a folk belief that the insect crawls into ears to lay eggs, which has no basis in fact — there is no recorded case, and earwigs have no behavior that would produce one.

What matters is not the insect. An earwig indoors is an environmental reading. Earwigs are one of the most reliable moisture indicators in a house, and their presence is telling you that a specific zone around your foundation is damp, shaded, and cluttered enough to support them. That zone is also the one that produces carpenter ants, termites, pillbugs, and basement dampness. The general principle — identify what the pest is using before treating the pest — is set out in the DIY pest control method; earwigs are the clearest example of it in the whole house.

What the Pincers Are Actually For

The cerci, as the pincers are properly called, are not a weapon against people. They are used to grab prey, to maneuver in tight spaces, and — in a detail that surprises most people — to fold the hindwings under the small leathery forewings after flight. Earwigs can fly, though they rarely do and they are clumsy at it.

The shape of the pincers tells you the sex, which is the only field identification worth learning. Males have curved, inward-hooking cerci; females have straighter ones. If you are seeing mostly one or the other, that is a small clue about whether you are looking at a wandering population or a local breeding one.

A pinch from an earwig feels like a firm pinch from a fingernail and does not break the skin. If you are careful when moving a flowerpot or a stack of firewood, you will never be pinched at all.

Why They Are in the House at All

Earwigs are nocturnal, thigmotactic — they seek tight spaces where their body is touched on all sides — and strongly attracted to moisture. Those three traits explain every earwig you will ever find indoors.

Outdoors, they live in the top inch or two of soil, in leaf litter, under stones, in the layers of a compost heap, and inside the hollow of a dead stem. They feed on decaying plant matter, aphids, and other small soft-bodied insects, which makes them mildly beneficial in a garden. A dense, moist mulch bed against the foundation is close to an ideal earwig habitat.

They come inside for one of two reasons. Either the population outdoors is large and the weather has turned dry or cold, pushing them toward moisture and shelter, or the outdoor habitat is directly against the house and the gap into the house is easy to find. In either case, the entry is at ground level: a door threshold, a garage-to-house door, a foundation vent, a utility penetration for a hose bib or an air-conditioning line, the gap under a sliding door, or a crawlspace hatch that does not seal.

Earwigs do not come in through the roof, they do not chew their way in, and they do not travel far once inside. They end up in bathrooms, kitchens, laundry rooms, and basements because those are the humid rooms, and they die there within a few days if they cannot find moisture.

The One Identification Worth Making: Adults or Nymphs?

Before doing anything, answer one question: are the earwigs you are seeing all full-sized adults, or are some of them small?

Nymphs look like adults but are shorter and lighter in color, and they do not yet have fully developed wings or pincers. They stay close to where they hatched. If you are finding nymphs indoors — particularly in a basement, a crawlspace, or a bathroom with a chronic damp patch — that is evidence of a breeding population inside the building, and the moisture problem is more serious than a wandering adult suggests.

Female earwigs do something unusual among insects: they guard their eggs and stay with the newly hatched nymphs, cleaning them and bringing them food. That maternal behavior means the nymphs do not disperse quickly, so finding several small earwigs in one location is a strong signal that the eggs were laid there.

If you are finding only adults, and they are scattered across rooms, the source is outside and the fix is exclusion plus drying. If you are finding nymphs in one place, treat that location as a moisture problem first.

Step 1: Measure the Humidity First

You cannot fix a moisture problem you have not measured. Buy or borrow an inexpensive hygrometer and take readings in the basement, the crawlspace if you can access it safely, and the room where you see the most earwigs.

Indoor relative humidity above 60 percent sustained is enough to support earwigs, silverfish, mold growth, and wood-destroying insects. Below 50 percent, they cannot thrive. A basement that reads 70 percent in summer is an earwig habitat whether or not you ever see one on the floor.

Take the reading at the same time of day for several days, because humidity swings. The highest reading is the one that matters.

Where the reading is high, the usual causes are a crawlspace without a vapor barrier, a downspout discharging against the foundation, soil that slopes toward the house, a dryer vented into the basement, or a basement bathroom without a working exhaust fan. Step 2: Create a Dry Band Along the Foundation

This is the highest-value action, and it costs almost nothing.

Earwigs travel from soil to house in a continuous band of moisture. Break that band and the population drops sharply.

  • Pull mulch back six to twelve inches from the foundation. A mulch bed touching the siding is a bridge. A bare, dry strip of soil or gravel between the mulch and the wall is a barrier earwigs will not readily cross in dry weather.
  • Reduce mulch depth to two inches. Deep mulch stays wet underneath even in dry weather. Two inches retains the weed suppression without holding a reservoir of moisture against the house.
  • Switch the material immediately against the wall. Coarse gravel or river rock dries fast and does not hold the layer of decaying organic matter earwigs feed on. Keep it to a narrow band, and keep it below the siding and above the soil line.
  • Rake out leaf litter from the corners where the wind piles it — against the garage, along a fence meeting the house, under a deck edge.
  • Move firewood and stored pots away from the wall. A woodpile against the house is a two-for-one: earwigs in summer, rodents in winter.

Step 3: Stop Water From Landing Against the House

Earwig problems that persist after the mulch is pulled back are almost always a water problem.

  • Extend every downspout at least four feet from the foundation, and make sure the discharge point slopes away. A downspout that drops water into a bed against the wall creates a permanently damp strip on the exact surface that needs to be dry.
  • Check the grade. Soil should slope away from the foundation at roughly six inches over the first ten feet. If the ground is flat or sloping toward the house, water pools at the wall every time it rains.
  • Water the garden in the morning, not the evening. Evening watering keeps the soil surface wet through the entire night, which is exactly when earwigs are active and feeding. Morning watering lets the surface dry before dark.
  • Fix the interior leaks that keep a room humid. A dripping supply line under a sink, a shower with failed caulk, a basement wall with efflorescence — each produces a localized high-humidity zone, and earwigs will find it.

Gutter overflow is a frequent hidden contributor, because water that spills over the edge lands in a concentrated line right at the foundation. For the fall cleanout sequence, see the fall gutter and yard cleanup.

Step 4: Seal the Ground-Level Entries

Earwigs need a gap, and the gaps that matter are all at the bottom of the building. This is a narrower job than full weatherization — you are sealing ground-level openings, not the whole envelope.

  • Door sweeps on every exterior door, set so the sweep touches the threshold across the full width. A visible line of daylight under a door is an earwig door.
  • Threshold seals on sliding doors. The track is a favorite route because it stays damp and leads directly into a humid interior.
  • Garage-to-house doors, which are frequently the widest gap in the building and the one most people never check.
  • Foundation vents and crawlspace hatches. Screen them, and make sure the hatch closes flush.
  • Utility penetrations — hose bibs, AC line sets, electrical conduit, gas lines. Fill the annular gap with expandable foam or a proper sealant, not with rags or steel wool alone.
  • Weep holes in brick veneer should stay open. Do not plug them; they exist to let water out of the wall cavity.

The broader approach to sealing, including the parts of the envelope above ground level, is in how to seal gaps around doors and windows. For earwigs, the bottom two feet of the building are the whole job.

Step 5: Dry the Interior Rooms They Reach

Sealing reduces arrivals. Drying removes the reason they stay.

  • Run a dehumidifier in the basement or crawlspace through the humid months, set to 50 percent and plumbed to a drain so it does not shut off when the tank fills.
  • Ventilate bathrooms and laundry rooms. Run the exhaust fan during and for twenty minutes after a shower, and check that it actually moves air — hold a tissue against the grille; if it does not hold, the fan is not working.
  • Dry the floors. A bathroom floor that stays damp after every shower is a persistent earwig invitation. Fix the caulk and the grout, and use a bath mat that dries rather than one that holds water.
  • Keep the laundry area dry. A washer hose that weeps, a dryer vented indoors, or a floor drain that stays wet all create the same effect.

Basements that stay humid regardless of these fixes usually need the walls and floor sealed, or the crawlspace encapsulated. That is a larger project, and a dehumidifier is the interim measure.

What NOT to Do

Do not spray the perimeter and consider it solved. A residual insecticide on the foundation kills earwigs that walk over it, but the population in the mulch regenerates from eggs, and the moisture that produced it is untouched. You will be spraying every three weeks indefinitely.

Do not treat the interior rooms. Earwigs found in a bathroom are almost always dead or dying by the next morning. Spraying a bathroom for a wandering insect adds pesticide exposure to a room where food and hands are present, for no measurable benefit.

Do not use glue boards as a control method unless you are using them to find the entry. They catch a few individuals and tell you nothing about the source.

Do not spread diatomaceous earth along the whole foundation and expect a barrier. It works only when dry, and the strip against a foundation in a wet climate is precisely where it stops working. A dry gravel band is more reliable.

Do not assume the earwigs are the problem. If you are also seeing silverfish, pillbugs, sowbugs, or damp-loving millipedes, you have a moisture problem with several visible symptoms, and treating any one species is treating a symptom. For the same reason, see why spiders keep appearing in the basement — spiders follow prey, and prey follows moisture.

When to Call Someone

Earwigs alone almost never justify a professional. Call when:

  • Nymphs are consistently found indoors and you cannot locate the damp breeding site.
  • The crawlspace is inaccessible and reads above 70 percent humidity — that is a structural condition, not a pest condition, and it needs a moisture contractor rather than an exterminator.
  • There is visible rot, efflorescence, or standing water in a crawlspace. Fix the water first; the earwigs are a symptom.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do earwigs pinch humans?

They can, but rarely do, and the pinch does not break the skin. The pincers are used for handling prey and folding wings. An earwig handled roughly may pinch in defense; one that is simply walked past will run.

Do earwigs really crawl into ears?

No. There is no recorded case, and the behavior does not exist. The belief is the origin of the name and nothing more. Earwigs seek tight, dark, damp spaces, but a human ear is not one they choose.

What does it mean if I find earwigs in my house?

That there is a moist, sheltered zone against or under the building. Earwigs are one of the more reliable indicators of excess moisture in a foundation, crawlspace, or ground-level wall. Finding them is a prompt to check humidity and drainage, not a reason to spray.

Why are there earwigs in my bathroom?

Bathrooms are the most humid room in most houses, and earwigs navigate toward moisture. They usually enter at ground level elsewhere in the building and follow humidity. Finding them in a bathroom is more often a sign of a damp floor or a failed seal than of an entry point in that room.

Do earwigs damage anything in the house?

No. They do not eat wood, fabric, food, or stored goods, and they do not breed in dry indoor spaces. A garden population is mildly beneficial, since earwigs eat aphids and other small pests. The damage they cause is psychological.

Will bug spray get rid of earwigs permanently?

No, because the population lives outdoors in soil and mulch and regenerates. Spraying kills the individuals present and leaves the habitat intact. Removing the moist mulch band and the water source is the only durable fix.

Are earwigs worse in certain seasons?

They peak in late spring and again in early autumn. Summer heat dries the soil surface and pushes them into mulch and wall voids; autumn cooling drives them toward shelter, which is when indoor sightings increase. A dry spell also pushes them toward the house.
