Cockroaches in an Apartment: Why Spraying Makes It Worse

Pest Control Insects Published October 29, 2026 13 min read

You turn on the kitchen light at 2 a.m. and something brown runs across the counter. You spray the counter. Two weeks later there are more of them, in more places, and now they are in the bathroom too.

That outcome is not bad luck. It is the predictable result of using a repellent insecticide on a species that responds to repellents by dispersing. Spraying a German cockroach population does not reduce it in a meaningful way — it breaks the population into fragments that scatter into new cracks, new rooms, and the units on either side of yours. The spray is doing the opposite of what you paid for.

The method that works is nearly the inverse of instinct: you put out a small amount of slow-acting bait in many places, you do not spray anything, and you wait two to four weeks for the colony to collapse. The identifying-then-excluding sequence that governs household pests in general is in the DIY pest control method; this guide handles the specific case of a cockroach in a building you share with other people, and the ant version of the same bait logic is in getting rid of ants in the kitchen.

Step 1: Identify the Species, Because the Method Depends On It

Three cockroaches show up indoors in North America, and they need different approaches. Catching one and looking at it under a light is worth ten minutes.

The German cockroach is the one that matters for most apartment problems, and it is the one that lives its entire life indoors. It does not come in from outside; it comes in through a shared wall, a plumbing chase, a laundry room, a used appliance, or a moving box. That is why an apartment with a German cockroach problem almost always has a building with one.

The American cockroach is usually a visitor from a sewer or a floor drain. Finding one large roach near a basement drain is a different problem from finding small ones in a kitchen cabinet, and it does not mean your kitchen is infested.

If you only ever see very large roaches in a basement or bathroom, start with the drain and moisture work before you buy any bait — drain flies in the bathroom covers the same wet-point inventory that this problem needs.

Step 2: Prove Where They Are With Monitors, Not Guesswork

Before treating, find out how many and where. Buying bait without this step means you will place it in the wrong places and conclude it did not work.

Fold sticky monitoring traps into their tent shape and place them flat against a surface, not in the open:

  • Under the sink, tight against the back corner and the pipe collar
  • Behind the refrigerator, on the floor at the compressor end
  • Inside the cabinet under the stove, at the back
  • Behind the toilet and inside the vanity
  • Along the baseboard behind the bin
  • Inside the hinge side of a kitchen cabinet door
  • Behind a picture frame or a loose baseboard in the bathroom

Leave them five to seven nights and check daily. Roaches follow edges, so a trap along a wall or in a corner catches far more than one in the middle of a floor.

The trap that fills fastest tells you the harbor — and it is often not where you saw the roach. In apartments, the heaviest catch is usually behind the refrigerator, under the sink at the pipe penetration, or inside a cabinet that shares a wall with a neighboring unit.

Mark each trap’s location with tape and a date. The count is your baseline; without it you cannot tell whether the treatment is working.

Step 3: Understand Why the Spray Failed

Worth two minutes, because it determines everything that follows.

German cockroaches live in cracks — literally. A first-instar nymph is about the size of a grain of rice and fits through a gap of a millimeter. A population in a wall void, behind a cabinet, or inside a pipe chase is physically unreachable by any spray you can buy.

Pyrethroid sprays are also repellent. That is deliberate for some uses, but here it means a roach that contacts a treated surface and survives will avoid treated surfaces afterward. The population splits: some individuals move deeper into the void, some move to a room you did not spray, some walk through the shared wall. You have not killed the colony; you have redistributed it and made it harder to treat, because now it avoids the very surfaces you would use to reach it.

Residual sprays have a legitimate role in professional programs — as a complement to bait, applied to specific harborages, not as a broadcast over counters. On their own, in a home, they reliably produce the outcome described above.

Step 4: Bait, Placed Like a Professional Would Place It

Gel bait is a food matrix with a slow-acting active ingredient. The mechanism is what makes it work where spray fails, and it has three parts:

  1. It is attractive. Roaches eat it voluntarily, so it reaches individuals inside voids that no spray contacts.
  2. It kills slowly, over hours to a few days. A fast kill is a failed bait — the roach dies in the open, its fellows associate the bait with danger, and the colony avoids it afterward.
  3. The dead are eaten. Cockroaches are cannibalistic and coprophagic. A roach that dies from the bait is consumed by others, which then die in turn. This secondary kill is why one application can take out a colony many times larger than the number that fed.

Placement rules, and these are the difference between working and not:

  • Small dabs, many locations. A dab the size of a grain of rice, in twenty to thirty places, beats a thick line along a baseboard. Roaches are neophobic — they investigate small new objects more readily than large ones.
  • In cracks, corners, and hinges. Underside of cabinet shelves at the back corner, the hinge side of the door, the gap where a counter meets a backsplash, the pipe collar under the sink, the seam of a drawer box, behind the refrigerator’s compressor panel, inside a wall void at an outlet cutout.
  • Out of reach of children and pets, and out of sight. This is not only safety; it also keeps the bait where the roaches travel.
  • Do not clean the area afterward. A wiped surface removes the bait. Do not mop around placements for at least a week.
  • Do not combine with spray in the same area. This is the single most common failure. The repellent keeps roaches away from the bait. If you have sprayed recently, wait until the residue has weathered (two weeks or more, or wash the surface thoroughly) before baiting that area.

Bait stations — the pre-filled plastic boxes — work better than gel for exposed areas like a cabinet floor or behind a toilet, because they protect the bait from moisture and dust. Gel is better for cracks where a box will not fit.

Step 5: Close the Movement Routes You Can Reach

You cannot seal a shared wall, but you can close the routes between rooms and units that are in your control.

  • Caulk the pipe penetrations under every sink, at the radiator, and where the gas line enters. This is where roaches cross between apartments most often, because a plumbing chase is the building’s shared highway.
  • Seal the gap where cabinets meet the wall and any open cutout behind an appliance.
  • Screen or seal the gap under the apartment entry door. A shared corridor is a real route; a door with a visible gap under it is the easiest fix on this list.
  • Fix the moisture. A dripping joint under a sink raises humidity in the cabinet, and German cockroaches prefer it. The wet-point work is the same as in removing mold from bathroom caulk, and the cabinet itself is covered in organizing under the sink.
  • Check appliances that came from elsewhere. A used refrigerator, microwave, or toaster from a thrift store can carry a population. Inspect the compressor area and the back panel.

Step 6: Deal With the Building, Not Just Your Unit

This is where apartment problems are won or lost, and it is also the part that no amount of product can solve alone.

A German cockroach population in one unit is a population in the building. The pipe chase behind your sink connects to every unit on the stack. Baiting your kitchen thoroughly will drop your count, and then it will rise again over weeks to months because new individuals are walking in through the wall.

What to do:

  • Tell the landlord or property manager in writing, with dates and photographs. In most of the United States, a landlord is obliged to maintain a habitable unit and that includes pest control in a multi-unit building; the specific rules vary by state and city, and asking what the building’s pest control arrangement is costs nothing.
  • Ask whether adjacent and above/below units have been treated. A treatment of your unit alone, with an active population next door, has a predictable outcome.
  • Ask for a whole-building or at least a block treatment — the same day across connected units — rather than a series of single-unit visits.
  • Cooperate with access. If a professional is coming, do not clean the bait away or refuse the kitchen access they need; the inspection is most of the treatment.
  • Document the pattern. A log with dates, trap counts, and photos is far more persuasive than a complaint that the bugs are back.

If your building’s management will not act, the practical option is to keep your own unit baited continuously — the bait is safe to leave in place and the count stays low — while you decide how much longer you want to stay.

Step 7: Verify With Traps and a Repeat at Two Weeks

Treating is not verifying. Set the same traps in the same places and count again.

  • Week one to two: the count may go up briefly. This is normal and is a good sign — poisoned roaches move in the open and wander into traps.
  • Week three to four: the count should drop sharply. Bait programs take two to four weeks because the secondary kill has to work through the colony.
  • Then re-bait. Apply a second round at the same placements after four weeks, even if the count is low. A residual population in a wall void will rebuild, and a second application catches the cohort that hatched after the first.
  • Maintain a permanent monitor — one trap behind the refrigerator and one under the sink, checked monthly. An increase in the trap count is your early warning that the building has a new activity, months before you would see a roach on a counter.

What NOT to Do

Do not spray and bait at the same time. The spray’s repellency defeats the bait’s attractiveness. This single mistake accounts for most “bait doesn’t work” reports.

Do not use a bug bomb. A fogger does not penetrate cracks, it deposits residue on every surface including food contact surfaces, and it drives the population deeper into voids and adjacent units.

Do not scatter boric acid powder. Roaches avoid crawling through powder on open surfaces, and it is a respiratory irritant in a kitchen. Boric acid in a professional formulation inside a bait station is a different product with a different delivery.

Do not rely on repellents. Bay leaves, cucumber peel, catnip, mothballs, citrus oil, and ultrasonic plug-ins have no reliable effect. Some are actively harmful — mothballs in a kitchen are a vapor exposure with no benefit.

Do not throw out the appliances or the cabinets first. Bait is far cheaper than a kitchen, and it usually resolves the problem.

Do not blame the cleaning. A clean kitchen can host a large population; German cockroaches eat grease film, glue, paper, and soap. Cleaning reduces the food supply and helps, but it is not the root cause and it will not resolve an established colony.

Do not move to sleep in another room. If the problem is in the bedroom, moving to the sofa spreads it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I have more roaches after spraying?

Because pyrethroid sprays are repellent. Roaches that survive contact avoid treated surfaces and move into untreated cracks, other rooms, or the neighboring unit. You redistributed the population instead of reducing it. Switch to bait and let the spray residue weather before you apply it.

How long does gel bait take to work?

Two to four weeks for a visible collapse. The active ingredient is deliberately slow, and the secondary kill — roaches eating poisoned dead roaches — has to work through the colony. A count that is still falling at week three is on track.

Where do I put roach bait?

Small dabs in cracks and corners: hinge side of cabinet doors, the back corner of the underside of a shelf, pipe collars under sinks, the gap between counter and backsplash, behind the refrigerator near the compressor, and inside outlet cutouts. Twenty to thirty small placements beats a few large ones.

Can I use bait if I have pets or a toddler?

Yes, if it is placed in cracks and behind appliances where they cannot reach it, and if you use enclosed bait stations in exposed areas. Do not place gel on a surface a child or pet contacts. Keep the applicator stored out of reach.

How do I know it is a German cockroach and not something else?

Two dark parallel stripes on the shield behind the head, about half an inch long, light brown, and it runs rather than flies. A large uniform reddish roach that glides is an American cockroach, which usually comes from a drain or a sewer and needs moisture work more than bait.

Will the neighbors' roaches come back?

If the building has an untreated population, yes. A single-unit treatment reduces your count and it will recover over weeks to months. The durable fix is a coordinated treatment across connected units, arranged with the property manager.

Does one roach mean an infestation?

Not necessarily. A single large American cockroach near a drain is often a visitor. A single German cockroach in a kitchen is a stronger signal, because that species lives and breeds indoors; set monitors for a week before deciding how far to go.

Does cleaning get rid of them?

No. Cleaning reduces food and helps the bait compete, but a colony living in a wall void or a cabinet seam does not depend on crumbs. Cleaning is part of the program, not the program.
