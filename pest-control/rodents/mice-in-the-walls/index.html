How to Tell If Mice Are in the Walls

Pest Control Rodents Published October 29, 2026 12 min read

Noises in a wall at night are the single most over-interpreted symptom in household pest work. A surprising fraction of “mice in the walls” turns out to be a heating duct expanding, a downpipe, a trapped bird, or a red squirrel in an attic. And a meaningful fraction of genuine mouse activity produces no wall noise at all, because the animals are travelling through a basement sill or a kitchen cabinet.

The reason to get this right before acting is that the remedies diverge sharply. A rodent problem is solved by exclusion and trapping. A bird in a vent is solved by removal and screening. A duct making noise is solved by doing nothing. Poison placed for a mouse in a wall that is actually a squirrel in an attic is both ineffective and a hazard.

This is the diagnostic sequence: read the sound, then the physical traces, then confirm whether the animal is still present.

What the Sound Tells You

Time, rhythm, and location all carry information. Listen for two or three nights before concluding anything, and write down what you hear.

Two structural details explain most of what people hear. Mice travel along edges rather than across open spaces, so the sound follows the line of a wall, a pipe, or a duct rather than crossing the middle of a room. And mice are active in bursts — a period of movement, then quiet, then movement again — whereas mechanical noise tends to be regular.

The location matters. Sound in an interior wall between two rooms is most likely a mouse using the wall cavity as a route. Sound in the ceiling above a top-floor room, especially near the eaves, is more often a squirrel or a bird. Sound in a baseboard area, particularly where a pipe enters, is typically mice moving between the cavity and the room.

The Four Traces and What Each One Means

Sound alone does not confirm a rodent. These four traces do, and they are more reliable than noise.

1. Droppings. The most definitive evidence. Mouse droppings are 3 to 6 millimetres long, roughly the size of a grain of rice, pointed at both ends, and dark. Rat droppings are 12 to 18 millimetres, capsule-shaped with blunt ends. Bat droppings crumble when pressed and often glisten with insect fragments; rodent droppings are firm. Look along baseboards, in the back of kitchen cabinets, under the sink, on top of the water heater, and in the corners of the basement — the places a mouse moves through rather than the places it eats. The size and shape distinction is worth getting right before deciding what you have, and it is set out in more detail in how to read rodent droppings.

2. Grease marks. Mice carry oil in their fur and brush against surfaces along their regular routes. Look for dark smudges along the base of a wall, on the edge of a pipe, or at the corner of a cabinet — a faint dark line tracing the path. A fresh, blackish smear is recent; a grey, dust-covered one is older. This is the trace that tells you the route, which is what you need to know for trapping.

3. Gnaw marks. Rodent teeth leave paired, parallel grooves. Look at the edges of food packaging in a cupboard, the plastic sheath on an electrical cable, the corner of a cardboard box, and the bottom edge of a wooden door. Fresh gnaw marks are lighter in colour and free of dust in the grooves; older ones are darkened.

4. Urine scent. A persistent, sharp, ammonia-like smell in a closed cabinet or a small room is characteristic. It is strongest in enclosed spaces with little airflow. Do not use a blacklight as a primary test — many substances fluoresce, and the result is easy to misread.

Two things that are not evidence: sounds alone, and a single dropping you cannot identify. Look for two independent traces before acting.

A squirrel indoors is almost always above the top floor, entering at the roofline or a soffit gap, and it is a different problem: exclusion at height, and in some jurisdictions a licensed removal requirement. Rat activity at ground level with large droppings near a drain or a garage is a different problem again, and a more urgent one.

The Powder Test: Proving the Animal Is Still There

Before you spend money or effort, establish whether the activity is current. This matters because many “infestations” are historic — an old dropping, a grease mark from a previous tenant, a sound that stopped three weeks ago.

The method is simple:

  1. Clean a defined area completely. Choose a stretch of baseboard, a shelf edge, or a corner where traces were found. Vacuum it and wipe it clean, so the surface is visibly bare.
  2. Dust a thin film of talcum powder or flour across the cleaned strip, about an inch wide, along the suspected route. A light dusting is enough; too much and the tracks blur.
  3. Check after 24 and 48 hours. Fresh tracks — small three-toed footprints or a drag line from a tail — are conclusive proof of current activity. No tracks after 48 hours in a place where traces were found means either the animal has left or the route has changed.
  4. Repeat in a second location before concluding there is no activity. Mice use several routes.

Do this before buying traps or calling anyone. It converts a suspicion into a fact, and in a meaningful number of cases it converts a suspected infestation into a cleaning job.

What Not to Do First

The instinct to act immediately is understandable and often counterproductive.

Do not put poison bait in a wall cavity. A rodent that dies inside a wall creates an odour problem that lasts weeks and cannot be resolved without opening the wall. This is the single most costly mistake in this category.

Do not use sticky traps in a wall void or behind a cabinet where you cannot check them daily. Humane and legal considerations aside, an unchecked trap produces the same odour problem.

Do not seal an entry point you have not confirmed is inactive. Sealing with an animal still inside traps it in the wall, and it will find another route — usually into the living space.

Do not set traps without knowing the route. Trapping works when traps are placed along a known path, against a wall, with the trigger end facing the wall. Traps in the middle of a floor catch very little.

Do not assume one species. A house can have mice in the kitchen and squirrels in the attic, and addressing one does not address the other.

When It Is Not a Rodent at All

A few common false positives are worth ruling out early, because the fix is entirely different.

Heating ducts. The most common cause of regular ticking or thudding. The sound correlates with the heating cycle — it starts when the system comes on and stops when it goes off. Test by noting the time of the sound against the thermostat schedule. If they match, there is no animal.

Water hammer. A single loud bang when a tap closes, or a series of knocks. This is a plumbing pressure problem, and the fix is a different article entirely.

Birds in a vent or chimney. Continuous scratching during daylight hours, often with faint chirping. A bird trapped in a dryer vent or a chimney flue is a real problem but not a rodent one, and poison is both useless and inappropriate.

Squirrels or raccoons in the attic. Heavier movement, often in daylight, with scratching near the eaves or the chimney. Larger droppings, and often damage visible at the soffit.

Settling and thermal movement. An older house makes noises at night as materials cool and contract. The tell is that the sound is not associated with movement patterns and does not follow a route.

Establishing which of these you have is worth a week of listening. The alternative is treating the wrong problem.

What to Do Once You Know

If the powder test confirms current rodent activity, the sequence is: identify the species, find the entry, trap inside, exclude outside, then clean.

Find the entry. Mice need a gap of about 1/4 inch — the width of a pencil — to get in. Walk the exterior at ground level and check the sill plate, the gaps around pipes and cables entering the wall, the dryer vent, the garage door corners, the foundation vents, and the gap under an exterior door. This walkthrough is set out in detail in how to mouse-proof before winter, and it is the step that decides whether the problem recurs.

Trap before you seal. Traps inside, along the confirmed routes, checked daily. Snap traps baited with peanut butter or a piece of nut work; place them against a wall with the trigger toward the wall, and use several in a row rather than one.

Seal after the activity stops. Copper mesh packed into a gap and covered with sealant, or steel wool plus caulk. A mouse cannot chew through copper mesh or steel wool. Expanding foam alone is not a barrier — they chew through it.

Clean the contaminated area. Droppings and nesting material should be removed with gloves, the surface disinfected, and the area left to dry. Do not dry-sweep rodent droppings in an enclosed space; dampen them first to avoid raising dust.

Then prevent. The same exclusion work that solved the problem is what stops next autumn’s version of it, and autumn is when the pressure peaks as outdoor temperatures fall. The seasonal sequence is in how to winterize your home.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if mice are in my walls?

Two independent traces, not sound alone: droppings 3 to 6 millimetres long with pointed ends, and grease marks along the base of a wall or a pipe. Confirm currency with a powder test — clean a strip, dust it with flour, and check for tracks after 48 hours.

What do mice in the walls sound like?

A light, fast patter rather than a run, in bursts, mostly in the first couple of hours after dark. It follows the line of a wall or a pipe rather than crossing open space. Heavier thumping is more likely a rat or a squirrel, and regular ticking that coincides with the heating cycle is usually a duct.

Can mice in the wall get into the room?

Yes. A wall cavity is a highway, not a sealed box, and gaps at pipe penetrations, electrical outlets, and baseboards connect it to the living space. That is why trapping inside and sealing the exterior is the combination that works.

Should I put poison in the wall?

No. A rodent that dies in a cavity creates an odour that lasts weeks and requires opening the wall to resolve. Use traps in accessible locations and exclusion at the exterior.

How long do mice stay in walls?

They use the cavity as a route between food and nesting sites, so they will stay as long as both are available. Removing the food source and sealing the exterior entry points ends the use of the cavity; there is no separate "wall treatment" that achieves it.

Is scratching in the wall at night always mice?

No. Duct expansion, water hammer, a bird in a vent, and squirrels in an attic all produce wall and ceiling noise. Note the timing: sounds that coincide with the heating cycle are usually mechanical, and sounds during daylight are more often birds or squirrels.

How big a gap can a mouse get through?

About 1/4 inch, the diameter of a pencil. That is why visual inspection for "large holes" misses most entry points, and why the exclusion walk has to be done at that scale with a flashlight.
