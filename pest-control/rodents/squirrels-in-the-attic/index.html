Squirrels in the Attic: Find the Entry Before You Close Anything

Pest Control Rodents Published October 29, 2026 13 min read

The noise starts at first light. A scratch, a thump, a rolling sound across the ceiling, then quiet for an hour, then more. By evening the attic is silent. That daytime schedule is the first thing that separates a squirrel from a mouse, and it matters, because the two require completely different methods and the mouse method applied to a squirrel will fail in a way that can kill animals and start a fire.

Squirrels in an attic are a structural problem wearing a wildlife costume. They chew wiring, they compress and soil insulation, they gnaw at rafters and fascia, and they enlarge the hole they came through until a raccoon can follow. The wire damage is the part that should drive the timeline: chewed conductor insulation inside a wood-framed attic is a recognized cause of attic fires, and it is not detectable from below.

The standard rodent approach — bait stations and sealing — is wrong here on both counts. Rodenticide is illegal for squirrels in most jurisdictions and dangerous when a squirrel dies in a wall. And sealing the hole without first confirming the attic is empty can trap a litter inside to starve. The general sequence for identifying and excluding a rodent is in the DIY pest control method; this guide handles the specific case where the animal is a squirrel, and the diagnostic work for the smaller rodent is in how to tell if mice are in the walls.

Why Squirrel Damage Is Not Mouse Damage

Four differences change the method entirely.

Size of the opening. A mouse needs a quarter inch. A squirrel needs about two inches — roughly the width of its skull — and it will chew a small gap into a two-inch hole over a season. Gaps you would dismiss as trivial (a lifted shingle, a gap at the ridge, a soffit that no longer meets the fascia) are entry points.

Timing. Squirrels are diurnal. Activity before 10 a.m. and after 3 p.m. is typical; a scratching that runs all night is a mouse or a flying squirrel. This single observation saves weeks of wrong work.

Chewing. Mice gnaw for access. Squirrels gnaw constantly to keep their incisors worn, and they gnaw on whatever is in the attic — including cable sheathing, PVC conduit, and the plastic housing of a junction box. They also nest in insulation, flattening its R-value, and they urinate in a fixed spot, which saturates the material.

Waste volume. A squirrel produces far more droppings and urine than a mouse, concentrated in one latrine area rather than scattered. That concentration is what makes the cleanup a genuinely different job — see the safety section below.

Step 1: Confirm the Animal and Its Schedule

Before you buy anything, spend two days observing.

  • Listen at dawn. Set an alarm for 30 minutes before sunrise and sit in the room below the noise. A squirrel starts moving at first light. A mouse works at night.
  • Look at the droppings. Squirrel droppings are blunt-ended, roughly a quarter to three-eighths of an inch long, and slightly barrel-shaped, often in a pile. Mouse droppings are pointed at both ends, rice-grain sized, and scattered along a travel path.
  • Check for a latrine. A single wet, dark, stained area with concentrated droppings is typical of a squirrel. Mice distribute.
  • Look for runs outdoors. Squirrels use a regular route to the roof — a particular branch, a fence line, a gutter edge. Binoculars from the ground will show you the approach.
  • Rule out the alternatives. Raccoons are heavier and noisier and leave larger droppings. Bats are nocturnal, squeak rather than thump, and their droppings crumble to dust when crushed. Flying squirrels are nocturnal and much smaller. Each has a different legal status and a different exit method.

Step 2: Find the Entry From Outside, Looking Up

The hole is nearly always on the roof, not the walls, and finding it is the step people skip.

Get on the ground with binoculars and walk a full loop of the house, in the morning light. Work along the roofline in this order:

  • The soffit-to-fascia junction, and every soffit vent. A lifted or rusted vent screen is the most common single entry on a house with a dormer.
  • The eave return and the corner where two roof planes meet. Gaps here are invisible from below.
  • Ridge vents and roof vents. A ridge vent with a torn or missing ridge cap leaves a continuous opening along the peak.
  • The chimney chase and any flashing where it meets the roof. Squirrels work the gap between the chase and the siding.
  • Gable-end louvers. If the screen is plastic or corroded, it has been chewed.
  • The junction of a porch roof with the main wall.
  • Utility penetrations — the mast, the conduit, the AC line set.
  • The gutters. A gutter that has pulled away from the fascia leaves a gap the width of the strap, and it also gives the squirrel a ladder.

Now the tree line. Any branch within about eight feet of the roof is a bridge. Squirrels routinely jump that distance, and they will use a power drop or an overhanging limb when a tree is further.

Look for staining at each candidate — a dark trail of body oils on the fascia or a shingle edge, and gnaw marks with a slightly frayed, splintered texture rather than the clean cut of a saw.

Step 3: Check for Young Before You Close Anything

This is the step that prevents the worst outcome of the whole job.

Squirrels in North America typically produce two litters a year: one in late winter to early spring, another in late summer to early autumn. A female with a litter is in the attic for roughly six to eight weeks while the young are dependent. Seal the entry during that window and the young are trapped inside, where they will die and decompose — a smell that lasts weeks and can require removing insulation and drywall to resolve.

Do not close anything between roughly March and May, or August and October, without inspecting the attic first.

How to inspect:

  • Enter the attic in daylight with a headlamp and look for a nest — a ball of insulation, shredded paper, or fiberglass, typically in a corner, near a gable vent, or between rafters.
  • Listen. Young squirrels make a high, rhythmic squeaking when the mother is away. Stand still for two minutes.
  • Watch from outside at dusk. A mother returning to nurse will go straight to the entry. If she is carrying nothing and making repeated trips, suspect young.
  • If you find a nest with young, wait. The litter will be mobile in a few weeks, and the exclusion can be done then. If the situation is urgent because of wiring, call a licensed wildlife control operator rather than handling it yourself.

Step 4: Get the Animal Out With a One-Way Door

Closing the hole with the animal inside produces a squirrel that chews a new one. The correct device is a one-way exclusion door: a frame with a hinged flap, mounted over the entry, that lets the animal push out and not get back in.

  • Mount it over the confirmed entry, on the exterior, sealed on all sides except the flap. Staples plus sealant; gaps around the frame are an invitation to dig.
  • Provide light and an escape route inside. A squirrel that can see daylight through the door will use it. Clear any obstruction in the attic between the nest site and the entry.
  • Leave it for three to five days and check it daily. Cover the flap in the evening and listen inside for movement; if there is activity, the animal has not left and the door needs another night.
  • One entry at a time. If there are multiple holes, exclude the primary one and monitor; use a cage trap at the others or seal them once activity has stopped.
  • Check for a second animal. Squirrels are often a pair during the breeding season. A door that lets one out and seals behind it leaves the other inside, and you will hear it within a day.

Cage traps are an option, but they bring complications: most jurisdictions regulate what you may do with a trapped squirrel, releasing on your own property is rarely effective (it comes back), and relocation onto public land is usually illegal. Check your state wildlife agency’s rules before trapping. In several states squirrel trapping on your own property for removal requires a permit.

Step 5: Seal With Metal, Not Foam

Expanding foam is the single most common mistake in this job. Squirrels chew it out in a day, and it does not stop rodents of any size.

The material that works is galvanized hardware cloth at half-inch mesh — small enough to exclude a mouse as well, which matters because a squirrel hole is a mouse highway.

  • Cut the patch two inches larger than the hole on every side and bend the edges to seat against the framing rather than over the siding.
  • Fasten with stainless staples or screws with washers, at roughly two-inch intervals, into solid wood. A patch stapled into fascia that is already soft will fail.
  • Seal the perimeter with exterior-grade sealant after fastening, not before.
  • Back the patch with a second layer where the gap is more than an inch wide, or fill the void with a scrap of wood first.
  • Do the same for every vent. Replace plastic soffit and gable vent screens with hardware cloth on a wood frame. A vent is a hole with a screen over it, and a plastic screen is not a barrier.
  • Seal the gaps you found in Step 2 that are too small to be an entry — the flashing gaps, the gutter strap gaps, the ridge cap issues. They will be entries next year.

Once the patch is on, check the roof edge again a week later. A thwarted squirrel often tries the same corner, and staples that were set into soft wood will work loose under that pressure.

Step 6: Clean the Attic Like a Contaminated Space

This is the part that gets skipped and shouldn’t be. Squirrel droppings and urine carry a real, if uncommon, disease risk, and the dried material becomes airborne when disturbed.

Wear a P100 respirator, not a dust mask, plus gloves and eye protection. Do not dry-sweep or use a shop vac without a HEPA filter; both aerosolize the dried material.

The sequence:

  1. Mist the waste with a disinfectant or a light detergent solution before touching it. Wet it down, do not dust it up.
  2. Remove droppings and soiled insulation into heavy bags, double-bagged, and dispose of them in household trash according to local rules.
  3. Remove and replace saturated insulation rather than trying to dry it. Urine-soaked fiberglass does not recover its R-value and the odor persists.
  4. Disinfect hard surfaces — rafters, the tops of ceiling joists, the sheathing in the latrine area — with a product labeled for the purpose.
  5. Inspect the wiring in the affected area. Look for chewed sheathing, exposed conductor, and gnaw marks on cable. If any conductor insulation is damaged, it needs to be replaced by an electrician; tape is not a repair. This check alone justifies the whole job.
  6. Look for gnawed structural members. A rafter or a top plate that has been chewed along an edge is usually cosmetic, but significant notching in a load path is worth a professional opinion.

An attic that held one animal holds others. For the fly that overwinters in the same space and leaves a carcass problem behind, see what is already dead in the attic.

Step 7: Remove the Bridge and Check Again in Spring

Exclusion closes the current hole. Prevention keeps the next squirrel from making a new one.

  • Prune every branch within eight feet of the roof. This is the highest-value preventive step and it is done once every few years.
  • Cap the chimney with a spark-arresting screen. An open flue is an entry for squirrels, birds, and raccoons.
  • Keep the gutter clear and attached. A sagging gutter is both a ladder and a gap; the seasonal route for this is in fall yard and gutter cleanup.
  • Screen the crawlspace and foundation vents with hardware cloth.
  • Walk the roofline with binoculars twice a year — in spring after the snow and in early autumn before the winter push. Five minutes with binoculars catches a lifted vent before it becomes a litter.
  • Check the attic once in late winter. A squirrel that got in during a cold snap is quiet in January and loud in March.

What NOT to Do

Do not seal the hole and hope. You will trap an animal inside, and it will either die there or chew a new exit through the ceiling.

Do not use expanding foam. It is chewed through in a day and it hides the damage while it happens.

Do not use rodenticide. It is prohibited for squirrels in most states, and a squirrel that dies in a wall or an attic void is a smell problem measured in weeks and a removal job measured in drywall.

Do not relocate a trapped squirrel. In most jurisdictions it is illegal to release wildlife on public land, and a squirrel moved a mile away is usually back within a week or dead.

Do not dry-sweep the droppings. Wet them down first. The disease risk in this job is the dust, not the animal.

Do not assume the noise is a mouse because it is small. Check the clock. Daytime means squirrel.

Do not enter a roof space you cannot move through safely. Walk on the ceiling joists, not the drywall. If the access is a small scuttle and the roof pitch is steep, hire the work.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if I have squirrels or mice in the attic?

Timing and droppings. Squirrels move at first light and again in late afternoon; mice are active at night. Squirrel droppings are blunt-ended, a quarter to three-eighths of an inch, and often piled in one wet spot. Mouse droppings are pointed, rice-grain sized, and scattered.

What is the biggest risk of squirrels in the attic?

Chewed wiring. Squirrels gnaw constantly to wear their incisors, and cable sheathing and junction box housings are within reach. Damaged conductor insulation in a wood-framed attic is a recognized fire cause and is invisible from inside the house. Have an electrician inspect the circuit that runs through the affected area.

Can I just seal the hole?

Not without confirming the attic is empty. Squirrels produce litters in spring and late summer, and sealing during those windows can trap young inside to die. Use a one-way door, confirm no activity for several days, then seal with hardware cloth.

What is the best material to seal a squirrel hole?

Half-inch galvanized hardware cloth, fastened with stainless staples or screws with washers into solid wood, with the perimeter sealed. Expanding foam fails within days.

Do mothballs or ultrasonic devices keep squirrels out?

No. Mothballs are a fumigant that requires a sealed atmosphere and are not labeled for wildlife exclusion. Ultrasonic repellers have no consistent effect on squirrels, which habituate within days. Neither is a substitute for closing the hole.

Will a squirrel in the attic go away on its own?

Sometimes, if the attic is a temporary shelter rather than a nest site. A female with a litter stays for six to eight weeks, and a squirrel using the attic as a winter den returns every night. Waiting is not a plan, because the chewing continues while you wait.

Is it legal to trap and relocate a squirrel?

In most of the United States, relocating wildlife onto public land is prohibited, and several states require a permit even for removal on your own property. Release on your own property rarely works because the animal returns. Check your state wildlife agency's rules, or use a licensed operator.
