Rats Around the Compost and the Bird Feeder: Cut the Food, Not the Hole

Pest Control Rodents Published October 29, 2026 13 min read

You hear the bin lid move at 11 p.m. There is a tunnel under the fence and a smooth worn path across the lawn from the compost to the shed. You seal every gap in the house, and the rats are still in the yard, still digging, still finding their way back to the same corner of the foundation.

That is because sealing and food removal solve different halves of the problem. Sealing closes the door; it does not change how many animals are outside trying it. A yard with a compost bin receiving kitchen scraps and a feeder dropping seed on the ground is a reliable food supply, and a reliable food supply supports a population that will keep testing the house all winter.

The three existing guides split the indoor work: telling rats from mice by the droppings handles identification, mice in the walls handles the diagnostic, and mouse-proofing before winter handles the sealing route. This one is about the outdoor subsidy, which is where the population is actually decided.

Why the Food Source Matters More Than the Hole

A rat needs roughly an ounce of food a day and a reliable water source. That is not much — and it is the reason a small, overlooked supply is enough to hold a colony in place.

Two numbers make the point:

  • A rat’s typical range is about 100 to 150 feet from its burrow. Anything edible within that radius is its food supply, which is why the bin and the shed and the feeder all belong to the same animal.
  • A feeder with sunflower seeds in it will drop a meaningful fraction of what is put in it. Birds scatter, wind blows, and hulls accumulate. A single hanging feeder over a lawn can deposit seed continuously for months.

Remove the supply and one of two things happens: the colony moves its range elsewhere, or it shrinks. Neither happens if the supply stays, no matter how well the house is sealed. That is why a house can be perfectly rodent-proofed and still have rats living under the deck.

Step 1: Read the Yard Before You Change Anything

Do this before you move a single item, because the evidence tells you which food source is the real one.

Walk the yard at dusk with a flashlight, or in daylight after rain when tracks show clearly.

Look for runs. Rats use the same routes repeatedly, and the vegetation wears away. A smooth, dark, bare strip about three inches wide along a fence base, a wall, or through grass is a run. Follow it in both directions; it will connect a burrow to a food source, and that pairing is the whole diagnosis.

Look for droppings. Rat droppings are blunt-ended and about half an inch long — noticeably larger than a mouse’s rice-grain droppings. They concentrate at feeding sites and along runs, not scattered at random.

Look for burrows. A hole about three inches across, with a smooth edge and no excavated soil fanned out in front, is an active rat burrow. Look under sheds, along foundations, under the base of a woodpile, and in banks and raised bed edges. Fresh soil at the mouth means it is in use.

Look for grease marks. Along a regularly used route, particularly at a gap the animal squeezes through, dark smudges from body oils build up on wood, masonry, and the edge of a hole.

Use a tracking patch. Sift a thin layer of flour or cornmeal over a three-foot section of run, or over a two-foot square in front of a burrow, and check the next morning. Footprints and tail drags confirm which routes are live and how many animals are using them.

Write down what you find: hole locations, run routes, feeding sites. You will re-survey the same points in four weeks, and the comparison is the only real measure of success.

Step 2: Change the Feeder So It Stops Feeding the Ground

The feeder is usually the largest continuous subsidy, and it is the easiest to fix without giving up birds.

  • Add a tray or a seed catcher. A wide tray under a tube feeder catches most of what is dropped. Empty it weekly into a sealed bin, not onto the lawn.
  • Switch to hulled seed or a no-mess blend if the mess is the main problem. Fewer hulls means less accumulation, though it also means less for ground-feeding birds.
  • Move the feeder at least 30 feet from the house, and away from shrubs, woodpiles, and the deck. The distance matters less for the birds than for the rat: open ground with no cover is where a rat does not want to spend time, and a feeder next to a hedge gives it a covered approach.
  • Take it in at night. If activity is heavy, bring the feeder in after dark for a few weeks. It is inconvenient and it is effective.
  • Sweep or rake under the feeder weekly. Seed on the ground is the actual food; the feeder is only the delivery mechanism.
  • Store seed in a metal can with a locking lid, in a shed or garage, not in a bag on the floor. A paper or plastic bag of seed in a shed is a rat pantry.
  • Suet and grease blocks are high-value food and should be hung on a baffled line, brought in at night if necessary.

If you feed birds and cannot accept rats, the honest answer is to stop feeding for a season and restart in a different location with a tray. That is the most effective single change available.

Step 3: Rebuild the Compost So It Is Not a Feeding Station

A compost pile is either a managed hot pile or an outdoor pantry. The difference is temperature, turning, and what goes in.

The temperature threshold. A properly built hot pile reaches 130 to 160°F in the core within a few days and holds it for a week or more. At those temperatures the material breaks down fast enough that it does not remain edible, and the pile is actively working rather than sitting. A cold pile — one that is never turned and never heats — sits for months, stays appetizing, and is where rats dig.

Fix the inputs first. This is the part that matters most and costs nothing.

  • Never add: meat, fish, bones, dairy, grease, oils, cooked food, or pet feces. These are what make a pile attractive.
  • Do add: fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, eggshells (rinsed), tea leaves, grass clippings, leaves, shredded paper and cardboard, and finished compost.
  • Bury each addition in the center of the pile, under a few inches of existing material, rather than dumping it on the surface. An exposed layer of kitchen scraps is an invitation.

Fix the container.

  • Base the bin on half-inch hardware cloth, bent up around the edges, and staple it to the inside of the frame. This stops burrowing from below, which is how rats enter most bins.
  • Use a lid that actually closes, weighted or latched. A loose sheet of plywood on top is not a lid.
  • Consider a tumbler. An elevated, enclosed drum with locked doors removes the ground-contact problem entirely and is the practical answer for a small yard with rat pressure.
  • Do not use an open heap if rats are already present. Open heaps work in large rural settings with space to site them far from structures; in a suburban yard next to a fence and a shed, they are a rat habitat.

Fix the management.

  • Turn every one to two weeks. Turning is what introduces oxygen and keeps the pile in the thermophilic range; an unturned pile goes anaerobic, cools, and sits.
  • Keep the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio near 25 or 30 to 1. Too many kitchen scraps and not enough dry leaves or paper produces a wet, cold, smelly pile that rats prefer.
  • Site it away from structures — at least 10 feet from the house, the shed, and the fence line, on open ground.
  • Empty the finished compost rather than letting the bin fill to the top. A full bin has no room to bury new material, so scraps go on top.

If you cannot manage a hot pile — no space, no supply of dry material, a schedule that does not allow turning — the honest option is berm composting or a municipal program, not an open bin.

Step 4: Deal With the Other Outdoor Buffets

Rats do not need much, and these are the sources people forget.

  • Pet food left outdoors. A bowl of kibble on a porch or in a garage is a nightly meal. Feed on a schedule, bring the bowl in, and store the bag in a metal can.
  • Fallen fruit. Apples under a tree, tomatoes that drop, berries on a vine — all high-value. Harvest or rake weekly during the season.
  • The barbecue. Grease in the drip tray and on the grill grates is a strong attractant. Clean it down after each use, and store the grill with the cover on.
  • The bin and the recycling. Outdoor bins with loose lids, cracked bases, or bags that tear are a continuous supply. Use a lidded container, rinse recyclables, and do not store bags beside the bin.
  • Chicken feed and coop spillage. Feed in a treadle feeder, store feed in a metal bin, and clean up spilled grain.
  • Drip irrigation and leaky hose bibs. Rats need water as much as food; a permanently damp spot under a faucet supports a burrow.
  • Fallen seed from a neighbour’s feeder. You cannot control it, which is why Step 2’s 30-foot distance is a compromise rather than a solution.

Step 5: Remove the Cover, Not Just the Food

Rats will cross open ground when they must, but they prefer a covered route. The habitat and the food are separate halves of the same problem.

  • Woodpiles. Stack off the ground on a rack at least a foot up and a foot away from any wall. A pile against a shed is a burrow with a roof.
  • Decks and sheds. Close off the space underneath with half-inch hardware cloth on a frame, buried a few inches down at the bottom edge. Skirting with lattice alone does not exclude anything.
  • Brush and debris piles. Remove them, or site them well away from structures.
  • Overgrown vegetation against the foundation. Trim back so there is a clear gap and no covered approach to the siding.
  • Stored materials in a garage. Cardboard boxes on a concrete floor become nests. Use shelving and plastic bins.
  • Crawlspace vents. Screen them with hardware cloth, and check that the screens are intact.

The overlap with the sealing route is real — the same hardware cloth, the same fastening method. The difference is that this work is about denying shelter around the building rather than closing the building.

Step 6: Bait Stations, and the Risk You Are Taking On

Poison is the option that works fastest and carries the most consequence, so it belongs last and with conditions.

Understand the mechanism. Most outdoor products use anticoagulants, which cause internal bleeding over several days. Because the death is delayed, a poisoned rat remains active and is available to predators before it dies — which is the route by which owls, hawks, foxes, and pets are exposed. Secondary poisoning is not a theoretical concern; it is documented in raptors repeatedly.

The conditions that make it defensible, if you use it at all:

  • Tamper-resistant, lockable stations only. Never loose bait, never bait in a bag, never bait thrown into a burrow.
  • Fixed in place — anchored to a wall, a fence base, or a stake, so it cannot be dragged.
  • Placed along a run, against a vertical surface, not in the open.
  • Out of reach of children, pets, and poultry, and disclosed to everyone in the household.
  • In the smallest quantity that addresses the problem, for a defined period, with a defined end date.
  • Checked on the label’s schedule and removed — with any carcasses and remaining bait — when the activity stops.

Check your local rules before buying. Several jurisdictions restrict who may use rodenticide outdoors, require it in tamper-resistant stations, or ban consumer use entirely. The label is legally binding and takes precedence over any general advice.

The alternative worth trying first. Trapping, combined with Steps 2 through 5, is slower but has no secondary risk. Snap traps along runs, covered with a box or a tile so non-target animals cannot reach them, set in the evening and checked daily. A burrow can also be treated with a one-way door or flushed with a hose, though both need care.

If you have children, pets, poultry, or a lot of raptor activity, the case for trapping over baiting is strong, and the case for a professional is stronger.

Step 7: Verify With Tracks, Not Absence

Rats are quiet and largely nocturnal, and the absence of a sighting means nothing.

Re-survey the same points from Step 1 in four weeks:

  • Re-dust the tracking patches. No footprints on a run that was active means that route has stopped being used.
  • Check the burrow mouths. Freshly excavated soil, a smooth worn lip, and new droppings all indicate continued use. A burrow with cobwebs across the entrance and no fresh soil is inactive.
  • Look under the feeder and in the bin area. Fresh hulls and turned material mean the supply is still being taken.
  • Check the foundation line and the deck skirt for new runs.

Success looks like: no fresh tracks on any patch, burrow mouths weathering over, no fresh droppings at feeding sites, and no noise after dark. That usually takes three to six weeks after the food sources are removed, because a colony does not leave immediately — it explores first, then shifts.

If activity continues at the same level after four weeks, one food source remains. The usual suspects are a neighbour’s feeder, a bag of seed in a shed, or fruit dropping beyond where you have been looking.

What NOT to Do

Do not scatter poison. Loose bait outdoors exposes children, pets, poultry, and every scavenger and predator in the area, and it is illegal in many jurisdictions.

Do not seal a burrow with a rat still inside. It will dig out, often into a structure.

Do not use ultrasonic repellers. There is no consistent evidence they work for rats, and habituation is rapid.

Do not rely on repellent plants and oils. Mint, cayenne, garlic, mothballs, and commercial “rat repellent” sprays have no durable effect outdoors. Mothballs are also a soil contaminant and are not labeled for outdoor use.

Do not leave the feeder up “because the birds need it” while the rats are active. Bringing it in for a month is a far cheaper intervention than a rodent problem in the walls.

Do not put meat or cooked food in the compost and expect the bin to be the problem. It is the input that decides whether the pile is attractive.

Do not assume the yard problem stays in the yard. Rats use the same gaps as mice, only bigger ones, and the pressure on the foundation is continuous while the food supply exists.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far should a bird feeder be from the house to avoid rats?

At least 30 feet, on open ground, away from shrubs and woodpiles. The distance is less about the birds than about removing the covered approach a rat prefers. A feeder 5 feet from a hedge is an easier food source than one in the middle of a lawn.

Will a compost bin attract rats?

An open, unturned bin receiving kitchen scraps will. A lidded bin on half-inch hardware cloth, receiving only plant material buried in the centre of an actively turned pile, generally will not. The container and the inputs both have to be right.

What temperature kills a compost pile's attractiveness to rats?

The target is not a killing temperature but a working one: 130 to 160°F in the core within a few days and held for a week or more. At that temperature the material breaks down fast enough that it is no longer a food supply. A pile that never heats is a cold pantry.

Do outdoor bait stations kill owls and hawks?

They can. Anticoagulant rodenticides cause a delayed death, so a poisoned rat stays active and available to predators before it dies, and the toxin accumulates up the food chain. That is why stations must be tamper-resistant, bait must be in the smallest effective quantity, and carcasses should be removed.

Is it legal to use rat poison in my yard?

It depends on where you live. Several jurisdictions restrict outdoor rodenticide use by homeowners, require tamper-resistant stations, or ban consumer products outright. The product label is legally binding and takes precedence over general advice.

How long does it take to get rid of rats after removing the food?

Three to six weeks for a colony to shift away from a food source that has been removed. They explore before they move, and a reduction in tracks is the reliable signal. If activity is unchanged after four weeks, a food source remains somewhere in the 100-to-150-foot range.

Do ultrasonic devices keep rats out of a compost bin?

No. There is no consistent evidence they work, and rats habituate to them within days. Hardware cloth, a locking lid, and correct inputs are what exclude rats from a bin.
