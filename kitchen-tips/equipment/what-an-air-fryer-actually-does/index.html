What an Air Fryer Actually Does (and What It Can't)

Kitchen Tips Equipment Published October 24, 2026 11 min read

An air fryer is a convection oven. Not “like” one — it is one, in a smaller box, with a fan that runs faster and a heating element that sits inches from the food instead of a foot away. Every real limitation of the appliance follows from that geometry, and so does every genuine advantage.

The name is the problem. “Fry” implies hot oil, and there is no oil bath in an air fryer. What happens instead is that a 1,500 to 1,800 watt element heats air to between 300°F and 400°F, and a fan pushes that air across the food at a speed a normal oven fan cannot match. The result is fast surface drying and fast browning — which reads as “fried” to most people, until they try to cook something that only frying can do.

The Physics, Briefly

Three numbers separate an air fryer from the oven it sits next to.

Oil conducts heat roughly twenty times better than air, even moving air. That is why a deep fryer makes fries in five minutes and an air fryer needs eighteen, and it is also why a deep fryer can set a wet batter into a shell in fifteen seconds while an air fryer cannot do it at all.

The browning itself is the same Maillard reaction in both cases. It needs a dry surface and a temperature above roughly 300°F. An air fryer achieves the dry surface by blowing moisture off the food continuously; a deep fryer achieves it by flashing water out on contact with oil. Both work. Only one of them can hold a liquid batter in place.

Where It Genuinely Wins

Reheating. This is the strongest case for the appliance and the one least mentioned in advertising. Fried food that has gone soft in the fridge — fries, wings, spring rolls, pizza slices — recovers its crust in four to six minutes at 350°F. A microwave makes it rubbery and an oven takes twenty minutes to preheat for one portion.

Small batches of things that should be crisp. A single chicken breast, a handful of Brussels sprouts, two servings of frozen dumplings. The air fryer’s small cavity is an asset here: it reaches temperature in three minutes instead of fifteen, and the fan speed is high enough that the food browns without being turned more than once.

Vegetables that usually go soggy. Cauliflower, green beans, and potatoes work well because the moving air removes the water they release. Toss with a tablespoon of oil — not for frying, but because oil is what carries heat into the surface and what browns.

Frozen convenience food. Nuggets, fish sticks, fries, and frozen spring rolls are formulated to be reconstituted by hot air or oil, and the air fryer does it in two-thirds the oven time.

Where It Fails, and Why

The failures are consistent enough to list, because they all trace back to the same two constraints: air cannot hold things in place, and the appliance cannot add moisture.

Wet batter is impossible. Beer batter, tempura, and anything with a loose liquid coating will run through the basket before it sets. The batter never gets a chance to form a shell because air contact is too gentle at the moment the coating is still fluid. If you want a batter-fried result, you need oil. Breading — flour, egg, crumb — works fine, because it is already solid.

Leafy greens fly. Spinach, kale, and thin herb sprigs get lifted by the fan and stuck to the heating element, where they scorch and produce smoke. Anything lighter than a Brussels sprout half needs to be weighted down or cooked another way.

Cheese on top is a mistake. Shredded cheese on a casserole or a stuffed pepper gets blown off the surface and lands in the basket, where it burns onto the coating. Cheese inside a sealed parcel is fine; cheese exposed on top is not, unless it is pressed into the food.

Large items do not fit, and forcing them is worse. A whole chicken in a 5-quart basket sits against the element and burns on one side while staying raw at the bone. The rated quart size is the volume of the basket, not the usable cooking area — the usable area is the flat bottom, and food must be in a single layer with space between pieces.

It cannot braise, steam, or hold moisture. The whole mechanism is moisture removal. Stews, risottos, and anything that needs a humid environment are the wrong job, and a slow cooker does it without any supervision at all.

Baking is uneven. Most basket air fryers have a heating element only above the food, so a cake browns on top and stays pale underneath. Small items — cookies, muffins in a silicone mould, small loaves — work if you drop the temperature 25°F and cover with foil for the first half.

The Capacity Number Nobody Explains

A 6-quart air fryer does not hold 6 quarts of food. The rating is the basket volume including the space above the food, which has to stay clear for airflow. In practice:

Rated sizeReal single-layer capacityFeeds
2–3 qt2 cups of cut vegetables, 2 chicken thighs1 person
4–5 qt4 cups, 4 thighs, 1 small chicken breast pair2 people
6–8 qt6–8 cups, a 3-lb chicken3–4 people, in two batches for a full meal
10 qt + (oven-style)Two racks, closer to a small oven4+ people

The practical rule: fill the basket no more than two-thirds, in one layer, with visible gaps. Overcrowding is the single most common reason people conclude their air fryer “does not crisp” — the fan cannot reach the food in the middle of a pile, so the batch steams instead of browning, and the outside burns while the centre stays pale.

The Five Mistakes That Account for Most Complaints

1. Not preheating. Three to five minutes empty at the target temperature. Without it, the first four minutes of cooking happen at 200°F while the element catches up, which is exactly the window where the surface should be drying.

2. Converting oven times by habit. Air fryer times run 20 to 25 percent shorter than a conventional oven at the same temperature, and roughly 10 to 15 percent shorter than a convection oven. Start with the lower number and add time; the reverse ruins food.

3. Using no oil at all. Oil is not there to fry. It is what fills the microscopic gaps between food and air so heat transfers into the surface. A tablespoon per pound of vegetables is the working amount. Without it, vegetables dehydrate before they brown.

4. Shaking too late or too rarely. For anything in pieces, shake at the halfway mark and again at three-quarters. Shaking once at the end redistributes food that has already set on one side.

5. Cooking wet food. Pat things dry before they go in. Surface water has to be evaporated before browning starts, and a wet piece of chicken spends six of its eighteen minutes just drying.

Basket or Oven-Style

Two form factors share the name and behave differently.

Basket models — a pull-out drawer with a perforated tray — dominate the market. They heat fast because the cavity is small, they are easy to shake, and the capacity is honestly stated. Their limitation is height: nothing taller than about four inches fits, and a whole chicken is impossible below 6 quarts.

Oven-style models — a door, two racks, a rotisserie — behave much more like a small convection oven. They hold more, fit taller items, and let you cook two levels at once. They also preheat more slowly, brown less evenly because the fan is further from the food, and take up more counter space for the same usable capacity.

For one or two people cooking pieces and vegetables, the basket is the better machine. For a family roasting a chicken or baking, the oven-style earns its footprint. Neither does anything a full oven cannot — the difference is speed and, for the basket model, the ability to shake the food mid-cook without opening a door.

Accessories worth buying: perforated parchment liners (cheap, keep the basket clean, must be weighted down), and a small rack for skewers. Accessories not worth buying: silicone basket inserts, which block the airflow the machine depends on, and anything marketed as a “grill plate”, which reduces the contact area that produces browning.

Should You Buy One

Three questions decide it honestly.

Do you already have a convection oven? If yes, the overlap is large. The air fryer is faster to preheat and better at small batches, but it is not doing anything the oven cannot. The purchase is a convenience decision, not a capability one - and the same test applied to the rest of the kitchen will tell you whether the counter space is worth it.

How many people are you cooking for? For one or two, the speed advantage is real and constant. For four or more, a single basket means two batches, which erases most of the time saved.

What do you actually cook? If the answer involves a lot of braises, stews, soups, and one-pot meals, an air fryer will sit unused. If it involves roasted vegetables, chicken pieces, frozen food, and reheating leftovers, it will earn its counter space within a week.

Cleaning Without Destroying the Coating

The basket is aluminium with a PTFE or ceramic coating, and the coating is the part that fails first. Three rules:

  • Wash by hand with a soft sponge. A scouring pad removes the coating in one session and the pan starts sticking permanently.
  • Soak for ten minutes in warm soapy water rather than scrubbing dried-on grease. The residue softens; the coating does not have to be abraded.
  • Most baskets are dishwasher safe in principle, but repeated dishwasher cycles degrade the coating faster than hand washing. If the basket is the only thing going in, hand wash it.

The heating element and the interior roof accumulate grease that eventually smokes - the same problem an oven has, in a much smaller space. Wipe them with a damp cloth when the unit is cold, every few weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an air fryer healthier than deep frying?

For foods that would otherwise be deep fried, yes — a tablespoon of oil instead of a quart, and the food absorbs far less fat. For foods that would otherwise be roasted in an oven, the difference is negligible. The health claim is about the comparison, not the machine.

Can I put foil or parchment in an air fryer?

Yes, if it is weighted down and does not touch the element. Loose foil gets lifted by the fan and can contact the heating element, which is a fire risk. Perforated parchment liners made for air fryers are the safer option.

Why does my air fryer smoke?

Almost always grease on the heating element or in the bottom of the basket from a previous batch, not the food itself. Clean the element. If it still smokes with a clean unit, the food has a high-fat marinade dripping onto the element.

Do I need to preheat?

For anything that should brown, yes — three to five minutes. For reheating, it matters less. Skipping preheat is why first attempts come out pale.

Can I cook a whole chicken in one?

In a 6-quart or larger basket, a 3-pound bird fits and cooks in about 45 to 55 minutes at 360°F - but check the thigh with [an instant-read thermometer](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/safe-cooking-temperatures/) rather than trusting the clock. Larger than that and the outside burns before the centre reaches temperature. Spatchcocking solves the problem better than a bigger machine.

Is it cheaper to run than a full oven?

Yes, substantially, for small quantities. An air fryer draws 1,500 to 1,800 watts but runs for 15 minutes; a full oven draws 2,000 to 5,000 watts and needs 15 minutes of preheat before the food goes in. For a single portion the air fryer uses a fraction of the energy. For a family meal in a large oven, the gap narrows or reverses.

Why do frozen fries come out uneven?

Overcrowding, plus starting from frozen in a cold basket. Preheat, keep them in one layer, and shake twice. If the basket is small, cook in two batches — it is still faster than the oven.
