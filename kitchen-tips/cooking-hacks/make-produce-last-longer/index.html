How to Make Produce Last Longer in the Fridge

Kitchen Tips Cooking Hacks Published October 14, 2026 7 min read
Advertisement

Produce spoils for three reasons, and each has a specific countermeasure.

  1. Ethylene gas — some produce emits it, some is damaged by it. Separating the two groups prevents premature ripening.
  2. Moisture — trapped surface moisture grows mold; total dryness wilts. The goal is the correct middle.
  3. Temperature and airflow — cold stops ripening, blocked vents create warm pockets, and ethylene accumulates when air cannot move.

Fix all three and you add days to most of what you buy. This guide sets up the whole crisper system.

Step 0: Learn the two ethylene lists

Ethylene is a ripening hormone that plants release naturally. Produce that emits a lot will accelerate the ripening — and spoilage — of sensitive produce stored nearby.

High ethylene producers (keep these away from the sensitive list)

  • Apples
  • Bananas
  • Pears
  • Avocados
  • Stone fruit (peaches, plums, nectarines, apricots)
  • Melons
  • Tomatoes
  • Mangoes
  • Kiwifruit
  • Figs
  • Onions

Ethylene sensitive (keep these apart from the list above)

  • Leafy greens
  • Broccoli and cauliflower
  • Cucumbers
  • Carrots
  • Peppers
  • Squash and zucchini
  • Asparagus
  • Berries
  • Watermelon
  • Lettuce

The practical takeaway: never store apples or bananas loose in the same crisper as leafy greens or broccoli. This one separation habit alone significantly extends the greens.

The exception that uses the rule: if you want to ripen an avocado or a firm peach faster, put it in a paper bag with an apple or a banana. Ethylene works for you when you want it.

Step 1: Set the two crisper drawers correctly

Nearly every refrigerator has two crispers with a humidity slider, and the slider is the entire point.

Slider positionEnvironmentUse for
Closed / high humidityRetains moistureVegetables that wilt: leafy greens, broccoli, carrots, peppers, cucumbers
Open / low humidityLets moisture escapeProduce that rots in humidity: apples, pears, stone fruit, melon, avocado, tomatoes (if refrigerated)

Set the drawers once and leave them. Most people never touch the slider, which means half their produce is in the wrong environment.

Refrigerator temperature: verify it is at 37°F (3°C). Produce lasts meaningfully longer at 37°F than at 42°F, and this is the cheapest improvement available.

Step 2: Manage moisture with paper towels

The single highest-return produce habit is a dry paper towel in the container.

How it works: the towel absorbs condensation that forms on the inside of the container, which is otherwise what molds and wilts produce. Change it when it is damp — every few days.

Apply paper towels to:

  • Berry containers (line the bottom, and top loosely)
  • Greens containers (one in the bottom, one on top)
  • Bagged herbs
  • Cut vegetables

Greens first, always: wash and then dry thoroughly before storing. A salad spinner is the right tool. Wet greens in a sealed bag last about a day; dry greens last several. This is the most common produce mistake, and the most costly.

Berries: do not wash before storing. Wash immediately before eating. Water on berries is the fastest route to mold.

The mushroom rule is worth emphasizing: mushrooms in a plastic bag or sealed container become slimy within two days. A paper bag holds a balance of airflow that keeps mushrooms dry without drying them out.

Step 4: Revive what has wilted

Wilted is not spoiled. Leafy greens, herbs, carrots, and celery that have gone limp can often be restored.

  1. Cut a fresh slice off the stems or ends. This reopens the water-uptake channels.
  2. Place in ice water for 15–30 minutes. Cold water moves into the cells faster than slightly warm water.
  3. Dry and use immediately. Revived produce should be cooked or eaten, not stored again.

What cannot be revived: anything slimy, moldy, or discolored. Wilting is water loss; slime is decomposition.

Bonus use for slightly wilted greens: they are perfect for soups, stir-fries, pesto, and smoothies, where texture does not matter. Do not throw them away — cook them.

Step 5: Freeze what you cannot finish in time

When produce is about to go past its peak, freezing preserves it. The correct method differs for each type.

ProduceFreezing method
BerriesFreeze on a tray first so they do not clump, then bag
Leafy greensBlanch 2 minutes, ice bath, dry, then bag
Broccoli, cauliflowerBlanch 3 minutes, ice bath, dry, then bag
CornBlanch 4 minutes, cut off cob, then bag
PeppersCut, freeze raw on a tray, then bag
OnionsChop, freeze on a tray, then bag
CarrotsBlanch 3 minutes, ice bath, then bag
HerbsChop into ice cube trays with oil or water
TomatoesFreeze whole raw — the skin slips off after thawing
Bananas (overripe)Peel, freeze for smoothies and baking

Blanching matters for vegetables because it deactivates the enzymes that cause flavor and color loss. Skipping it produces mushy, off-flavored frozen vegetables after a few months. Berries, peppers, and onions do not need it.

Label every bag with contents and date. Frozen produce is unrecognizable after two months.

Why This Works

Produce is living tissue that continues ripening and respiring after harvest. Three controllable factors decide how fast that happens, and each has a direct countermeasure:

  • Ethylene signals ripening. It diffuses through the air between produce, so separation physically prevents cross-triggering. You can use it deliberately in a paper bag to ripen, or prevent it by separating.
  • Moisture is a balance problem rather than a “keep dry” problem. Too much moisture on a leaf surface grows mold; too little loses turgor and it wilts. Matching each item to the correct humidity and absorbing condensation with a paper towel targets the specific failure mode.
  • Temperature sets the metabolic rate. Every few degrees warmer measurably shortens storage life, which is why verifying the refrigerator temperature matters more than any container purchase.

The revival method follows the same biology in reverse: wilting is water loss from living cells, and rehydrating through a fresh cut restores turgor. Sliminess is decomposition, which no amount of water can undo.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the single best thing I can do to make produce last longer?

Dry it and separate the ethylene producers from the sensitive items. Wet greens and apples next to broccoli account for a large share of household produce waste.

How do I keep berries from molding so fast?

Do not wash them until you eat them, and store them in a container lined with a paper towel that you change when damp. Remove any bruised berries immediately, because one moldy berry spreads.

Should herbs go in the fridge or on the counter?

Soft herbs like basil belong on the counter with stems in water — cold below about 50°F damages basil. Cilantro and parsley do better refrigerated with stems in water and a loose plastic cover. Hardy herbs like rosemary and thyme keep well loosely bagged in the fridge.

Why do my mushrooms get slimy?

Too much moisture. Store them in a paper bag, not plastic, and do not wash them until just before cooking. Wipe with a damp cloth instead.

How do I keep cut avocado from browning?

Leave the pit in, press plastic wrap directly onto the exposed flesh, and refrigerate. Lemon or lime juice on the surface helps because the acid slows oxidation.

Can I store onions and potatoes together?

No. Onions emit ethylene and moisture that make potatoes sprout, and potatoes' moisture makes onions rot. Store them in separate cool, dark, dry spots.

Do crisper drawers really make a difference?

Yes, when you set them correctly. A closed vent creates high humidity that keeps vegetables crisp; an open vent suits produce that rots when wet. Most people never change the setting, which defeats the drawer's purpose.

Is it safe to eat slightly wilted vegetables?

Yes, if they are only limp. Wilting is water loss and is reversible. Anything slimy, moldy, or discolored has begun decomposing and should be discarded.
