How to Clean a Cutting Board (Wood and Plastic)

Kitchen Tips Cooking Hacks Published October 14, 2026 7 min read

Cutting boards accumulate more bacteria than almost anything else in a kitchen, and the knife-scarred surface is exactly where bacteria hide. The care routine differs meaningfully between wood and plastic, and applying the wrong one damages the board.

First, the research question people argue about: does wood or plastic harbor more bacteria? Studies have found that wood actually reduces bacterial populations over time, while plastic boards with deep knife scars hold bacteria in the cuts where washing may not reach. Both are safe when handled correctly, but plastic boards must be replaced once deeply scored.

Step 0: Separate boards by use

The single most important food safety practice with cutting boards has nothing to do with cleaning chemicals.

Use separate boards for raw meat and everything else. Cross-contamination from a board that held raw chicken to a salad is one of the most common routes to foodborne illness at home.

A three-board system, or a two-board system with one dedicated to raw meat, is the practical minimum. Color-coded boards make this automatic:

UseCommon color
Raw meat and poultryRed
Raw fishBlue
Raw vegetables and fruitGreen
Cooked food and breadYellow or white

If you only have two boards: one for raw meat and fish, one for everything else. Never the reverse.

Step 1: Daily cleaning (both types)

  1. Scrape food debris into the trash or compost — do not rinse it down the drain.
  2. Wash with hot water and dish soap, using a brush or sponge. Hot water plus detergent lifts grease and food residue.
  3. Scrub the knife scars, not just the flat surface. A brush works better than a sponge because bristles reach into the cuts.
  4. Rinse thoroughly with hot water.
  5. Dry standing up so air circulates on both sides. A board dried flat on a counter traps moisture underneath.

Never soak a wooden board. Prolonged soaking swells and cracks the wood and lifts the grain. Wash it promptly and dry it immediately.

Never put a wooden board in the dishwasher. Heat and prolonged water exposure crack, warp, and split wood. Some plastic boards are dishwasher safe, but check the manufacturer’s guidance.

Rinsing under the tap does less than most people assume — washing produce properly covers the cases that need more.

Step 2: Sanitize after raw meat

Washing removes most bacteria. Sanitizing kills what washing leaves behind.

For plastic boards

  • Dishwasher with a heated dry cycle is the simplest reliable sanitization, if the board is dishwasher safe.
  • Bleach solution: 1 tablespoon of unscented chlorine bleach per gallon of cool water. Flood the surface, let it stand for a minute, then rinse thoroughly with clean water.
  • Hydrogen peroxide: spray, let stand a minute, and rinse. It is gentler and pairs well with vinegar used in sequence (never mixed in the same bottle).

For wooden boards

Wood is porous and absorbs liquids and oils, so bleaching or prolonged soaking is a bad idea. Instead:

  • Vinegar spray on the surface, let stand a few minutes, then wipe with a damp cloth.
  • Hydrogen peroxide applied after the vinegar — as separate steps, not mixed — then a rinse and a thorough dry.
  • A diluted bleach wipe is acceptable if you rinse well afterward and re-oil the board soon after.

Important safety note: never mix bleach and vinegar, and never mix bleach with ammonia. Bleach plus an acid produces chlorine gas. Use one at a time, rinse in between.

Step 3: Deodorize and deep clean

Boards pick up odors from onions, garlic, and fish. Two methods work, and both are safe for wood.

The salt and lemon method

  1. Sprinkle coarse salt generously across the board.
  2. Rub with a lemon half, cut side down, working the salt into the surface. The salt abrades and the acid deodorizes.
  3. Let it sit 5–10 minutes.
  4. Scrape off the paste, rinse, and dry standing up.

The baking soda method

  1. Sprinkle baking soda over the board and the stains.
  2. Add a little water to form a paste.
  3. Work it in with a brush or sponge.
  4. Rinse well and dry.

Why these work without damaging wood: both use mild abrasives with a neutral or acidic chemistry that lifts residue instead of penetrating deeply, and neither requires the prolonged soaking that warps wood.

Step 4: Oil and maintain wooden boards

Wood dries out with washing, and dry wood cracks. Cracking is what makes a wooden board unsafe, because cracks are unreachable by washing.

Oil a wooden board once a month, or whenever it looks pale and dry.

  1. Wash and dry the board completely. Never oil a damp board — you will trap moisture underneath.
  2. Apply food-safe mineral oil generously with a cloth, in the direction of the grain.
  3. Let it sit 10–30 minutes so the wood absorbs the oil.
  4. Wipe off the excess. Do not leave a wet pool of oil on the surface.
  5. Let it cure for several hours or overnight before use.
  6. Use board wax or beeswax after oiling for extra protection, if you have it.

What to use: food-safe mineral oil or a dedicated board conditioner. Never use cooking oils such as olive, vegetable, or canola oil — they are unsaturated and go rancid inside the wood, producing an off smell that transfers to food. This is the most common mistake with wooden boards.

Never use tung oil, linseed oil, or varnish on a board used for food unless it is specifically labeled food safe. Many of these are toxic or contain metallic driers.

Step 5: Repair minor damage

Fuzzy or raised grainSand lightly with fine sandpaper along the grain, wipe off dust, then oil
Shallow knife scarsNormal. Sand lightly during an annual refresh
Light stainsSalt and lemon method, or a baking soda paste
A slight warpLay flat on a damp towel with a weight overnight, then oil and dry upright

Sand wooden boards lightly once a year to remove a thin surface layer, then re-oil. This restores color and removes embedded odors. Do not remove more than a thin surface layer.

Step 6: Know when to replace

Cutting boards have a lifespan. Continuing to use a worn-out board is a food safety risk, not a frugal choice.

Replace the board when:

  • A plastic board has deep, unreachable knife scars — the cuts harbor bacteria that no amount of washing removes
  • A wooden board has deep cracks that cannot be closed
  • The board has persistent mold that returns after treatment
  • A plastic board has warped enough that it will not sit flat
  • The board cannot be cleaned to a visibly clean state

How long they last: a plastic board used daily might need replacement in a few years, sooner if you cut aggressively. A well-maintained wooden board can last decades with annual sanding and monthly oiling.

A tip for food safety: keep raw meat work contained to one board, so the board that takes the abuse is the one you replace, not your bread board.

Why This Works

Bacteria need moisture, nutrients, and a protected surface. A cutting board supplies all three, and the knife scars are the protection.

Wood and plastic fail differently. Plastic is non-porous when new, so it cleans easily — but a knife cut is a physical cavity that traps food and moisture beyond the reach of a sponge. Wood is porous and absorbs moisture, but it also draws bacteria below the surface where they dehydrate and die. That is why a well-oiled wooden board can be as safe as plastic, while a deeply scored plastic board becomes progressively less safe.

Oil is a structural treatment, not a cosmetic one. Dry wood absorbs and releases moisture unevenly, which is what produces warping and cracking. Oil fills the wood’s pores so it holds a stable moisture content. A cracked board is an unsafe board because cracks cannot be cleaned.

Why cooking oils are wrong: unsaturated oils oxidize and go rancid. Inside wood, rancidity persists, smells, and transfers to food. Mineral oil is saturated and stable, so it does not go rancid.

Why the salt-and-lemon method works: salt provides gentle mechanical abrasion and draws out residue; lemon’s acid neutralizes the amine compounds responsible for fish and onion odors. Neither penetrates deeply enough to damage the wood.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is wood or plastic safer for cutting boards?

Both are safe with correct handling. Studies suggest wood can reduce bacterial populations over time, while plastic is easier to clean when new but becomes harder to sanitize once deeply scored. The deciding factor is the board's condition, not its material.

How do I sanitize a wooden cutting board?

Spray with vinegar, let it stand a few minutes, wipe, then apply hydrogen peroxide as a separate step, rinse, and dry standing up. Avoid prolonged soaking, and never mix bleach and vinegar.

Can I use bleach on a wooden board?

Only as a dilute wipe with thorough rinsing, and re-oil soon afterward. Wood is porous, so bleach penetrates and dries the wood. Vinegar or hydrogen peroxide is the better routine choice.

How often should I oil a wooden board?

About once a month, and whenever the wood looks pale or feels dry. Oil after any deep clean or sanitization, since washing strips some oil.

What oil should I use on a wooden cutting board?

Food-safe mineral oil or a dedicated board conditioner. Never cooking oils, which go rancid. Never tung or linseed oil unless explicitly labeled food safe.

How do I remove onion or fish smell from a board?

Rub coarse salt into the surface with a lemon half, let it sit 5–10 minutes, then scrape, rinse, and dry. A baking soda paste works well for stubborn odors.

When should I throw out a cutting board?

When a plastic board has deep unreachable cuts, when a wooden board has structural cracks, when mold returns after treatment, or when the board can no longer be cleaned to a visibly clean state.

Can I put a plastic cutting board in the dishwasher?

Many are dishwasher safe, and a heated dry cycle is an effective sanitization. Check the manufacturer's guidance, and avoid the dishwasher for any wooden board.

Should I use a glass cutting board?

Glass is non-porous and easy to sanitize, but it is very hard on knife edges and will roll and chip them quickly. Reserve glass for serving, not for cutting.
