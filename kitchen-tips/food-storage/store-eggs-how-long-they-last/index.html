How to Store Eggs (and How Long They Really Last)

Kitchen Tips Food Storage Published October 19, 2026 10 min read

Egg storage advice contradicts itself constantly — refrigerate them, don’t refrigerate them, keep them on the counter, never keep them on the counter — and both sides are right for a different egg.

The dividing line is the cuticle, the thin protein coating the hen puts on the shell as the egg is laid. It seals the pores and keeps bacteria out. In the United States, Japan, Australia, and a few other markets, eggs are washed before sale, which removes the cuticle and makes refrigeration mandatory. In most of Europe, eggs are sold unwashed with the cuticle intact, and room-temperature storage is normal.

Once you know which kind of egg you bought, everything else — the carton, the shelf, the dates, the freshness tests — follows.

Step 1: Work out which egg you have

Look at where you bought it and how it was stored.

Washed eggs (cuticle removed): typical of US, Canadian, Japanese, and Australian supermarkets. Sold refrigerated. The shell looks clean and uniformly matte. These must stay refrigerated, because there is no longer a barrier to bacterial entry through the pores.

Unwashed eggs (cuticle intact): typical of European and many farmers’ market eggs. Often sold at room temperature, sometimes with a visible bloom on the shell. These can be kept at room temperature — provided the temperature stays cool and, more importantly, stable.

The rule that follows is simple: if the egg was refrigerated when you bought it, keep it refrigerated. Moving a washed egg to the counter and back is the single worst thing you can do to it.

Step 2: If refrigerating, put it in the body of the fridge — never the door

The refrigerator door is the classic egg spot, and it is wrong.

The door is the warmest part of the fridge and the part that swings most. Every time it opens, the eggs go up several degrees and then back down. That cycling causes condensation on the shell, and moisture on a washed shell is a direct path inward. It also makes the egg’s internal quality degrade faster — the albumen thins, the yolk membrane weakens, and the chalazae (the white cords holding the yolk centered) loosen.

Correct placement: a middle or lower shelf, toward the back, where the temperature is stable at 35–40°F (2–4°C).

Tip orientation: store eggs pointy end down. The air cell is at the blunt end, and standing them this way puts the air cell at the top, away from the yolk. This keeps the yolk centered, reduces contact between yolk and shell membrane, and slows the loss of internal quality.

Step 3: Keep them in the original carton

The carton does three things: it stops the eggs from rolling and cracking, it shields them from fridge odors (egg shells are porous and will pick up smells), and it carries the dates you will need in Step 5.

A sealed plastic container is worse than open air, because it traps any moisture that does escape. An egg holder with the eggs exposed looks nice and is fine for unwashed room-temperature eggs, but for refrigerated washed eggs the carton is genuinely better.

If you transfer to a holder, cut the date panel off the carton and keep it in the holder.

Step 4: Treat the shell as the barrier it is

The shell looks solid; it is actually covered in thousands of pores. Everything that goes into an egg goes through them.

Don’t wash eggs before storing. Washing removes or damages whatever cuticle is left and, on a washed egg, does nothing useful. If an egg is dirty, wipe it with a dry cloth or a barely damp one — never soak it.

Wash only right before use, and wash your hands after handling raw shells.

Cracked eggs are not automatically garbage, but they are urgent. A hairline crack with the inner membrane intact should be used immediately, cooked thoroughly, in a dish that gets fully cooked. An egg that is leaking or has a slimy shell should be discarded.

Keep eggs away from strong-smelling foods — onions, garlic, fish, cut fruit. The shell does not filter odors, and a raw egg can pick up a fridge’s aromas within a day or two.

Step 5: Read the dates correctly

There are usually two numbers, and they mean different things.

Sell-by / best-before is a retailer date, not a safety deadline. It typically sits 3–5 weeks after packing.

Packing date / Julian date is the real starting point. On US cartons it appears as a three-digit day of year plus the plant code, sometimes with a use-by 45 days out.

Practical windows for refrigerated washed eggs:

StorageRealistic window
In the carton, fridge, unopened3–5 weeks past the packing date, often longer

Quality and safety are different clocks. An egg two weeks past its date is usually still safe if it has been refrigerated consistently — but the white will be thin and it will spread in the pan rather than sit up.

Step 6: Test freshness properly — and know what the test proves

The float test is the most cited and most misunderstood method.

How it works: fill a glass with water and lower the egg in.

  • Sits flat on the bottom — very fresh.
  • Stands upright on the bottom, blunt end up — a few weeks old, still good.
  • Floats — the air cell has grown large; the egg is old.

What it actually measures: the size of the air cell. As an egg ages, moisture evaporates through the pores and the air cell expands. A floating egg is old — that is all it tells you. Floating does not equal spoiled, and sinking does not guarantee safe. A floating egg can still be edible, particularly in baking; a sinker that has been stored warm can still be bad.

Better tests, in order:

  1. Smell. Crack it into a separate bowl. A sulfurous or rotten smell is definitive; discard it and wash the bowl.
  2. Look at the white. Fresh whites are thick and stay gathered around the yolk. Old whites are thin and run across the plate.
  3. Look at the yolk. A domed, firm yolk is fresh; a flat, easily broken one is old.
  4. Shake it next to your ear. A distinct sloshing sound means a large air cell and thin albumen — old, though not necessarily unsafe.

Always crack eggs into a separate bowl rather than directly into the pan or batter. It costs one extra dish and saves an entire batch.

Step 7: Handle cooked, frozen, and leftover eggs separately

Once an egg is cooked, the cuticle-and-membrane protection is gone and the rules change.

  • Hard-boiled eggs: refrigerate within two hours, keep in the shell if possible, eat within a week. Do not leave them at room temperature for hours — a common picnic mistake.
  • Peeled boiled eggs: 2–3 days, in a covered container.
  • Fried or scrambled: refrigerate leftovers within two hours, reheat to steaming, eat within 3–4 days.
  • Raw whites: freeze well in ice cube trays, 3–4 months.
  • Raw yolks: freeze only with salt or sugar added (about ⅛ teaspoon salt or 1½ teaspoons sugar per yolk) to stop them gelling; 3–4 months.
  • Eggs in shells, whole, frozen: don’t. The liquid expands and cracks the shell, and the texture is ruined.

A hard-boiled egg that is hard to peel is usually a fresh egg, not a bad one — fresh eggs have a low internal pH and the membrane clings. Eggs a week or two old peel far more cleanly.

Why This Works

An egg is a sealed package designed to keep a chick alive for three weeks, and every storage rule traces back to one of its three defenses: the cuticle, the shell membranes, and the air cell.

The cuticle is the outermost barrier against bacteria. Washing removes it, which is why washed eggs must be kept cold — refrigeration slows bacterial growth enough to compensate for the missing barrier. Unwashed eggs keep the barrier, so they tolerate room temperature as long as it stays stable.

Temperature stability matters more than temperature alone. Every warm-up expands the contents and the air cell; every cool-down contracts them. That pumping action pulls air — and anything suspended in moisture on the shell — through the pores. A fridge door provides exactly this cycling, dozens of times a day.

The air cell explains both the float test and the storage orientation. It grows as water evaporates, so its size is a direct measure of age. Storing pointy end down keeps the air cell at the blunt end, at the top, so the yolk doesn’t rest against it and the membrane doesn’t stretch.

And the smell test works because the compounds that make a rotten egg smell are volatile and unmistakable at extremely low concentrations — the nose detects spoilage long before any visual sign appears.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do eggs last in the fridge?

Three to five weeks past the packing date in the original carton, and often two or three weeks beyond the sell-by date with declining quality. Keep them at 35–40°F (2–4°C) on a stable shelf.

Should eggs be refrigerated?

If they were washed before sale — the norm in the US, Canada, Japan, and Australia — yes, without exception. Unwashed eggs with an intact cuticle can be kept at cool room temperature, but only if the temperature stays stable.

Does the float test tell you if an egg is bad?

No. It tells you how big the air cell has grown, which is a measure of age. A floating egg is old and should be cracked into a separate bowl and smelled; a sinking egg is not automatically safe if it was stored warm.

Can you eat eggs past their sell-by date?

Usually yes, if they have been refrigerated the whole time. The sell-by date is a retailer's quality marker. Crack into a bowl and check the smell, the thickness of the white, and the firmness of the yolk.

Why do my hard-boiled eggs peel badly?

Very fresh eggs have a low internal pH, which makes the inner membrane cling to the white. Eggs a week or two old peel cleanly. For a batch you plan to boil, buy them a few days ahead.

Should eggs be stored pointy end up or down?

Pointy end down. That puts the air cell at the blunt end, on top, which keeps the yolk centered and reduces contact between yolk and shell.

Can you freeze eggs?

Yes, out of the shell. Whites freeze well as they are; yolks need a pinch of salt or sugar to stop them turning rubbery. Three to four months, thawed in the fridge.

Why do my eggs taste like the fridge?

The shell is porous and absorbs odors. Keep eggs in their closed carton and away from onions, garlic, fish, and cut fruit.

Are cracked eggs safe to eat?

A hairline crack with an intact membrane, used immediately and cooked thoroughly, is generally fine. A leaking or slimy cracked egg should be thrown out.
