How to Store Onions, Potatoes and Garlic So They Last Months

Kitchen Tips Food Storage Published October 19, 2026 11 min read

The three staples that sit together in every supermarket display — onions, potatoes, garlic — have almost nothing in common when it comes to storage. Put them in one basket under the sink and you accelerate the decline of all three.

The reason is that each one is killed by a different thing. Onions die of moisture. Potatoes die of light. Garlic dies of humidity and temperature swings. Once you separate those three variables, each gets the treatment it needs and all of them last far longer than the plastic bag they came in would suggest.

Step 1: Understand what each one is actually afraid of

Before choosing a spot, know the enemy:

ItemMain enemySecondary enemyTypical life, stored well
Onions (dry, bulb)Moisture and trapped airLight2–4 months
PotatoesLightWarmth, then moisture2–3 months
Garlic (whole bulbs)HumidityHeat and cold swings3–6 months

All three are storage organs — modified leaves, tubers, and cloves designed to survive a dormant season. That is why they keep so long in the first place, and also why they fail in specific ways: each is trying to stay alive, not stay fresh.

A sprout on a potato is the tuber spending its stored energy to grow a plant. Green skin on a potato is chlorophyll plus solanine, produced because light reached it. Mold on garlic is moisture finding a crack in the papery skin. None of these is random decay — each is a response to a specific condition you can control.

Step 2: Give onions air and darkness, and never a sealed bag

Onions ship in mesh for a reason. They need continuous airflow across the whole bulb, and they need it in the dark.

Do this:

  • Keep them in a mesh or net bag, a wire basket, or a wooden crate with gaps.
  • Hang the bag, or set the basket on a shelf — anywhere air moves on all sides.
  • Pick a spot that is dark, dry, and cool: 45–55°F (7–13°C) is ideal. A pantry floor, a basement step, or a garage shelf in mild weather.
  • Keep the papery skins on. They are the wrapper.
  • Store them whole. A cut onion starts losing water and taking on odors immediately; wrap the cut half tightly and use it within a few days.

Don’t do this:

  • A plastic bag of any kind. Condensation forms inside within hours and the onion rots from the base up, usually with a soft brown patch you only notice when the whole bag smells.
  • The refrigerator. Cold plus high humidity turns the texture soft and mealy, and the flavor spreads to everything else in the drawer.
  • Right next to the stove or under a window. Warmth pushes sprouting.

The test for a good onion: firm, dry neck, papery skin that rustles. An onion with a soft or green neck is already going.

Step 3: Keep potatoes dark and cool, and out of the fridge

Potatoes have two hard requirements and one common myth to clear up.

Darkness comes first. Light triggers chlorophyll production, which is the green tint, and simultaneously raises solanine — the same bitter compound that makes green potatoes unpleasant and mildly toxic in quantity. Even a few days of indirect light on a potato sitting on an open shelf will green the exposed shoulder.

Cool, but not cold. The sweet spot is 45–50°F (7–10°C). Above that, they sprout. Below about 40°F (4°C), potato starch starts converting to sugar — the potato tastes oddly sweet, browns too fast when fried, and forms more acrylamide at high heat. That is the real reason “never refrigerate potatoes” is repeated so often, and it is about cooking quality as much as storage life.

Practical setup:

  • A cardboard box with the flaps folded over, or a paper sack, in a dark cupboard.
  • A ventilated crate or basket lined with newspaper, covered with a cloth.
  • Never a sealed plastic bag — potatoes respire and need to breathe, and trapped moisture grows mold and soft rot.
  • Don’t wash them before storing. Dirt protects the skin; washing opens the surface to infection. Brush off loose soil, let them dry fully if they are damp, then store.

About sprouting: an apple in the box genuinely helps. Apples release ethylene gas, and ethylene at the right concentration suppresses potato sprouting — it is the basis of commercial sprout inhibitors. One apple per few pounds of potatoes, replaced when it softens, buys you weeks. Do not use this trick with onions; ethylene makes them sprout.

Step 4: Treat garlic as a dry bulb, not a fresh vegetable

Garlic is the one people most often ruin by trying to be helpful. Refrigerating it is the standard mistake.

Why the fridge fails: a cold, humid environment is exactly what a garlic bulb reads as “winter is ending.” It starts to sprout internally, and once you bring it back to room temperature the sprouting accelerates. Meanwhile the humidity softens the cloves and invites mold under the papery skin, where you can’t see it until you peel.

What works:

  • Whole bulbs, in the dark, with air. A mesh bag, a wire basket, a terracotta garlic keeper with holes, or a braid hung on a hook.
  • Room temperature is fine — 55–65°F (13–18°C) — provided the air is dry.
  • Keep the papery wrapper on and break cloves off only as needed. Every broken clove is a wound.
  • Never in oil at room temperature. Garlic in oil is a low-acid, anaerobic environment and a documented botulism risk. If you want garlic oil, make it with dried garlic, or acidify it, or keep it refrigerated and use within a week.

Peeled cloves are a different product with a different clock: refrigerate them in a covered container, use within about a week, and freeze them in oil or vacuum bags for longer.

Green shoots inside a clove are not dangerous, just bitter. Split the clove and pull the green germ out if you are using it raw.

Step 5: Keep the three apart, physically

This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that makes the whole system work.

Onions and potatoes must not share a basket. The mechanism is humidity: potatoes release moisture as they respire, onions absorb it through their skins, and the onion in contact with that damp air rots first. A common result is a bag where the potatoes are fine and the onions are a wet brown mush.

Garlic and onions can technically share a cool dry shelf, but keep them in separate containers so a moldy bulb doesn’t seed the other.

Nothing goes in the same bin as potatoes if it needs humidity — potatoes want dry cool, most vegetables want humid cold, and the compromise ruins both.

A simple arrangement that works in a small kitchen: onions in a hanging mesh bag on the pantry wall, potatoes in a lidded (but vented) box on the floor, garlic in a crock or braid on a shelf. Three separate locations, none of them the fridge.

Step 6: Start clean, and check weekly

Storage life is decided in the first five minutes and then maintained by inspection.

  • Buy sound stock. No soft spots, no damp necks, no green potatoes, no black powder under garlic skin. One bad bulb in a bag will spread mold to its neighbors within days.
  • Cure them if they are fresh from a garden or farm stand. Home-grown onions and garlic need two to three weeks in a dry, airy, shaded place to dry their necks and skins before going into storage. Supermarket bulbs are already cured.
  • Inspect weekly. Pull the bag out, look, squeeze, put back. Anything soft, wet, sprouting heavily, or moldy comes out immediately.
  • Use the damaged ones first. A bruised potato or a broken clove is still fine — it just has to be dinner tonight, not next month.

Step 7: Know what’s still edible and what isn’t

A lot of good food gets thrown away here, and a little bad food gets eaten. The lines:

Potatoes:

  • Sprouted with small white shoots — cut out the sprouts plus a wide margin and use the rest, especially for cooking where the potato is mashed or boiled. Sprouts concentrate solanine.
  • Green skin — peel generously; if the green goes more than a couple of millimeters deep, or covers a large area, discard the potato.
  • Soft, wet, leaking, or foul-smelling — discard. Soft rot spreads through the whole tuber.
  • Wrinkled but firm — fine, just older; use for mashing rather than roasting.

Onions:

  • Sprouted green top — edible but the bulb is getting soft; use it soon.
  • Soft brown layer under the skin, no mold — peel down to firm flesh and use immediately.
  • Black or grey fuzz, or liquid — discard the bulb and wash the container.

Garlic:

  • Green germ inside — edible, slightly bitter.
  • Brown or soft cloves — discard those cloves; check the rest of the bulb.
  • Black powdery patches under the skin — discard the bulb; that is a storage mold.

Never store any of the three next to the fridge’s cooling vents or in a cupboard above the oven. Both spots look convenient and both shorten storage by half.

Why This Works

Onions, potatoes, and garlic are all dormant plant organs, and dormancy is a race between how fast they burn their stored reserves and how quickly external conditions push them to break dormancy or decay.

Temperature controls the burn rate. Every 18°F (10°C) of warmth roughly doubles the metabolic rate of a stored tuber or bulb, which is why a cool pantry stretches life from weeks to months and a warm shelf does the opposite.

Light is a signal, not an energy source here. Potato skin responds to light by producing chlorophyll and solanine as a defensive reaction — the tuber thinks it has been exposed and arms itself. Blocking light blocks the whole response.

Moisture is what decides rot versus desiccation. Onions and garlic need their outer skins dry to resist surface mold, which is why airflow matters more than any container. Potatoes need to respire without sitting in their own condensate. In all three cases, ventilation removes the water they give off; a sealed bag keeps it in contact with the surface.

And the ethylene chemistry explains the separation rule: potatoes want ethylene to stay dormant, onions respond to ethylene by sprouting, and apples supply it. One gas, opposite effects — which is exactly why one shared basket can’t serve all three.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should onions and potatoes be stored together?

No. Potatoes release moisture that makes onions rot, and onions release ethylene that makes potatoes sprout. Store them in separate containers in separate spots.

Why do my potatoes sprout so fast?

Warmth is almost always the cause. Above about 50°F (10°C) sprouting accelerates sharply. Move them somewhere cooler and darker, and add an apple to the box — ethylene suppresses sprouting.

Can you put potatoes in the fridge?

Better not. Below 40°F the starch converts to sugar, so they taste sweet, brown too fast when fried, and form more acrylamide. Refrigeration also turns the texture waxy and mealy.

How long do onions last?

Two to four months in a dark, dry, cool spot with airflow. Cut onions are a different story — wrap tightly and refrigerate, and use within three or four days.

Should garlic be kept in the fridge?

No. Cold and humidity make garlic sprout and grow mold under the skin. Keep whole bulbs dry, dark, and at room temperature, and break off cloves as you need them.

Are sprouted potatoes safe to eat?

Small sprouts can be cut out with a wide margin and the potato cooked. If the potato is also green, soft, wrinkled all over, or smells off, discard it — solanine concentrates in sprouts, green skin, and damaged areas.

Can you store onions and garlic in the same basket?

They can share a cool dry shelf, but keep them in separate containers so mold from one bulb doesn't spread to the other. Both need the same conditions, so a two-pocket hanging bag works well.

Why did my garlic turn brown inside?

Usually moisture and temperature swings. Brown, translucent, or soft cloves should be discarded, and the rest of the bulb checked. Store the remaining bulbs drier and cooler, with more airflow.

Do you have to cure home-grown onions before storing?

Yes. Two to three weeks in a dry, airy, shaded place lets the necks and skins dry fully. Skip curing and the bulbs rot at the neck within weeks.
