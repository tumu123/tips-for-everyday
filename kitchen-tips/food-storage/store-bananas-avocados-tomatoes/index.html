How to Store Bananas, Avocados and Tomatoes

Kitchen Tips Food Storage Published October 16, 2026 9 min read

Bananas, avocados and tomatoes are grouped together in storage advice for one specific reason: they are all climacteric, meaning they keep ripening after being picked, and they do it in response to a gas they produce themselves.

That gas is ethylene. It’s odorless, it’s invisible, and it’s the single most useful lever you have over these three foods. Once you understand that ripening is a gas-driven process rather than a calendar-driven one, the whole confusing set of rules — separate the bananas, put the avocado in a bag, never refrigerate tomatoes — becomes one principle with obvious applications.

The practical consequence: ethylene can be concentrated to speed ripening, or dispersed to slow it. You choose.

The second thing to know is that these three do not all behave the same way in the fridge. Tomatoes are genuinely damaged by cold. Avocados tolerate it well once ripe. Bananas sit in the middle — the skin suffers, the fruit is fine. Treating them identically is the most common source of bad results.

Step 1: Understand ethylene, the one mechanism behind all of this

Ethylene is a plant hormone. Climacteric fruit releases it as it ripens, and exposure to it triggers more ripening — in itself and in whatever is nearby.

Two consequences follow directly:

  • Trap the gas and things ripen faster. A closed paper bag holds ethylene around the fruit.
  • Ventilate and things ripen slower. Open air lets the gas drift away.

That’s the whole mechanism. Everything below is an application of it.

It also explains why these foods shouldn’t be stored next to ethylene-sensitive produce. Leafy greens, broccoli, carrots, cucumbers and cut flowers all degrade faster when exposed to it. A bowl of ripening bananas sitting next to a bag of spinach will cost you the spinach.

And it explains the reverse: if you want something to ripen, put it near a banana. That’s the oldest trick in the list and it works because the banana is a strong ethylene emitter.

They need dry air and separation, not the fridge — storing onions, potatoes and garlic covers the setup.

Step 2: Slow bananas down

Bananas are usually bought slightly green and ripen on the counter over several days — often faster than you can eat them. To stretch that window:

  1. Separate them from the bunch. Individual bananas release less gas at the cut stem, and one ripe banana in a bunch will pull the rest along. If you only separate them, you already buy meaningful time.
  2. Wrap the stem. The stem (the crown where they were joined) is where most ethylene escapes. Wrap each stem, or the whole crown, in plastic wrap or tape. This is the single highest-leverage thing you can do.
  3. Hang them if you can. A banana hook keeps them off the counter, reduces bruising, and improves airflow around the fruit. Bruised spots ripen and brown much faster than intact skin.
  4. Keep them away from other produce. A ripening banana will hurry along avocados, tomatoes, peaches and pears sitting beside it — sometimes desirable, usually accidental.

Don’t refrigerate unripe bananas. Cold interrupts ripening and damages the skin, and green bananas put in the fridge often never ripen properly afterward. If bananas reach your preferred ripeness and you can’t eat them yet, then the fridge is reasonable — see Step 4.

Step 3: Speed ripening up

The flip side, for hard avocados, green bananas or underripe tomatoes:

  1. Put them in a paper bag, loosely closed, at room temperature. The bag concentrates the ethylene they’re already producing. A plastic bag works too but traps moisture, which encourages mold on tomatoes especially — paper breathes.
  2. Add a banana or an apple to the bag if the target isn’t itself a strong emitter. For a hard avocado or a firm tomato, adding a ripe banana roughly halves the waiting time.
  3. Check daily. Bagged fruit moves from underripe to overripe fast. Once it’s where you want it, take it out and either eat it or move it to the fridge.
  4. Use heat as a secondary lever. Room temperature ripens faster than a cool spot. Not direct heat or sunlight — just don’t put the bag somewhere cold.

Expect roughly 1 to 3 days for an avocado, a day or two for tomatoes, and 2 to 5 days for green bananas, depending on how firm they started.

Skip the oven and microwave tricks. Baking an avocado doesn’t ripen it; it cooks it unevenly and the texture is wrong. There’s no shortcut faster than a paper bag.

Step 4: Use the fridge — but only at the right moment

The fridge’s role differs by food, and this is where most advice gets muddled.

  • Avocados: refrigerate once ripe, and it genuinely helps. A ripe avocado held at room temperature has a window of roughly a day or two. In the fridge it holds 3 to 5 days more. Halved or cut, it should always go in the fridge. Cold doesn’t harm ripe avocado flesh.
  • Bananas: refrigerate only once fully ripe, and expect the skin to blacken. Cold damages the peel’s cells fairly quickly, so the skin goes dark — sometimes within hours. The fruit inside is unaffected and stays good for several more days. The black skin looks alarming and means nothing.
  • Tomatoes: keep them out of the fridge while they’re ripening. This is the one case where refrigeration does real, lasting damage. Below about 50°F (10°C), tomatoes lose the volatile compounds that carry most of their flavor, and the recovery is incomplete — a chilled tomato tastes flat even after it warms back up. The texture also goes mealy. Only refrigerate a tomato that is already fully ripe and would otherwise spoil, and even then accept the flavor cost.

So: avocado yes, banana yes-with-cosmetic-damage, tomato no-unless-you-must.

Step 5: Handle cut avocado and tomato

Once cut, the rules change because oxidation becomes the problem rather than ripening.

  • Avocado half with the pit: leave the pit in, rub the cut face with a little acid (lemon or lime juice) or oil, and press plastic wrap directly against the surface so no air touches it. Refrigerate. Good for 1 to 2 days. The brown top layer is harmless — scrape it off.
  • Avocado without the pit: same treatment. The pit itself does little; what helps is excluding air.
  • Cut tomatoes: cover and refrigerate, cut side down on a plate if you’re only keeping them a few hours. Use within 2 to 3 days. Tomatoes lose quality in the fridge whether cut or whole, but a cut tomato at room temperature spoils faster still, so the fridge wins once it’s opened.
  • Freezing avocado: mash it with a little acid and freeze in a flat bag. Texture changes enough that it’s best for guacamole or smoothies, not for slicing.

Step 6: Place them deliberately in your kitchen

Putting the mechanism together into a layout:

  • A ripening zone — a bowl or hook on the counter, away from other produce, where bananas and anything you want to hurry can sit and off-gas without affecting anything sensitive.
  • A paper bag drawer or hook — for the items you’re actively trying to ripen.
  • The crisper drawer — for ripe avocados and any bananas you’re holding. Kept away from leafy greens and broccoli, which are ethylene-sensitive and will yellow or wilt faster near them.
  • Away from the fridge entirely — tomatoes, until the point where spoilage risk outweighs flavor loss.

The mistake most kitchens make is a single fruit bowl holding everything at once: bananas gassing the avocados, avocados gassing the tomatoes, and all of it sitting next to nothing sensitive but wasting the ripening control you could have. Two separate spots — one to ripen, one to hold — gives you control instead of coincidence.

Why This Works

Ethylene is a hormone the fruit makes itself, so ripening after harvest is self-driving rather than passively decaying. That’s why these three respond to gas concentration in a way non-climacteric produce — berries, citrus, grapes, peppers — simply doesn’t. Berries will rot but never ripen further, no matter what you put them in a bag with. Knowing which category a fruit is in tells you whether storage can improve it or only preserve it.

Trapping gas works because concentration drives the response. A closed bag raises the local ethylene level around the fruit, which accelerates the enzymes that soften cell walls, convert starch to sugar, and break down chlorophyll. Ventilation does the reverse by keeping concentration near zero.

The stem wrap is effective because the cut crown is the main release point — sealing it removes most of the source rather than merely diluting what escapes. That’s why wrapping outperforms simply separating the bunch, though both help.

The tomato refrigeration problem is different in kind. It isn’t spoilage, it’s that the biochemical pathways producing aroma compounds are cold-sensitive and don’t restart properly. The tomato stays edible and looks fine while losing the compounds that make it taste like anything. That’s why the advice for tomatoes is a quality rule rather than a safety rule — and why a tomato that would otherwise spoil should still go in, since spoiled beats bland.

Banana skin blackening is the same category: cosmetic, not predictive. Cold damages the peel’s cells and the pigment oxidizes, while the flesh, protected inside, is largely unaffected. Judging a refrigerated banana by its skin will make you throw away good fruit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I ripen an avocado faster?

Put it in a paper bag at room temperature, ideally with a ripe banana or apple, and close it loosely. Expect 1 to 3 days depending on how firm it started. Check daily — bagged avocados go from hard to overripe quickly.

Should tomatoes be refrigerated?

Generally no. Below about 50°F they lose aroma compounds and the flavor doesn't come back, and the texture turns mealy. Keep them at room temperature out of direct sun, and refrigerate only if a fully ripe tomato would otherwise spoil.

Why did my bananas turn black in the fridge?

Cold damages the peel, not the fruit. The skin blackens within hours while the flesh inside stays perfectly good for several more days. It's a cosmetic change and not a sign of spoilage.

How do I keep bananas from ripening so fast?

Separate them from the bunch and wrap the stem cluster tightly — the stem is where most ethylene escapes. Hang them if you can, and keep them away from other produce. This typically buys several extra days.

Can I store bananas and avocados together?

Only if you want both to ripen faster, since the banana is a strong ethylene emitter. Otherwise keep them apart, and keep both away from ethylene-sensitive items like leafy greens and broccoli.

What's the brown layer on a stored avocado half?

Oxidation, not spoilage. It's harmless to eat but has an off flavor, so scrape it off and use the green underneath. Prevent it by pressing wrap directly onto the cut surface.

Do tomatoes ripen better on the counter or in the sun?

Counter. Direct sunlight warms unevenly and can cause soft spots and faster spoilage. Room temperature out of direct light is the right condition, and a paper bag speeds it if you're in a hurry.

How long does a ripe avocado last in the fridge?

About 3 to 5 days whole and refrigerated once ripe, versus a day or two at room temperature. Cut avocado lasts 1 to 2 days if the surface is acid-treated and wrapped airtight.
