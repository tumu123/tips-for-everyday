How to Pack and Unpack a Move So You Can Find Things

Organize Home Systems Published October 24, 2026 13 min read

Moving advice is almost entirely about protecting things: wrap the plates, blanket the furniture, tape the drawers shut. All of it is correct and none of it solves the problem people actually complain about afterwards, which is that the new house is full of boxes and nobody knows which one has the kettle, the phone chargers, or the screws for the bed frame.

The fix is a labelling and sequencing system, decided before the first box is filled. It costs about an hour of setup and it removes most of the misery from the week after the move.

Why Room-by-Room Packing Fails

The default method is to pack one room at a time and write the room on the box. It feels organized and it produces a specific failure: everything for a room arrives at once, including things you will not need for six months. The box of winter bedding blocks the box of plates, the office is one enormous undifferentiated pile, and the first week is spent moving boxes rather than unpacking them.

The alternative is to sort by when you will need it, not by where it came from. Room still matters — it is the second axis, not the first.

Step 0: Pack the first-night box before anything else

Before the kitchen, before the books, before anything that requires a decision, fill one box (or one suitcase) with what the household needs to function for a single evening and the following morning.

CategoryContents
SleepBedding for each bed, pillows, one set of pyjamas each
BathTowels, soap, toilet paper, toothbrushes, one roll of bin bags
KitchenKettle, mugs, tea or coffee, one pan, one knife, plates and cutlery for the household, dish soap, one tea towel
TechPhone chargers, router and its power supply, one power strip, a lamp
ToolsBox cutter, screwdriver, tape, marker, Allen keys for flat-pack furniture
PaperID, lease or completion documents, a pen, the inventory sheet
ComfortSnacks, water, medication, something for a child or pet to do

This box travels with you, not in the van. It is the single highest-value hour in the whole move, because arriving at 9 p.m. with no bedding and no kettle turns a hard day into a crisis.

Step 1: Number the rooms before you own a box

Assign every room in the new place a number and a colour, and put the same list on the back of the front door and in your phone. Numbers work better than names because they sort, they are fast to write, and they do not shrink to unreadable when written on tape with a fat marker.

  • 1 Kitchen
  • 2 Main bedroom
  • 3 Second bedroom
  • 4 Bathroom
  • 5 Living room
  • 6 Hall, entry, and coats
  • 7 Office or desk area
  • 8 Storage, basement, or garage
  • 9 Garden and outdoor

The number goes on every box. The room’s destination is now a single digit rather than a word that has to be read at a glance while carrying something heavy.

Step 2: Pack by first-week use, not by room

Sort each room’s contents into three tiers as you pack, and label the tier. This is what makes unpacking possible.

TierWhat belongsWhen it gets unpacked
AUsed in the first three days: toiletries, everyday clothes, cooking basics, work kitNight one, before anything else
BUsed in the first month: most kitchen equipment, bedding for all beds, cleaning supplies, children’s toysFirst weekend
CEverything else: seasonal items, decor, archives, spare linens, hobby gearWhen the room is otherwise done, or not at all

Tier C is where the money is saved in time. A box marked C can go straight to the storage area unopened, and if it is still unopened after a year, that is information about whether the contents were needed at all.

An A-B-C split also changes what you pack first. Pack tier C first, while energy is high and decisions are easy, and leave tier A until the last day, when it is genuinely the only thing you still need.

Step 3: Keep one inventory sheet

One sheet of paper, one line per box, four columns: number, room, tier, short contents. Written by hand as boxes are sealed, photographed at the end of each packing session so a lost sheet is not a lost move.

#RoomTierContents
141 KitchenBSaucepans, lids, colander
151 KitchenCServing dishes, rarely used

The sheet does two jobs. It answers “where is the X” without opening forty boxes, and it is the only reliable record if something goes missing or a box is damaged. For anything valuable, add a fifth column or a separate note with a photo of the contents — which is also what an insurer will ask for if there is a claim, the same principle as documenting a house before damage happens rather than after.

Step 4: Label three faces of every box

One label on top is useless once boxes are stacked. Write the number, room, and tier on the top and two opposite sides, large enough to read from a metre away. Then add one word describing the contents — “kitchen pans”, “bedding”, “books”.

Three additional conventions save time at the other end:

  • Mark “OPEN FIRST” on the first-night box in a way that cannot be missed.
  • Mark fragile boxes with a single word, not a symbol. Arrow and glass symbols are ignored by tired people; the word is not.
  • Write the room number on the tape seam, so a box that gets re-taped is still identifiable.

Step 5: Build the open-first kit and the hardware bag

Beyond the first-night box, two small items prevent most day-one problems.

The open-first kit is a clear bag or small box with the things you will need to open other boxes: box cutter, scissors, screwdriver, tape, marker, spare batteries, a cloth. Without it, the first hour in the new house is spent looking for a knife.

The hardware bag is the more important one. Every time you dismantle furniture, put the screws, bolts, and Allen keys into a zip-top bag, label it with the item name, and tape the bag to the item itself rather than into a box. Furniture hardware in a box is hardware you will find in three weeks, after a trip to the hardware store.

Step 6: Load in reverse order of need

The van or truck is a stack, so the order it is loaded is the order it comes out. Load tier C first, at the front, then tier B, then tier A and the first-night box last, nearest the door.

If you are using movers, give them the numbers and tell them which rooms are priority. A professional crew will place boxes by number if the labels are clear, which is most of the reason for numbering in the first place.

Anything that cannot go in a box — mattresses, mirrors, plants, the things that must not be stacked — goes in last and comes out first.

Step 7: Unpack in a fixed order

Unpacking has a correct sequence, and it is not the same as the packing sequence. The order below is chosen so that each stage makes the next one possible.

OrderWhatWhy this position
1Beds made, bathrooms usableNothing else functions without sleep and a working bathroom
2Kitchen tier A, then BMeals stop being takeaway, which is the biggest hidden cost of a slow unpack
3Cleaning supplies and bin bagsYou cannot clean around boxes that are still sealed
4Clothes for the seasonRemoves the daily laundry emergency
5One living space, fully finishedA single completed room is what makes the house feel habitable
6Children’s rooms and toysReduces friction for everyone else
7Everything else, room by roomNow there is somewhere to put things

Two rules speed this up considerably. Finish one room before starting the next — half-unpacked rooms multiply the work. And put the empty boxes out as you go, flattened and stacked by the door, rather than letting them fill the hallway; a corridor of cardboard is the main reason people stop unpacking.

Step 8: Apply the thirty-day graveyard rule

After thirty days, whatever is still in a sealed box goes to a corner — garage, basement, spare room — labelled “unopened since the move”. Anything still sealed at the one-year mark gets opened once, in front of a bin bag, and 80 percent of it leaves the house.

This rule works because moving is the one moment when the cost of keeping something becomes visible. A box that survived a move unopened has already told you what it is worth.

Step 9: Close out both places

Two loose ends that cause real problems if left.

The old place: photograph every room empty, keep the photos until the deposit or sale is settled, and confirm the meter readings on the day you hand over the keys. A dated photo of an empty, clean room is the answer to most deposit disputes.

The new place: find the fuse box, the water shut-off, and the boiler controls in the first week, and photograph their positions. Then note where the stopcock is on the back of the inventory sheet, because nobody remembers it at the moment they need it.

What Not to Pack

Some items are not worth moving and a few are genuinely unsafe in a van.

  • Open liquids, paint, and chemicals. Half-used paint tins leak, and moving companies will not carry most of them.
  • Gas cylinders and propane. Not permitted in most vans, and dangerous in a hot vehicle.
  • Perishable food. Plan the fridge down to empty over the last week rather than moving it.
  • Anything you were already going to throw away. Use the move as the deadline.
  • Original documents you cannot replace. Passports, deeds, and certificates travel with you, not in the box marked “office”. How long to keep the rest is a separate question, covered in which home records to keep and for how long.

The Cost Side

Everything above is about time, and time is the part of a move that is not on the invoice. The money side — quotes, timing, materials, and the costs that hide until the last week — is covered in how to save money when moving house. The two fit together: a well-labelled move is also a cheaper one, because it uses fewer boxes, fewer packing materials, and less paid labour time.

The Prep That Makes All of It Easier

The single biggest variable in how hard a move is, is how much you own. Packing a house you have already decluttered takes half the boxes and produces a fourth of the decisions. If there is time before the move, the highest-value work is not wrapping plates — it is removing things you do not want to pay to transport, which is covered in decluttering without regret.

And if the new place is smaller than the old one, the storage problem has to be solved before the boxes are opened, not after. The systems for that are in small-space storage ideas.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far in advance should I start packing?

Six weeks for a household with children or a garage; three weeks for an apartment. Start with tier C — seasonal items, books, decor, archives — because those decisions are easiest and they remove the most volume before the stressful week arrives.

Should I pack myself or use movers?

Packing yourself is cheaper and slower; having movers pack is faster and less predictable in quality. A common compromise is to have movers handle the kitchen, glassware, and furniture, and to pack clothes, books, and garage contents yourself.

How many boxes does a typical house need?

A two-bedroom apartment is usually 40 to 60 boxes, a three-bedroom house 80 to 120. Buying in three sizes and keeping the count on the inventory sheet prevents the mid-move dash for more.

What is the fastest way to find something after a move?

The inventory sheet plus numbered rooms. If the sheet is current, the answer takes seconds. Without it, the only method is opening boxes until you find it, which is how a whole weekend disappears.

When should I unpack the last box?

After thirty days, stop treating unpacking as an active task and move the remainder into storage. A house reaches a liveable state in about two weeks; anything still boxed at a month is not going to unpack itself and does not need to.

Do I need to label every single box?

Every box gets a number, room, and tier. Not every box needs a full contents list — a word or two is enough for tier C, while tier A and anything valuable deserve a proper list and a photo.

What should I do with the old boxes afterwards?

Flatten and recycle them as you unpack rather than stacking them in a spare room. A box pile is the thing that makes an unpacked house feel unfinished, and most of it can leave the same week it was emptied.
