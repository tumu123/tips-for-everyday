How to Organize a Laundry Room (or a Laundry Closet)

Organize Laundry Published October 19, 2026 10 min read

Most laundry rooms are not short of storage. They are short of a sequence.

Stand in the doorway of a typical one and the problem is visible in ten seconds: a basket of dirty clothes balanced on the dryer, detergent on the floor because the shelf is too high, a pile of clean towels on the ironing board, and one sock that has been on the windowsill since spring. Nothing is in the wrong room. Everything is in the wrong order.

Laundry is a production line with six stages, and every stage needs somewhere for its output to land. When two stages share a surface, that surface fills up and the line jams — which is why buying more shelves so often fails. Shelves give you more places to put things down. They do not give you more places to move things through.

The fix is to arrange the room in the order the work actually happens, so a load enters dirty at one end and leaves folded at the other without ever doubling back.

Step 1: Write the flow down before you buy anything

Draw the room as a rectangle and mark six points on it:

  1. Arrival — where dirty laundry enters (usually the door).
  2. Sorting — where it is separated into loads.
  3. Washing — the machine.
  4. Drying — dryer, rod, or rack.
  5. Folding — a flat surface at standing height.
  6. Exit — where clean laundry waits to go back upstairs.

Now draw arrows in the order you actually work. In most rooms the arrows cross themselves at least twice, and one stage has no home at all. That missing stage is almost always folding, and it is the reason clean clothes live on the ironing board for three days.

You do not need to rebuild the room. You need to move one or two things so the arrows stop crossing. A three-hour project, most of it spent measuring.

Step 2: Put the dirty end at the door

The entrance point should be the first thing you touch, not the last. If the hamper sits at the far end of the room, every load gets carried past the washer and back — and in a narrow room that walk is repeated four or five times per load.

Three constraints on the hamper position:

  • It must be at the door, on the hinge side. A hamper directly behind a swinging door wastes the swing arc.
  • It must open without being moved. A lidded bin you have to drag out is a bin that stays closed with laundry on top.
  • It must hold one week for your household. Roughly 1.5 cubic feet per person per week in a temperate climate, more if you have kids in sports. A family of four needs about 6 cubic feet of dirty storage, which is two standard 40-liter hampers, not one.

If floor space is tight, a two-bag frame on a rolling base slides under a counter and pulls out far enough to load. That single item solves both the capacity and the access problem.

Step 3: Sort in place, not on the floor

Sorting on the floor is the single largest source of clutter in laundry rooms, because the floor is also the walking path. Every sort creates a pile you then have to step over.

The workable version is three fixed containers in a row, at the sorting point, labeled once and never moved: whites/lights, colors, and heavy (towels, denim, bedding). Three is enough for almost every household. Four or five sounds thorough and in practice means two get used and three become storage for odd socks.

A few rules that keep this station functional:

  • Sort at the machine, not at the bedroom. If clothes get sorted where they are taken off, you have two sorting stations and neither gets emptied.
  • Give each bin a maximum, not a target. When the colors bin is full, that is the next load — not a reason to start a fourth bin.
  • Keep a small lidded jar on top for pocket contents. Coins, receipts, and tissues pulled out before the wash prevent most of the damage people blame on the machine.

Step 4: Make the machine zone do two jobs

The top of a front-loading washer and dryer is the most underused surface in the house. It is at the exact height where you want to stack, pretreat, and eventually fold — but only if it is flat and continuous.

A single counter over both machines, cut to the room’s width, gives you:

  • Pretreat space at the point where stains are noticed, which is the only point where pretreatment actually happens.
  • Landing space for the load coming out of the washer, so wet clothes are not held in your arms while you hunt for the dryer.
  • A folding surface if you leave the last 20 inches clear and nothing else migrates onto it.

Two practical details: leave a 1-inch gap at the back for hoses and cords, and use a counter depth of 24 to 27 inches — deeper than that and you cannot reach the back of the shelf above it without climbing.

If you have a top-loading washer, the counter cannot span both machines. Put a narrow shelf over the dryer only, and accept that the washer lid is not a surface.

A machine that only fails on big loads is telling you about vibration rather than the motor — diagnosing a washer that shakes or will not spin covers what that points to.

Step 5: Solve drying before you need it

Drying capacity is the bottleneck in most laundry rooms and the least planned for. People buy a dryer, then discover that half their clothes should not go in it, and end up with a rod wedged across the doorway.

Plan for the volume, not the exception. A household of four generates roughly 20 to 25 linear feet of hanging clothes per week — delicates, shirts, workout wear, and anything with elastic. A single 4-foot rod holds about 8 feet of clothes if you hang them tightly, so you need either a longer run or a second rod.

Placement rules that matter more than the hardware:

  • Rod height around 66 to 70 inches. Higher and short people cannot reach; lower and long items pool on the floor.
  • At least 12 inches of clearance from the wall. Clothes need air on both sides or the side against the wall stays damp and smells.
  • Over the machines, never over the walking path. A rod across the doorway is a head injury waiting to happen.
  • A retractable line for occasional big days, mounted at the far end so it only appears when bedding is being dried.

Layout decides whether drying is a station or an improvisation. For load size, spacing, and the dehumidifier that makes it work, see the drying station the room needs.

Step 6: Build a folding surface at the right height

Folding standing up is comfortable at 36 to 40 inches from the floor, which is roughly counter height. Folding on a bed or a table works but requires carrying the load somewhere else and bringing it back — the doubling-back problem again.

If the counter over the machines is your folding surface, protect it from becoming a shelf. One rule: nothing is stored on the folding surface, ever. Not the detergent, not the lint bin, not the “to deal with later” pile. The moment it holds something, folding moves back to the bed and the room reverts.

For a laundry closet with no room for a counter, a wall-mounted drop-leaf table 30 inches wide is the standard solution. It folds flat against the wall and takes up 3 inches when closed.

Step 7: Give clean clothes an exit

This is the stage almost everyone omits, and it is why clean laundry becomes a permanent resident. The exit should be a container that matches the size of one load and can be carried in one hand — typically a 2-bushel basket or a canvas tote with handles.

Put it at the end of the flow, next to the folding surface, and treat it as a staging area with a time limit. The rule that works in practice: the basket leaves the room the same day it is filled. Not folded and put away immediately — just out of the laundry room, into bedrooms, where it will actually get dealt with.

A short hanging rod for items that go straight to a closet (jackets, dress shirts) is worth 18 inches of wall. It removes a whole category from the basket.

Step 8: Store supplies where you use them, not where they fit

Detergent, softener, and stain remover belong within arm’s reach of the machine door, at a height between your waist and your shoulder. That is a 14-inch-deep shelf at 48 to 54 inches, not the deep cabinet over the door that requires a step stool.

  • Keep only one open container of each product. Backstock goes on a high shelf or in another room; a laundry room with four bottles of detergent lined up is a storage problem disguised as readiness.
  • Measure the bottle height before you build the shelf. The tallest jugs are 11 to 12 inches; a 10-inch gap means the shelf above is unusable.
  • Put powder above liquid, always. A leaking powder box ruins everything below it; a leaking bottle only ruins the shelf it is on.
  • Keep bleach and ammonia on separate shelves, never adjacent. The failure mode is a spill mixing them, and the result is chlorine gas.

Proximity is what produces the accidental mix. For the three chemical families and where each belongs, see storing products by reaction rather than by height.

Step 9: Solve the laundry closet’s depth problem

A closet built for laundry is typically 60 to 63 inches wide and 27 to 30 inches deep — enough for machines but not for doors, hoses, and a person. The three changes that make the difference:

  1. Replace bifold doors with a curtain or nothing. Bifolds eat 20 inches of the swing and block access when open, which is when you need access most.
  2. Stack the machines if the closet is under 60 inches wide. A stacked pair frees the entire opposite wall for a 12-inch-deep cabinet — enough for detergent, lint bin, and stain kit.
  3. Use the space above the door. A shelf at 78 inches holds off-season items, spare hangers, and the iron, and is out of the working zone entirely.

One more: a closet with the door closed has almost no ventilation. Leave the washer door ajar and the closet door open for an hour after each load, or you will be cleaning mildew off the gasket within a month.

Step 10: Keep it running with a monthly pass

A laundry room fails slowly and then all at once. Ten minutes once a month prevents both:

  • Empty the lint filter housing with a vacuum nozzle, not just the screen.
  • Check the dryer vent hose for kinks and crush points behind the machine.
  • Run a hot cycle with a washing machine cleaner or 2 cups of white vinegar to control biofilm.
  • Clear the sorting bins completely — a bin that is never empty becomes a permanent resident.
  • Sweep behind and under the machines; that is where the missing socks live.

Why This Works

The room is organized around movement rather than around categories of objects. Every stage of the work has a designated landing place, so no stage’s output ends up on a surface that belongs to another stage. That is what stops the pile from forming — not more storage, but fewer collisions.

The secondary effect is that each stage becomes visible. When folding has a defined surface and the exit basket has a defined spot, a full basket reads as “unfinished work” instead of “normal.” Visible flow is what makes the system self-maintaining.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you organize a laundry room with no cabinets?

Use the wall above the machines. A 12-inch-deep shelf at 54 inches holds detergent within reach, and a second shelf at 78 inches holds backstock and the iron. Add a rail with S-hooks under the lower shelf for lint brushes, stain sticks, and a spray bottle. Total cost is often under $40 and it outperforms a cabinet because everything is visible.

What is the best layout for a small laundry room?

A single-wall layout, ordered door → sorting bins → washer → dryer → folding counter → exit basket. Keep the walking path at least 30 inches wide. If the room is under 5 feet wide, stack the machines and use the freed wall for shallow storage, which converts a cramped room into a corridor you can actually work in.

Should the washer be on the left or the right?

Put the washer closest to the sorting station and the dryer closest to the folding surface. Since most people are right-handed and load a front-loader with the right hand, a washer on the right of the pair feels more natural — but the flow order matters more than handedness. Never put the dryer between the door and the washer.

How many laundry baskets does a family of four need?

Three: two for dirty sorting (lights and colors) and one for clean staging. Add a fourth small bin for delicates if they are washed separately. More than that and you are storing laundry rather than moving it.

Where should you store laundry detergent?

Within arm's reach of the machine door, between waist and shoulder height, on a shelf no deeper than 14 inches. Liquid detergent degrades in heat and direct sun, so avoid a shelf above a south-facing window. Keep the bottle capped — the dispenser cup residue attracts dust and drips onto whatever is below.

How do you stop clean laundry from piling up?

Give it a container with a capacity limit and a rule that it must leave the room the day it fills. The pile forms because there is no defined endpoint; a basket at the end of the flow creates one. The second half of the fix is a folding surface that is never used for storage.

Is it worth putting a counter over the washer and dryer?

Yes, if the machines are front-loading and side by side. It creates pretreatment space, a landing zone for wet loads, and a folding surface in one move, and it costs less than a cabinet. Skip it with top-loaders, where the lid needs clearance, and skip it if the counter would be deeper than 27 inches, since you will not be able to reach the shelf above.
