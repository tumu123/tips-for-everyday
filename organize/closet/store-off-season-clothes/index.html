How to Store Off-Season Clothes (So They Last)

Organize Closet Published October 14, 2026 7 min read
Advertisement

Off-season storage fails for exactly two reasons: moisture and pests. Everything else — wrinkles, fading, odors — follows from those, or from a third problem: packing clothing that was not clean to begin with.

Get three rules right and clothing comes out of storage in the same condition it went in:

  1. Store it clean — never pack worn clothing
  2. Store it dry — moisture causes mold, mildew, and yellowing
  3. Store it sealed against pests — but not in a material that traps humidity

This guide covers the method, the materials, and the mistakes that ruin stored clothing.

Step 0: Never store dirty clothing (this rule is absolute)

This is the single most important rule, and it is the one most often broken.

Why worn clothing must be washed first:

  • Body oils and sweat oxidize over months, producing yellow stains, especially on whites and light colors. These stains often cannot be removed later.
  • Food and beverage residue attracts insects and grows mold.
  • Perfume and deodorant can discolor fabric and set over time.
  • Skin cells and oils are a food source for moth larvae.

Store it laundered and completely dry. Not “mostly dry.” Not “I’ll wash it when I take it out.” Completely.

Cleaning by fabric type

FabricCleaning before storage
CottonWash per label, dry fully

Down and down-alternativeWash per label and dry thoroughly — down holds moisture and molds
SilkDry clean
LeatherClean and condition before storing
SyntheticsWash per label; they dry quickly
KnitwearWash gently, dry flat to avoid stretching

Down jackets deserve special attention: if down is packed even slightly damp, it grows mildew and develops a lasting smell. Dry it completely, and if you are unsure, dry it longer.

Step 1: Choose the container by clothing type

There is no single best container. Match it to the item.

ContainerBest forAvoid for

Vacuum-seal bagsBulky items: comforters, bulky sweaters, puffer jacketsWool, silk, delicates, anything with structure
Cotton bags or pillowcasesWool, silk, knits, natural fibersLong-term protection from moisture
Cardboard boxesNothing long termEverything — they hold moisture and attract pests
Garment bagsSuits, coats, formal wear on hangersFolded items

Why vacuum bags are not universal

Vacuum bags save a large amount of space and are excellent for bulky non-delicate items. But:

  • Wool and natural fibers need some airflow — compressing them tightly and sealing for months can cause fiber damage and creasing.
  • Anything with structure — a blazer, a structured hat — will be permanently creased.
  • Down is generally fine to compress, but only when completely dry.

The cardboard rule: never store clothing long term in cardboard. Cardboard absorbs and holds moisture from the air, and it is a food source and hiding place for insects. It is fine for a short move, not for a season.

Step 2: Fold and pack correctly

How you pack determines how it looks when it comes out.

  1. Fold along existing seams and creases rather than inventing new folds. Garments hold the folds they were stored in.
  2. Avoid folding along the same line every season — this eventually creates permanent creases. Rotate fold positions.
  3. Line fold points of delicate or prone-to-creasing items with acid-free tissue paper.
  4. Do not overstuff bins. Overfilled bins stress the fabric and produce deep creases. Leave a little air.
  5. Put heavier items at the bottom and lighter ones on top so nothing is crushed.
  6. Roll knits rather than folding to avoid fold lines. Rolled knits also take less space.
  7. Hang structured items — suits, coats, formal dresses — rather than folding them, in a garment bag.

Sweaters: fold, do not hang. Hanging a sweater stretches the shoulders permanently.

Suits: brush, then hang on a proper hanger with the jacket and trousers separated, inside a breathable garment bag. Do not seal a suit in plastic.

Step 3: Add pest and moisture protection correctly

This is where most storage fails, and it usually fails because the wrong products were used.

What works

ProtectionHow to use it
Cedar blocks or cedar chipsPlace in the bin. Cedar oil repels some pests and smells pleasant. Refresh by lightly sanding the wood
Lavender sachetsRepels some insects; pleasant scent
Moisture absorbers (silica gel packs)Place in bins in humid climates
Breathable cotton bagsFor items needing airflow
Sealed bins with gasketsFor genuine pest protection in a humid environment

What does not work, or is dangerous

  • Mothballs — contain toxic chemicals that are hazardous to people and pets. They are also a respiratory irritant. Cedar and lavender are the modern, safer alternatives, though they are less potent.
  • Plastic dry cleaner bags — these trap moisture and solvents, and can permanently discolor clothing. Never store anything in a dry cleaner’s plastic bag.
  • Air-tight sealing of slightly damp clothing — guaranteed mold.

The three-layer approach that works best:

  1. Clean and dry the clothing.
  2. Place in a breathable cotton bag for natural fibers, or a clean bin for synthetics.
  3. Add cedar or lavender inside, and keep the bin closed but not vacuum-sealed unless the item is bulky and non-delicate.

Where to store matters as much as how: a dry, cool, dark location. Avoid attics, which cycle through high heat, and basements, which are damp. Under a bed or on a high closet shelf is ideal.

For under-bed storage, measure the clearance first — typically 6 to 10 inches. Flat bins fit where taller ones do not, and a bin that does not slide out cleanly will not get used.

Step 5: Label and track

Labels turn a storage bin from a mystery into a usable resource.

  • Label the visible face, not the top of the bin.
  • List the contents, not just the category: “Winter — wool sweaters, heavy socks, gloves” beats “Winter.”
  • Add the season so you know when to retrieve it.
  • Photograph the packed bin if you keep a photo album — useful for remembering what is where.

For multiple bins, number them and keep a list on your phone. In six months, the list is what lets you find the specific item instead of opening every bin.

Step 6: The seasonal swap (this is the maintenance)

Twice a year, do the swap. It should take about an hour if the system is in place.

  1. Retrieve the incoming season’s bins.
  2. Unpack and inspect each item. Check for stains, damage, or pest evidence. Deal with problems now, not when you want to wear the item.
  3. Launder or dry clean anything that needs it before it goes into rotation.
  4. Pack the outgoing season following Steps 0 through 3.
  5. Add fresh cedar or lavender.
  6. Update the labels and the bin list.

Check for pest damage when you unpack. Look for small holes, especially in wool and silk, and for the fine webbing or shed skins that indicate larvae. Finding it in the fall is far better than discovering it in a garment you were about to wear.

Shoes deserve a note: footwear should be cleaned before storage, especially winter boots with road salt residue. Salt residue draws moisture and can degrade leather and rubber seals over a season.

Why This Works

Stored clothing degrades through three mechanisms, and each has a specific countermeasure.

Chemical degradation comes from residues left on fabric. Body oils, sweat, perfume, and food oxidize over months into compounds that stain and weaken fibers. Washing removes the reactants, which is why clean storage is non-negotiable and why yellowed whites are almost always the result of storing worn clothing.

Biological degradation comes from mold and insects. Both need moisture and a food source. Drying the item removes the moisture, cleaning removes the food, and cedar or lavender plus a sealed container blocks access.

Physical degradation comes from compression, hanging stress, and light. Wool and down lose loft when compressed for months, which is why vacuum sealing natural fibers is counterproductive. Sweaters stretch permanently on hangers. Light fades dye and weakens fibers, which is why dark storage matters.

The material choices follow directly. Cardboard fails because it is hygroscopic — it absorbs and releases moisture, feeding mold. Dry cleaner plastic fails because it traps residual solvent and moisture against the fabric. Breathable cotton bags for natural fibers allow slight moisture exchange, which prevents the condensation that a sealed bag in a varying temperature would produce.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really have to wash clothes before storing them? Yes. Body oils and sweat oxidize over months and cause yellowing that often cannot be removed afterward, and residue attracts insects. This is the highest-impact rule in off-season storage.

How do I prevent moths in stored clothes? Clean items thoroughly, store them in sealed bins or breathable bags with cedar or lavender, and inspect at each seasonal swap. Cedar and lavender are safer than mothballs. Pay attention to wool and silk, which moths prefer.

Are vacuum-seal bags safe for all clothing? No. They are excellent for bulky, non-delicate items such as comforters, puffer jackets, and bulky synthetics. Avoid them for wool, silk, knits, and anything structured, because compression damages fibers and creates permanent creases.

Can I store clothes in cardboard boxes? Not long term. Cardboard absorbs and holds moisture from the air and provides harborage for insects. Use clear plastic bins or cotton bags instead.

Where is the best place to store off-season clothes? A cool, dry, dark place. Under a bed or on a high closet shelf is ideal. Avoid attics, which reach extreme temperatures, and basements, which are damp.

Should I keep the dry cleaner’s plastic bag on a stored suit? No. Those bags trap residual solvent and moisture against the fabric and can cause discoloration. Remove the plastic and use a breathable garment bag instead.

How do I store a wool coat? Have it cleaned, then hang it in a breathable garment bag with cedar or lavender inside, in a cool dark location. Never store it in plastic, and never fold it tightly enough to crease the shoulders.

What if I find moth damage when I unpack? Inspect the whole bin. Discard anything badly damaged, launder or dry clean the rest, and clean the bin before reusing it. Check for larvae and webbing, and add fresh cedar. Isolate suspect items before they go back into your closet.

How do I store shoes and boots seasonally? Clean them first — especially winter boots with road salt residue. Stuff them to hold their shape, store them in a bin with cedar, and keep boots upright. Salt residue draws moisture and degrades leather and seals over time.
