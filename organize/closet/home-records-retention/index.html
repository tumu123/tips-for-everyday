Which Home Records to Keep (and for How Long)

Organize Closet Published October 24, 2026 11 min read

Households tend to fail at records in both directions at once. There is a drawer of utility bills from 2014 that nobody will ever look at again, and somewhere in the same house there is a missing receipt for a 9,000 dollar roof that would have reduced a capital gains bill.

The waste is not really about space. It is about which paper survives. Retention is a decision made once per document type, and once it is written down the drawer stops being a judgment call.

The Two Clocks

Every record runs on two clocks, and they are different lengths.

The legal or tax clock is how long the document could still be demanded — by a tax authority, an insurer, a court, or a buyer. This is usually three to seven years, and it is the reason you cannot simply shred anything old.

The useful-life clock is how long the document still does something for you: proves a warranty, supports an insurance claim, settles a deposit, answers a question about the house.

Keep the longer of the two. A roof receipt has a useful life of about a week and a legal life of decades, because it is evidence of a capital improvement for as long as you own the house and for a few years after you sell it.

The Retention Table

This is the whole system in one place. Print it, keep it in the front of the box, and stop deliberating.

Two simplifications cover most households that do not want to think about it: seven years for anything financial, and forever for anything that proves ownership of an asset. Everything else is a one-year question or a life-of-the-product question.

The Capital Improvement Folder

If one folder in the house deserves to be treated carefully, it is this one. It is also the one almost nobody has.

Every improvement that adds value or prolongs the life of the house — a roof, a furnace, a bathroom remodel, new windows, a deck, a driveway, solar panels, insulation — is capital, not repair. When the house is eventually sold, those costs adjust the basis and reduce the taxable gain. In a house owned for twenty years, that adjustment is frequently larger than the gain itself, which means the folder can be worth more than the roof.

What goes in per project:

  • The contract and the final invoice, showing what was actually paid.
  • The permit number, if one was required.
  • Before and after photos, dated.
  • The warranty document, and who holds it.
  • The date completed, and the address if the project was at a previous property.

Two rules make the folder work. One project, one envelope or one PDF — not a shoebox of mixed receipts. And record the project even when you did the work yourself: materials receipts count, and labour does not, which means the receipts are the entire record.

This is where records and money meet. The time to establish the folder is before a project starts, and the same documentation habit pays off in a different direction when something goes wrong and you need to file a home insurance claim — the difference between a fast, full settlement and a slow, argued one is almost always paperwork that already existed.

Digital Copies Are Fine — With Three Exceptions

Scanning is the correct default. A searchable PDF in one folder, backed up in two places, beats a filing cabinet for almost every document type in the table above.

The exceptions, where the original matters:

  1. Estate documents and powers of attorney. A scanned will is not a will in most jurisdictions. The signed original lives somewhere fire-resistant and known to the executor.
  2. Deeds, titles, and notarised documents. Keep the original for as long as you own the asset. A scan is a convenience copy.
  3. Anything with a physical requirement attached — vehicle titles with a signature panel, some immigration documents, certificates that must be produced in original form.

For everything else, keep the scan and shred the paper after the retention period. A digital archive also solves the problem paper cannot: it is copyable, so one copy can live offsite and one in the cloud, and a fire does not end the record.

If you are scanning a backlog, do it by category rather than chronologically — all the medical records, then all the warranties, then the tax years — and name files with the date first so they sort correctly. A year of scanned receipts in random order is not an archive, it is a second pile.

The One-Page Index

The most useful document in the whole system is a single page that says where everything else is.

QuestionAnswer on the index
Where are the deeds and title?Location, and whether original or scan
Where is the insurance policy and the home inventory?Folder name and storage location
Where are the capital improvement records?Folder or box, by address
Where are the tax returns?Years covered, physical or digital
Who has a copy of the will?Name, and where the original is held
Where are the warranties still in force?Folder, plus expiry dates worth knowing
Who are the utility and service providers?Account numbers, for a house sale or an emergency

One page, kept with the important documents and copied to whoever would have to run the house in an emergency. It takes twenty minutes to write and it is the difference between a records system and a records hoard.

What to Shred, and What Never to Shred

Shred anything with an account number, a signature, a date of birth, or a full name and address that is leaving the house. That covers bank statements, medical letters, utility bills, pre-approved credit offers, and anything with a barcode tied to an account.

A cross-cut shredder is enough. Burning is not necessary and is a fire risk; tearing is not enough for anything with an account number.

Never shred: deeds, titles, estate documents, notarised papers, the current year’s tax documents, or anything you have not yet scanned and verified as readable. Verify the scan before the paper goes — a blurry scan and a shredded original is the same as no document at all.

Records You Inherit

Two situations catch people out.

Records from a previous owner. When you buy a house, ask for the permit history, the improvement records, and any warranties that transfer. These are legitimately valuable — a twenty-year-old furnace warranty occasionally transfers — and they cost nothing to request during the sale. Once you have them, they join your capital improvement folder under the previous owner’s projects with the date noted.

Records from someone who has died. Do not shred anything for at least a year. Tax filings, insurance, and probate can all reach back, and the paper that looks meaningless in month one frequently matters in month eight. Box it, label it by year, and revisit once the estate is closed.

Records for a Sale

When the house goes on the market, the records system gets a second job: disclosure. Buyers and their lawyers will ask about permits, repairs, water damage, and HOA rules, and a seller who can produce documents answers in an afternoon what otherwise takes weeks and costs credibility.

Before listing, assemble: permits and certificates, the capital improvement folder, service records for the boiler and any major system, the survey, HOA documents, and any insurance claims from the last five years. This is also the moment the property tax assessment becomes relevant again, because an assessment that looks wrong at sale is worth appealing rather than absorbing.

The Twenty-Minute Annual Pass

Once a year, ideally when the tax return is filed, run one pass:

  1. Shred everything past its retention date. The table tells you which years are now safe.
  2. Scan the year’s paper into the right folder, named by date.
  3. Update the capital improvement folder with anything completed in the past twelve months.
  4. Update the insurance home inventory with significant purchases and anything sold.
  5. Check the index page still describes reality.

Twenty minutes, once a year, keeps the drawer from becoming a project. The system that fails is not the one with the wrong retention rule — it is the one nobody opens for four years.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should I keep tax returns?

Seven years is the safe answer. Three years is the standard audit window, six applies where income was understated by more than 25 percent, and seven covers claims involving bad debts or losses. Keeping seven years of returns and their supporting documents covers every case without needing to remember which rule applies.

Do I need to keep paper utility bills?

No, beyond twelve months, and even those are only useful for comparison. Download the annual summary if the provider offers one and let the monthly bills go.

Should I keep receipts for home improvements if I did the work myself?

Yes, and they are the only records that exist in that case. Materials receipts establish the cost basis; your own labour does not count. Keep them with the project, dated, until at least three years after the house is sold.

Is a scanned copy of a deed legally valid?

It is useful evidence but it is not the same as the original in most jurisdictions, and a title company or court may require the original. Keep the paper deed, keep a scan as backup, and store the original somewhere fire-resistant.

What should I do with old insurance policies?

Keep each policy until it is replaced, then keep it one more year. Liability claims and disputes can surface after a policy period ends, and the policy language in force at the time of the loss is what governs.

How long do I keep appliance manuals and warranties?

For the life of the appliance. Write the purchase date and the warranty expiry on the front of the manual, and keep the receipt with it. When the appliance leaves the house, both go with it or into recycling.

What if I am not sure whether to keep something?

Scan it, then decide. A digital copy costs nothing to store, and the decision about the paper becomes easy once the content is safe. The only documents that should never be reduced to a scan alone are the three listed above.
