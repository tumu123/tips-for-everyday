How to Organize Under the Sink (and Keep It That Way)

Organize Small Spaces Published October 14, 2026 6 min read

The under-sink cabinet is the most difficult storage space in a home. It has obstructions (a drain trap, supply lines, a disposal), poor access (a low, deep, dark space), and moisture (leaks and humidity from the sink above).

The approach that works:

  1. Use two-tier sliding drawers to bypass the pipes and use the center of the cabinet
  2. Line the cabinet floor with a waterproof mat to protect against leaks
  3. Categorize into bins so you pull out a group rather than digging for one bottle
  4. Mount the door to reclaim that surface

The most important design decision is the two-tier drawer: the pipes occupy the center of the cabinet, so a flat-bottomed organizer straddling them is the only way to make that space work. The top drawer sits above the horizontal pipe; the bottom drawer slides beneath it.

Step 0: Measure Around the Obstructions

This step determines every product decision. Measure before buying, or you will buy an organizer that almost fits.

  1. Measure the cabinet interior — width, depth, and height (floor to the underside of the counter).
  2. Measure the obstructions:
    • The height of the bottom of the drain trap from the cabinet floor
    • The depth of the trap assembly from the back wall
    • The position and thickness of any vertical supply lines
    • Whether a disposal occupies space to one side
  3. Sketch a simple top-down diagram with the obstruction positions marked.

Typical two-tier drawer sizing: Look for a set where the bottom drawer is short enough to slide under the trap and the top drawer is narrow enough to clear the trap’s vertical height. Because trap position varies, most commercially available under-sink organizers are designed with this constraint in mind — but verify against your measurements.

The alternative if a two-tier rack will not fit: Use two small bins placed either side of the trap, plus a slim pull-out on one side. This is less elegant but works in especially awkward cabinets.

Step 1: Empty and Assess Everything

  1. Remove everything from the cabinet.
  2. Inspect for moisture damage. Look for dark staining, warped wood, or a musty smell. These indicate a current or past leak.
  3. Check all connections — the drain trap, the supply lines at the shutoff valves, and the disposal connection. Tighten anything loose. Address a leak before organizing, because a leak will ruin whatever you store there.
  4. Check the cabinet floor and back wall for mold. If there is mold, treat it before proceeding. See our caulk and mold guide for the method.
  5. Sort contents into four groups:
    • Used weekly or more (dish soap, sponges, trash bags)
    • Used monthly (specialty cleaners, scrub brushes, drain cleaner)
    • Used seasonally or rarely (holiday cleaning items, extra sponges in bulk)
    • Should not be here (food, kitchen tools, paper goods, chemical products that are hazardous with others)

Important safety note on chemical storage: Never store chlorine bleach and ammonia-based cleaners in the same undivided space. If they leak and mix, the result is toxic gas. If you must keep both, put them in separate bins with lids, or store one elsewhere entirely.

Step 2: Line the Cabinet Floor

A waterproof liner is the highest-value item in this project. It protects the cabinet from leaks and small drips, and it can identify leaks early.

  1. Choose a waterproof liner — a vinyl or rubber shelf liner, a shallow plastic tray, or a purpose-made cabinet mat.
  2. Cut to fit around the pipes. Leave a small margin but allow water to pool rather than run off the edge.
  3. Use a light color if possible. A white or light gray liner makes leaks and spills immediately visible, which means catching a problem early.

Note: Some guides recommend peel-and-stick paper liners. Avoid these under a sink — the adhesive bonds to the cabinet, and when moisture inevitably gets behind them, the adhesive lifts and hides the damage.

Step 3: Install a Two-Tier Sliding Drawer

  1. Follow the manufacturer’s installation instructions. Most require attaching rails or brackets to the cabinet floor or back wall.
  2. Position the unit to straddle the trap, not in front of it. The top drawer passes over the horizontal pipe; the bottom drawer slides beneath the vertical section.
  3. Check the drawer travel before loading anything. Pull both drawers fully out and confirm the door can close, the drawers do not hit the trap, and nothing collides with the supply lines.
  4. Test with the cabinet door closed. A drawer that clears when open may still be blocked by the closed door.

If drilling into the cabinet: Use a drill-bit sized to the provided screws, drill pilot holes, and avoid drilling near the pipes. Do not drill into a pipe.

Step 4: Categorize and Load the Drawers

Load by frequency of use, not by category — the same principle as any high-efficiency storage.

DrawerContents
Top drawer (easiest to reach)Dish soap, hand soap, sponges, scrubber, trash bags, towel
Bottom drawerDrain cleaner, specialty cleaners, extra sponges and refills, rubber gloves
Bins beside the trapBackstock and bulk items

Within a drawer:

  1. Use a small divider or bin inside the drawer so bottles do not tip and roll.
  2. Put the most-used item at the front.
  3. Stand bottles upright. Lying a bottle down means it leaks.
  4. Keep caps tight and check them whenever you reload the drawer.

If a two-tier drawer will not fit:

  1. Place two clear bins on either side of the trap, using the clear space.
  2. Add a slim vertical bin or a hook rack on the inside of the cabinet door.
  3. Keep the lowest-frequency items at the deepest point — you will reach them rarely.

Step 5: Use the Cabinet Door

The door interior is usable and surprisingly high-value.

  1. A door-mounted organizer holds sponges, brushes, gloves, or trash bags.
  2. Adhesive hooks hold spray bottles by their trigger, which saves shelf space.
  3. Measure the depth of the door-mounted items so they clear the drawer or bins inside when the door closes. This is the most common failure of door-mounted storage — the door will not shut.
  4. Avoid mounting heavy items on the door — the hinges will sag over time and the door will stop closing properly.

Step 6: Handle Moisture and Odors

  1. Check the trap connections periodically. A slow drip is easy to miss and eventually damages the cabinet.
  2. Ventilate the cabinet — leave the door slightly ajar after use, or install a small vent if the space is especially damp.
  3. Use a moisture absorber in humid climates, replaced regularly, if mustiness persists.
  4. Never store cleaning chemicals and food in the same location. Some cleaners release vapors that are absorbed by food.
  5. Remove expired products. Cleaning products do lose effectiveness over time, and expired items take up prime space.
  6. Keep a small drip tray under any bottle that has leaked before — glass bottles that have cracked before, or any that previously dripped.

The bathroom is the worst room in the house for most medicines — organizing a medicine cabinet safely covers safer storage.

Step 7: Build the Maintenance Habit

  1. Weekly, ten seconds: Return anything you used to its bin, and check that all caps are tight.
  2. Monthly, one minute: Wipe the drawer surface and check for drips under the trap.
  3. Quarterly, three minutes: Pull out the drawers, check the back of the cabinet for moisture, and discard anything expired or unused for a year.
  4. Annually, ten minutes: Pull everything out, wipe the liner, inspect all connections, and check the cabinet floor for staining.

The under-sink cabinet’s defining constraint is the drain trap, which occupies the center of the space and blocks ordinary storage. Every part of this system is designed around that constraint — which is why the two-tier drawer is the central element and why measuring the trap before buying anything matters more than the aesthetic choices.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I organize under-sink storage with pipes in the way?

Use a two-tier sliding drawer designed to straddle the trap. The top drawer passes over the horizontal pipe section and the bottom slides beneath. Measure the height of the trap from the cabinet floor and the depth of the assembly before buying, and verify the drawer travel clears the closed cabinet door.

What should I keep under the sink?

Cleaning supplies used in the kitchen, sponges, dish soap, trash bags, rubber gloves, and drain cleaner. Do not store food, kitchen tools, paper goods, or anything that moisture can damage.

Is it safe to store bleach and ammonia under the sink?

Not in the same undivided space. If both leak and mix, the combination produces toxic chloramine gas. If you must keep both, store them in separate lidded bins, or keep one in a different room entirely. Also never mix them when cleaning.

How do I stop the under-sink cabinet from getting musty?

Fix the moisture source first — check the trap and supply connections for drips. Then line the cabinet with a waterproof mat, keep the door slightly ajar to allow air circulation, and use a moisture absorber in humid climates. Mustiness is almost always an unresolved leak or a lack of airflow.

Should I use a pull-out drawer or bins?

A pull-out drawer is best in a kitchen sink cabinet because it addresses the pipe obstruction directly. Bins work better in a bathroom vanity, where pipes are usually at the back and the cabinet is shallower, or where a pull-out does not fit.

How do I keep the cabinet door storage from blocking the drawer?

Measure the depth of both the door-mounted items and the drawer pull-out, and confirm they clear each other when the door is closed. Door-mounted storage failing to clear interior items is the most common reason the door will not shut after organizing.

What is the best type of cabinet liner for under a sink?

A waterproof vinyl or rubber liner, or a shallow plastic tray, in a light color so leaks show immediately. Avoid adhesive paper liners — moisture reaches behind the adhesive and hides damage while the adhesive fails.

How do I organize a bathroom vanity under-sink cabinet?

The same principles apply but the cavity is usually shallower with pipes at the rear. Use two bins placed either side of the plumbing, a slim stackable drawer in front of the pipes at floor level, or a simple two-tier riser. The moisture concern is identical, so line the floor and check connections regularly.
