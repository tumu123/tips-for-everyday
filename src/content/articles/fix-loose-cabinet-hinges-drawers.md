A cabinet door that sags or will not stay closed is one of the most satisfying repairs in a house, because the cause is nearly always the same and the fix takes minutes.

**It is almost never the hinge.** The hinge is a steel part that outlasts the cabinet. What fails is the connection between the hinge and the cabinet — the screw holes in particleboard or MDF, which is what most cabinet boxes are made from.

**There are three failure modes, and they need different fixes:**

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Screws spin and will not tighten | Stripped holes in particleboard | Fill the hole and re-drive, or use a longer screw |
| Door aligned but will not stay shut | Hinge cam out of adjustment, or a worn soft-close damper | Adjust the cam, or replace the hinge |
| Door physically hits the frame | Hinge arm bent, or the cabinet is out of square | Adjust or straighten; check the cabinet itself |

Diagnosing which one you have takes about thirty seconds and saves you from replacing parts you do not need.

## Step 0: Find Out What Is Actually Loose

Open the door and lift it gently at the far edge, near the handle.

**If the door moves at the hinge side**, the [hinge screws](/fix-it/home-repair/fix-squeaky-door-hinge/) are loose or stripped. This is the common case.

**If the door is firm at the hinge but does not latch**, it is an adjustment problem, not a fastener problem.

**If the door rubs the frame on one side**, check the hinge arm for bending and the cabinet for square.

While you are here, tighten every hinge screw on every door in the kitchen — not just the loose one. They are all the same age and they all work loose at about the same rate. Doing them all at once takes ten extra minutes and prevents a repeat visit in three months.

## Step 1: Tighten First, Always

Before filling anything, try simply tightening.

Use the correct driver bit. A bit that is slightly the wrong size cams out and makes the screw head worse, which turns a tightening job into a stripped-head job.

**Tighten by hand if you can.** A drill on high torque strips holes that were merely loose, and it is very easy to overdrive into particleboard.

If a screw tightens and holds, you are done with that screw. Move to the next.

## Step 2: Fix a Stripped Hole — Two Reliable Methods

If the screw spins without gripping, the hole is stripped. Choose based on how bad it is.

**Method A — longer screw.** If the screw spins but the cabinet side is intact behind it, a screw 6 to 10 mm longer will often reach fresh material and bite. This is the fastest fix and works more often than people expect.

**Method B — fill and re-drive.** This is the fix that lasts.

Remove the hinge. Squirt wood glue into the hole, then tap in a wooden dowel, a golf tee, or several wooden matches cut to length. Pack it tight.

Let the glue set fully — an hour is usually enough, but check the label. Trim the filler flush with a knife.

Re-drill a **pilot hole** with an awl or a small drill bit, then drive the original screw in.

**Why the pilot hole matters:** particleboard splits easily, and driving a screw into a freshly plugged hole without a pilot tends to split the plug apart rather than grip it. The awl creates the path so the screw threads cut cleanly.

Method B is more work than Method A, and it is the one that survives repeated opening.

## Step 3: For European Hinges, Use the Adjustment Cams

Most modern cabinets use concealed European hinges with two or three adjustment screws. These are not looseness fixes — they are alignment controls, and they are why a door can be firmly attached and still not fit right.

With the hinge screws tight:

- **The screw nearest the door** moves the door sideways within the frame — this controls the gap between doors
- **The screw furthest from the door** moves the door in and out — this controls how proud the door face sits
- **The screw on the mounting plate** moves the door up and down — this controls height relative to the neighbouring door

**Adjust a quarter turn at a time** and check after each. These are fine-pitch adjustments, and it is easy to overshoot.

If the hinge has no adjustment screws, it is an older face-frame hinge, and alignment there is done by shimming behind the hinge leaf with thin cardboard.

## Step 4: Check the Soft-Close Damper

If the door is aligned and tight but slams or will not stay closed, the soft-close mechanism may be the culprit.

Look at the hinge arm for a small piston. If the door closes hard or will not hold shut, that damper has likely failed.

**A failed damper is not adjustable and not repairable** — the hinge gets replaced. This is the one case where buying a hinge is correct.

Replacement is straightforward: release the hinge from the mounting plate, remove the plate screws, and fit the new hinge. Match the existing type — the overlay measurement matters, and a hinge with the wrong overlay will not let the door close.

## Step 5: Fix a Sticky Drawer

Drawers stick for different reasons than doors.

**First, determine whether it is the drawer or the slide.** Pull the drawer completely out and look at the slides. If they are bent, rusted, or the ball bearings have escaped their race, replace them — that is not adjustable.

If the slides are fine, the usual causes are:

- **The drawer is overloaded.** Drawer slides have a weight rating, and a heavy drawer flexes the box so it binds. Remove half the contents and test.
- **The drawer box is racking.** Push on the sides. If the box flexes, it is out of square and will bind under load.
- **The slides are dirty.** Clean the tracks and the wheels, then dry-lubricate.

## Step 6: Lubricate Correctly

**Use dry lubrication on drawer slides: paraffin wax, a candle, or silicone spray.**

Do not use oil or grease. Wet lubricants attract dust and lint, and over a few months turn into a sticky paste that makes the drawer worse than it was. This is the single most common mistake people make with a sticky drawer.

Wax the slide surfaces and the wheels, then run the drawer in and out several times to distribute it.

If the slide has exposed ball bearings, clean them first with a dry cloth and check for flat spots or missing bearings.

## Step 7: Check the Cabinet Itself

If a door binds after all the adjustments are correct, look at the cabinet.

Check for square with a small level on the face frame, and look at whether the cabinet is still firmly attached to the wall. A cabinet that has pulled away from its mounting screws will rack, and no amount of hinge adjustment fixes that.

**Also check for water damage under sinks.** A cabinet base that has swollen from a leak will push the box out of square, and that is a moisture problem before it is a hardware problem.

## Why This Works

The repair works because the failure is at the interface, not in the mechanism.

European hinges are adjustable in three axes for exactly this reason: cabinets are built from materials that move, and a hinge that could not be adjusted would need replacing constantly. The cams are there to absorb that movement, which is why step 3 fixes doors that are firmly attached but badly fitted.

The stripped-hole fix works because particleboard's weakness is that it has no grain to grip. A screw in particleboard holds by compressing the material around its threads, and once that compression fails, the same screw in the same hole will never grip again. Filling with glued wood replaces the failed material with something that behaves like solid wood, and the pilot hole lets the screw cut fresh threads rather than wedge the plug apart.

Dry lubrication works because drawer slides are open to dust. The choice of lubricant is not about reducing friction on day one — it is about not accumulating grit by day ninety.

And the reason to do all the hinges at once is simple: they were installed together, they carry similar loads, and particleboard fatigue is a function of cycles. Fixing only the visibly loose one means the next one fails in a few months.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not assume the hinge is broken and replace it.** The hinge is usually fine. The screw holes are the problem.

**Do not use a drill on high torque to tighten cabinet screws.** It strips holes that were only loose. Tighten by hand.

**Do not use the wrong driver bit.** It cams out and ruins the screw head.

**Do not use oil or grease on drawer slides.** It collects dust and becomes sticky. Use wax or silicone.

**Do not skip the pilot hole when re-driving into a plugged hole.** The plug splits and you are back where you started.

**Do not try to adjust a failed soft-close damper.** Replace the hinge.

**Do not ignore water damage under a sink.** A swollen cabinet is a moisture problem, and hardware will not fix it.

**Do not overtighten into particleboard.** Snug is enough; more than that strips.

## FAQ

**Why do my cabinet screws keep coming loose?**
Repeated opening cycles fatigue the particleboard around the threads. Once it loosens once, it will loosen again unless the hole is filled.

**Can I use a bigger screw instead?**
Yes, if there is solid material behind to bite into. A screw of the same gauge but longer is usually better than a thicker one, which can split the board.

**Do I need to remove the door to fix a stripped hole?**
Usually yes for the fill method — you need the hinge off to work on the hole. Supporting the door with a helper or a shim underneath makes it much easier.

**How do I know if my hinge needs replacing?**
If the hinge arm is bent, the knuckle is cracked, the spring no longer holds, or the soft-close damper has failed. Alignment problems are adjustable; mechanical failures are not.

**Why does my drawer stick only when it is full?**
Weight rating. A loaded drawer flexes the box and binds the slides. Either lighten it or fit slides with a higher rating.

**What is the overlay measurement on a hinge?**
How far the door covers the cabinet face. Matching it matters — a hinge with the wrong overlay will not let the door close properly. Bring the old hinge when buying a replacement.

**Can I fix this without any tools?**
Tightening with a screwdriver is the entire job in many cases. Start there before assuming you need anything else.

**How long does the dowel-and-glue repair last?**
Years, in normal use. It replaces failed particleboard with solid wood, which grips threads properly.
