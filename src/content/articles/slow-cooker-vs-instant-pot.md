Most comparisons of these two appliances are feature lists, and feature lists do not predict which one you will use in March. The thing that actually decides it is a schedule question: **do you start dinner at 8 a.m. or at 6 p.m.?**

A slow cooker takes six to ten hours and must be loaded before you leave. A pressure cooker takes forty-five minutes and must be started when you get home. Both produce braised meat and beans and stew. If your household eats at seven and someone is home at four, the pressure cooker wins by default. If you leave at seven and eat at six, the slow cooker wins by default, because the pressure cooker requires you to be present at both ends.

Everything else is detail. But the details matter once the schedule question is settled.

## What Each Machine Physically Does

They are not two versions of the same process. They are two different heat mechanisms.

| | Slow cooker | Electric pressure cooker |
|---|---|---|
| Heating | Element under a ceramic crock | Element under a steel pot |
| Cooking temperature | 190–205°F (low), 200–210°F (high) | 240–250°F (high pressure, 11 psi) |
| Cooking time for a 3-lb chuck roast | 8–9 hours | 60–75 minutes plus 20 minutes to pressurize and release |
| Evaporation | Almost none — the lid seals | None during cooking, some during release |
| Browning | Cannot brown | Cannot brown (needs a separate sauté step) |
| Minimum liquid | None, though 1 cup is usual | 1 cup thin liquid, non-negotiable |
| Maximum fill | Two-thirds | Two-thirds (half for grains and foaming foods) |
| Power draw | 200 W low, 300 W high | 900–1,200 W while heating |
| Can you open it mid-cook? | Yes, and stir | No — pressure must be released first |

The temperature is the important row. At 200°F, in a sealed humid environment, collagen in tough meat breaks down into gelatin over hours, and nothing caramelises because there is no dry heat and the temperature never approaches the 300°F that browning requires. At 245°F under pressure, the same conversion happens in a fraction of the time, for the same reason a pressure cooker makes stock in an hour.

Neither machine browns. Both need a skillet — or the pressure cooker's own sauté mode — for that step. This is where most "the stew tasted flat" complaints come from: browning is not optional, it is the flavour, and neither appliance does it.

## Where the Slow Cooker Is Genuinely Better

**It is truly unattended.** A slow cooker running on low is about as dangerous as a lamp. You can leave the house for nine hours. The pot holds food around 200°F, comfortably outside [the temperature danger zone](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/temperature-danger-zone/), for as long as you are gone. A pressure cooker cannot be left alone, and the instruction manual will tell you so — the lid is locked under pressure, and you need to be present for the release.

**It handles large, tough cuts gracefully.** A 4-pound pork shoulder in a slow cooker at 200°F for nine hours comes out shreddable and moist, with a wide margin for error - which is also what makes it useful for [cooking several days of meals at once](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/meal-prep-a-week/). The same cut in a pressure cooker is done in ninety minutes, but the window between "perfect" and "overcooked to mush" is narrower because the temperature is higher and the process is faster.

**It does not care about liquid thickness.** Thick tomato sauce, a stew with very little added liquid, a pot of chili — none of it causes a problem, because there is no pressure to build.

**It is quiet and cheap.** Forty to eighty dollars for a 6-quart model, 200 watts on low. No seals to replace, no steam jet, no learning curve.

## Where the Pressure Cooker Is Genuinely Better

**It converts a decision made at six into dinner at seven.** This is the whole argument. Dry beans in thirty-five minutes instead of overnight plus two hours. A frozen-solid chicken breast poached in twelve minutes. Stock from a carcass in forty-five.

**It does the sauté step in the same pot.** Brown the meat in the steel insert, add liquid, lock the lid. That saves a pan and, more importantly, saves deglazing the pan separately — the browned bits stay in the pot and become the sauce.

**It makes [rice](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/cook-rice-so-it-turns-out-right/) and yogurt well.** Both are functions a slow cooker cannot do at all, and both are used more often than the pressure function in many households, which is worth knowing before you buy it for stews.

**It uses less electricity per meal than people expect.** A slow cooker draws 200 watts for nine hours — roughly 0.9 to 1.2 kWh. A pressure cooker draws 1,000 watts but only for about twenty-five minutes of actual heating in a forty-five-minute braise — around 0.4 to 0.6 kWh. At 17 cents a kilowatt-hour, that is roughly 15 cents against 8 cents. The higher-wattage appliance costs less to run because it runs so briefly.

## The Failure Modes Nobody Warns You About

**The pressure cooker's "slow cook" mode is not a slow cooker.** It heats from the bottom of a thin steel pot with no ceramic mass and no wrap-around element, and the temperature swings more than a dedicated slow cooker's. Reviews that say "it works fine" are usually about soups. For a pork shoulder over eight hours, it produces uneven results and sometimes a burn notice.

**The pressure cooker hates thick sauces.** Tomato paste, a ragù with little liquid, a curry built on purée — anything viscous cannot circulate heat, so the bottom scorches and the machine shuts off with a burn notice. The fix is to add water or broth and reduce afterwards, or to use the slow cooker.

**Quick release is wrong for starchy or large-volume foods.** Releasing pressure immediately on a full pot of beans or a large roast makes the liquid boil violently and can push starch into the valve. Use a natural release — ten to twenty minutes off the heat — for anything thick, starchy, or filled near the maximum line.

**A slow cooker cannot brown, and adding the meat raw costs you the dish.** Pat the meat dry, sear it in a skillet first, and deglaze that skillet with a splash of the cooking liquid to get the fond into the pot. Five minutes of work. Without it, the stew is grey and tastes of boiled meat.

**Old beans never soften.** Both appliances fail on beans that have been stored for years, and acidic ingredients added too early make it worse. If the beans are more than two years old, no amount of time fixes them.

**The sealing ring holds odours.** The silicone ring in a pressure cooker absorbs the smell of every curry and chili it cooks. A spare ring, or a ring reserved for savoury dishes, is a real accessory, not a marketing one.

## The Three Questions That Decide It

**1. When do you start cooking?**

If dinner is assembled before work and eaten after, buy the slow cooker. If dinner is decided when you walk into the kitchen, buy the pressure cooker. This one question predicts usage about 80 percent of the time.

**2. How much of your cooking is braises and stews versus rice, beans, and stock?**

Slow cooker: braises, stews, chili, pulled meat, overnight porridge.
Pressure cooker: beans from dry, stock, rice, yogurt, whole chicken, anything you forgot to thaw.

**3. Do you have the counter space for one, or for two?**

A 6-quart slow cooker is roughly 15 inches wide. A 6-quart pressure cooker is roughly 13 inches wide and 14 tall, and cannot go under a low cabinet while in use because the lid needs clearance. If only one can live on the counter, the schedule question decides it.

## Accessories That Are Worth It, and One That Is Not

**Spare sealing ring (pressure cooker), $10.** The silicone ring absorbs the smell of every curry and chili. A second ring, one for savoury and one for neutral, is the difference between yoghurt that tastes of cumin and yoghurt that does not.

**Silicone sling or trivet (pressure cooker), $8.** Lifts a ramekin or a foil-covered dish out of the pot, which is how you make custards, cheesecake, and pot-in-pot rice. Without it, those recipes are not possible.

**Glass lid (slow cooker), $12.** A slow cooker lid is opaque, and lifting it loses heat that takes twenty minutes to recover. A glass lid lets you look. If your model does not offer one, skip it — the loss is minor.

**Slow cooker liners, $5.** These work and they are unnecessary. Soaking the crock in warm water for twenty minutes achieves the same result without producing plastic waste per meal.

**Extra inner pot (pressure cooker), $25.** Worth it only if you cook beans and yoghurt in the same week and do not want to wash between. Most households do not need it.

## Size, Which Matters More Than the Model

| Household | Slow cooker | Pressure cooker |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 people | 3–4 qt | 3 qt (limited for beans and stock) |
| 3–4 people | 6 qt | 6 qt — the sweet spot |
| 5+ or batch cooking | 7–8 qt | 8 qt |

Two notes. A slow cooker should be filled between half and two-thirds; a 6-quart cooker filled to the top will not come to temperature properly and will cook unevenly. And a 3-quart pressure cooker cannot hold a whole chicken or a useful batch of stock, which is where most people discover they bought too small.

## What About Buying Both

If you cook at home most nights and have the space, the pair is not redundant: the slow cooker owns unattended weekday braises, the pressure cooker owns everything you decided too late, and [an air fryer](/kitchen-tips/equipment/what-an-air-fryer-actually-does/) covers the fast dry-heat jobs neither one does. The overlap is smaller than it looks, because the two machines are used at different times of day.

If you cook three nights a week and have one appliance's worth of space, buy for your schedule and accept the gap. A pressure cooker used four times a week beats two appliances used twice a month.

## FAQ

**Can I leave an Instant Pot unattended while it cooks?**

Not in the way you can leave a slow cooker. The pot is under pressure, the lid is locked, and you need to be there to release it. Most manufacturers say the appliance should not be left unattended during use.

**Why does my Instant Pot say "burn"?**

The bottom of the pot got hotter than the sensor allows, which almost always means too little liquid or a sauce too thick to circulate heat. Add at least a cup of thin liquid, put thick ingredients on top of it rather than on the metal, and do not stir before pressurising.

**Is a slow cooker cheaper to run than an oven?**

Yes, considerably. A slow cooker on low draws about 200 watts; an oven held at 325°F for three hours draws far more. For a braise, the slow cooker is the cheapest way to cook it.

**Can I use a slow cooker without a liner or bag?**

Yes, and you should. Slow cooker liners are convenient but unnecessary; soaking the crock in warm water for twenty minutes makes it wash clean.

**Does food in a slow cooker need to be covered in liquid?**

No. An inch or two in the bottom is enough for most braises, because the sealed lid traps steam. Submerging the meat produces boiled texture and diluted flavour.

**What is the minimum liquid for a pressure cooker?**

One cup of thin liquid — water, broth, wine, or a thin sauce. Thick ingredients like tomato paste or cream should sit on top of the liquid, not directly on the metal base.

**Does the pressure cooker really cook beans without soaking?**

Yes, for most varieties: 30 to 40 minutes at high pressure for black or pinto beans from dry, plus a natural release. Older beans need longer, and beans with added acid or salt at the start may never soften.

## Bottom Line

Buy the slow cooker if your cooking happens in the morning and finishes unattended, and if your meals are braises, stews, and pulled meat. Buy the pressure cooker if you decide dinner when you get home, and if beans, stock, rice, and thawing-from-frozen are regular problems. Both need a skillet for browning, neither browns on its own, and the pressure cooker's slow-cook mode is a fallback rather than a substitute. Size up rather than down — a 6-quart pot is the smallest one that does everything.
