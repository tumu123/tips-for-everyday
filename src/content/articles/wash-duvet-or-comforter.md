Most guides to washing a duvet open with technique. The honest opening is a measurement, because the failure mode of home duvet washing is almost never a technique error. It is that the duvet did not fit.

A wet king-size down comforter weighs 30 to 40 pounds. In a drum that is too small, it cannot tumble, so it bunches into one mass, the detergent never rinses out of the center, and the drying stage — which was already marginal — now takes eight hours and still leaves the middle damp. Two weeks later the smell starts.

So start with the size, then the filling, then the method.

## Step 1: The Fit Test

The rule that matters: **the duvet must be able to tumble freely, and should fill no more than half to two-thirds of the drum.**

| Duvet size | Typical dimensions | Minimum drum | Realistic? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crib / toddler | 36 × 48 in | 2.5 cu ft | Yes, easily |
| Twin | 68 × 86 in | 3.0 cu ft | Yes |
| Full / Queen | 88 × 92 in | 4.0 to 4.5 cu ft | Only in a large front-loader |
| King / California King | 104 × 92 in | 5.0+ cu ft | Rarely at home — commercial or laundry |
| Any size, down-filled, high loft | — | Add 0.5 cu ft | The loft is the volume, not the fabric |

Measure your drum's capacity, or look it up by model. A typical US top-loading machine is 3.5 to 4.5 cu ft; a compact apartment machine is 2.0 to 2.5; a large front-loader is 4.5 to 5.0.

**A top-loader with an agitator is a separate problem.** It is not just small — the central agitator grabs the duvet and wrings it, which tears baffles (the internal walls that keep down from shifting to one end) and can crack the drum. If the only option is a top-loader with an agitator, the honest answer is: do not wash it at home. Use a laundromat's large front-loader or a professional.

**The physical test:** put the dry duvet in the drum and close the door. If you have to compress it to close the door, it is already too big, because wet fabric is heavier and less compressible. If it fills more than two-thirds of the drum, it cannot tumble, and a duvet that cannot tumble cannot be rinsed or dried evenly.

## Step 2: What Is Inside

The filling decides the detergent, the temperature, and most importantly the drying time.

| Filling | Wash? | Dryer | Drying time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Down / feather** | Yes, if it fits | Low heat, balls, long | 3 to 5 hours |
| **Down alternative (polyester cluster)** | Yes | Low, balls | 2 to 3 hours |
| **Cotton batting** | Yes, but heavy | Low, long | 4 hours plus |
| **Wool** | No | No | Felts irreversibly in water |
| **Silk** | No, or hand wash only | No | Loses loft, water-spots |
| **Any with a "dry clean only" label plus a coated shell** | No | No | The coating is the constraint |

The counterintuitive one: **dry cleaning is generally worse for a down duvet than washing is.** Dry cleaning solvent strips the natural oils from the down clusters, and the solvent residue is harder to rinse out than detergent. Manufacturers of good down bedding usually specify water washing for exactly this reason, and a "dry clean only" label on a down duvet is often a liability decision rather than a fiber requirement — the same pattern as [the label on curtains](/home-hacks/fabrics/wash-curtains-without-shrinking/).

That said, do not override the label on a coated or quilted synthetic shell, or on anything with a decorative trim or appliqué.

## Step 3: Before It Goes In

- **Check every seam and baffle.** A single split seam empties the down into the drum, clogs the drain filter, and coats the inside of your machine with feathers for months.
- **Close zippers and snap closures** so they do not snag the shell.
- **Pre-treat stains** with a drop of mild detergent worked in with a fingertip, ten minutes before washing. For anything beyond a light mark, check [the fabric-safe stain chart](/home-hacks/laundry/remove-laundry-stains/) first, because heat sets several of them permanently. Focus on the corners and the head end, where body oils concentrate.
- **Wash alone.** Never with other laundry. A duvet needs the whole drum to tumble.

## Step 4: Wash

**Detergent:** a down-specific wash, or a mild liquid detergent for wool and delicates. One to two tablespoons depending on size.

Three things never to use:

- **Standard laundry powder.** Alkaline, and it strips the natural oil coating from down clusters. Stripped down does not loft; it compacts into flat sheets that never fully recover.
- **Fabric softener.** It coats the clusters and does the same thing by a different mechanism.
- **Bleach.** It degrades both down protein and polyester fibers.

**Settings:**

- **Cycle:** gentle, delicate, or a dedicated "down" or "bulky" cycle if the machine has one.
- **Temperature:** cold or warm (max 30°C / 86°F). Hot water sets stains and can shrink the shell.
- **Spin:** low — 400 to 600 rpm. A high spin wrings the baffles and can tear internal stitching.
- **Extra rinse:** yes, at least one, ideally two. Residual detergent inside a duvet is the primary cause of clumping and of that sour smell that returns after a week.

If the machine has a "bulky" setting, use it — these cycles add water and reduce agitation specifically for items like this. Whatever the setting, a machine that has just washed bedding needs its own maintenance, covered in [cleaning the washer](/home-hacks/appliances/how-to-clean-washing-machine/).

## Step 5: Drying Is Where It Succeeds or Fails

A duvet takes three to five hours to dry. That is not a conservative estimate; it is what a king down comforter needs on low heat with balls in a large drum. Plan for it.

**Method:**

1. **Low heat, never high.** High heat scorches the shell and can degrade down protein. It also dries the outside fast while the inside stays wet, which is the worst possible outcome because it feels done.
2. **Three dryer balls or clean tennis balls.** They beat the filling as the drum turns and break up the clumps that wet down forms. Without them the duvet dries as a hard slab in the middle.
3. **Stop every 30 minutes and break it apart by hand.** Take the duvet out, shake it, and separate the clumps with your fingers, working from the center outward. This is the step people skip and it is the step that matters most.
4. **Check the center, not the edges.** Feel the middle of the thickest part with the back of your hand. Cool means wet.

**If you do not have a dryer:** down and down-alternative duvets air-dry badly. They need to be shaken and turned every few hours for two to three days in a warm, dry, ventilated room, and even then the center can stay damp. A damp duvet grows musty and there is no way to launder the smell out of a filling this thick. Do not attempt air drying for a king-size duvet in a humid climate.

**The dryness test:** after taking it out, lay it flat and press both hands into the thickest area. Then hold it up to your cheek — the fabric will feel cool and slightly damp if there is moisture inside. Any doubt, put it back for another 30 minutes. A duvet that is 95% dry will smell within a week, and the smell is inside the filling.

## Wool, Silk, and the Ones to Leave Alone

**Wool duvets** felt in water. The scales on the fiber lock together with heat and agitation, and a felted wool duvet is a permanent, shrunken, stiff object. Brush it, air it, spot clean it, and have it professionally dry cleaned if it must be cleaned.

**Silk duvets** lose loft in water and water-spot badly. Hand washing is theoretically possible in cool water with a silk detergent, laying flat to dry out of sunlight, but the risk-to-reward ratio is poor for a large item.

**Very old or fragile duvets** — where the shell fabric tears under light tension — should not be washed at all. The shell is already degraded, and agitation finishes it.

## Spot Cleaning Instead of Washing

If the duvet is not due for a full wash, most marks can be handled in place:

- **Body oil at the head end:** a paste of mild detergent and water, worked in with a soft cloth, left ten minutes, then blotted with a damp cloth and dried with a towel.
- **Spills:** blot, do not rub. Cold water only, and work from the outside of the stain inward.
- **Odor:** air it outdoors in shade for a few hours and shake it. Do not use fabric spray — it adds another layer of residue to a filling you cannot rinse.

## How Often

| Situation | Interval |
|---|---|
| With a duvet cover, no pets, no eating in bed | Once a year |
| With a cover, pets on the bed | Every 6 months |
| No cover | Every 3 to 4 months |
| Allergies or asthma | Every 3 months |
| After illness with fever | Immediately |

The duvet cover is doing most of the work — it is what gets washed weekly or biweekly. Pillows sit in the same bed and follow the same logic at a smaller scale: [how often to wash pillows](/home-hacks/fabrics/how-often-to-wash-pillows/) is set by the protector, not the calendar. A duvet with a cover and a protector underneath genuinely only needs an annual wash. Without one, the shell is in direct contact with skin and the interval drops sharply.

## FAQ

**Can I wash a king-size comforter at home?**

Rarely. A king needs a drum of at least 5 cubic feet, and most home machines are 3.5 to 4.5. If you have to compress it to close the door, it is already too large, because wet fabric is heavier and less compressible. Use a laundromat's large front-loader or a professional service.

**Is it better to dry clean or wash a down comforter?**

Washing is usually better. Dry cleaning solvent strips the natural oils from down clusters and the residue is hard to rinse out. Manufacturers of quality down bedding typically specify water washing. Dry clean only if the shell is coated or the label is explicit and the item is valuable.

**How do I stop a comforter from clumping after washing?**

Use low heat with three dryer balls, and stop the dryer every 30 minutes to break the filling apart by hand from the center outward. Clumping comes from wet down matting together and drying as a slab. It also comes from leftover detergent, so run at least one extra rinse.

**How long does a comforter take to dry?**

Two to three hours for down alternative, three to five for down, on low heat in a large drum with balls. Anything faster means the heat is too high and the center is probably still wet. Check the thickest part with the back of your hand, not the edges.

**Can I use fabric softener on a duvet?**

No. It coats the fibers and the down clusters, which reduces loft and traps odor. It also makes the shell feel slick rather than crisp. Use a down-specific detergent and an extra rinse instead.

**Can I wash a wool or silk duvet?**

Wool, no — it felts irreversibly in water with any agitation. Silk, technically by hand in cool water with a silk detergent, laid flat to dry away from sun, but the risk is high for a large item and professional cleaning is the safer call. Brush and air both instead.

**Why does my comforter smell after washing?**

Either the center was still damp when you put it away, or detergent remained in the filling. Both produce the same sour smell, and neither is fixed by washing again with more detergent. Re-wash with no detergent and two extra rinses, then dry far longer than you think necessary.

**How do I wash a duvet if I do not have a dryer?**

Do not, for down or down alternative. They need to be shaken and turned every few hours for two to three days in a warm, dry, ventilated room, and the center often stays damp anyway. A damp filling this thick cannot be laundered clean of the smell afterwards. Use a laundromat dryer.

## Bottom Line

Measure the drum before anything else. If the duvet fills more than two-thirds of it, or the machine has a center agitator, do the job at a laundromat. If it fits: mild or down-specific detergent, gentle cycle, cold or warm water, low spin, two extra rinses, then low heat with three balls and a hand-break every half hour until the center is genuinely dry. The drying stage is the whole job, and rushing it is how duvets get ruined without anything visibly going wrong.
