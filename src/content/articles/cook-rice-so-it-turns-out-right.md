Rice is one of the few foods where the failure is almost always structural rather than a matter of skill. Nobody's rice comes out wrong because they stirred it too gently. It comes out wrong because the water-to-grain ratio was for a different rice, or the lid came off, or the pot went straight from heat to plate.

Fix those three and rice stops being a coin flip — on a stove, in a cheap pot, without any special equipment.

---

## Step 1: Match the water to the grain, not to the bag

The single biggest error is using one ratio for every rice. Different varieties absorb different amounts, and they are not close.

| Rice type | Water : rice (by volume) | Cook time after boiling | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long-grain white (basmati, jasmine) | **1 : 1.5** | 12–15 min | Soak basmati 20–30 min for separate grains |
| Medium-grain white | 1 : 1.25 | 15 min | Slightly stickier, good for bowls |
| Short-grain / sushi | 1 : 1.1–1.2 | 12–13 min | Rinse very well; do not stir |
| Brown rice (long or short) | **1 : 2 – 2.25** | 40–45 min | Soaking 30 min cuts time and improves texture |
| Parboiled / converted | 1 : 2 | 20–25 min | Forgiving, hard to overcook |
| Sticky / glutinous | 1 : 1 – 1.1 | 15–20 min, steamed | Soak 4+ hours; steam rather than boil |
| Wild rice (a grass, not rice) | 1 : 3 | 45–55 min | Needs far more water than it looks |
| Arborio / risotto | 1 : 3–4, added gradually | 18–20 min | Stirred on purpose; different method entirely |

Two secondary factors shift these numbers:

- **Altitude.** Water boils cooler at elevation. At 5,000 ft, add roughly 10–15% more water and 3–5 minutes.
- **Pot width.** A wide pot with a large surface area evaporates more; add a splash.

If you switch rice varieties and don't adjust the ratio, you will get the two classic failures: crunchy centers (too little water for a thirsty grain) or a sticky porridge (too much for a lean one).

## Step 2: Rinse until the water tells you to stop

Put the rice in a bowl, cover with cold water, swirl with your fingers, and pour off the cloudy water. Repeat.

**How many times:** 2–3 rinses for everyday long-grain rice, until the water is only slightly cloudy. **For sushi or sticky rice, rinse 5–6 times until it runs almost clear** — the surface starch is exactly what makes rice gluey, and sushi rice needs the opposite.

Two cautions:

- **Swirl, don't scrub.** Grinding the grains against each other breaks them, and broken grains release starch and cook unevenly.
- **Rinsing does remove some water-soluble nutrients** — notably B vitamins in fortified rice. If you eat fortified rice for that reason, rinse once rather than five times.

Skip rinsing only if you are making risotto, where the surface starch is the point.

## Step 3: Soak long-grain and brown rice, skip it for most others

Soaking is not a ritual — it changes the outcome in specific cases.

- **Basmati: 20–30 minutes.** The grains lengthen noticeably and cook up separate and fluffy rather than clumping. This is the difference between decent basmati and restaurant basmati.
- **Brown rice: 30 minutes (up to 2 hours).** The bran layer is the barrier; soaking softens it so the interior cooks before the outside turns to mush. You can cut 5–10 minutes off the cooking time and get a more even texture.
- **Jasmine, medium-grain, sushi rice: skip it.** They cook fast and evenly already, and soaking makes them fragile.
- **Sticky rice: 4 hours or overnight.** Non-negotiable for the texture.

Drain well before cooking. Rice going into the pot with a couple of tablespoons of soaking water attached will throw off the ratio.

## Step 4: Boil, then drop to the lowest heat and cover

The sequence matters more than the numbers.

1. **Put rice and measured cold water in the pot.** Add salt if you want it — about ½ teaspoon per cup of rice for a seasoned base, or none if you will sauce it.
2. **Bring to a full boil, uncovered.** This is the only stage where you watch it.
3. **As soon as the surface is bubbling across the whole pot, turn the heat to its lowest setting and put the lid on.** Low means low: a bare simmer, not a gentle boil.
4. **Do not lift the lid.** Every time you open it, steam escapes and the temperature drops. Two lid-lifts in a batch can add five minutes and leave the top layer underdone.
5. **Start timing from the moment the lid goes on.** Use the times in the table above.

**Why no stirring:** stirring agitates the grains and releases surface starch, which is exactly the mechanism that turns rice sticky. The one exception is risotto, which is stirred deliberately.

If your stove's lowest setting still bubbles vigorously, use a flame tamer or a heat diffuser. A heavy-bottomed pot helps more than a nonstick one here, because it spreads heat evenly and doesn't develop a scorched center.

## Step 5: Rest it off the heat before you open it

When the timer ends, **turn off the heat and leave the lid on for 10 minutes** (5 for thin white rice, 10–15 for brown).

This step is skipped by almost everyone and is the cheapest improvement available. During the rest:

- Residual steam finishes hydrating the grains from the inside.
- Moisture redistributes from the bottom of the pot to the top.
- The grains firm up and stop being fragile, so they don't break when you fluff them.

Rice taken straight from heat to bowl is wet at the bottom, dry at the top, and breaks apart when you touch it. Ten minutes fixes all three.

## Step 6: Fluff with a fork, never stir

Run a fork or a rice paddle through the rice in **lifting, separating strokes** — down to the bottom, up and over. Break up the clumps by lifting, not by mashing.

Do not stir in circles. Stirring compresses the grains, crushes the ones at the bottom, and turns the whole pot creamy.

Serve immediately, or spread the rice on a tray to cool quickly if you are storing it (Step 8).

## Step 7: Fix the two failure modes

Both are recoverable if you catch them before the rice cools.

**Too much water (soupy, sticky, or still wet after resting):**

1. Take the lid off and put the pot over low heat for 3–5 minutes, stirring gently once or twice, until the excess moisture steams off.
2. Or spread the rice on a wide tray for a few minutes — surface area does the work.
3. Don't add more rice to compensate; you'll just have more bad rice.

**Too little water (crunchy center, dry top):**

1. Add 2–3 tablespoons of boiling water per cup of rice.
2. Put the lid back on, return to the lowest heat, and cook 5 more minutes.
3. Rest 5 minutes off the heat, then test a grain from the top and one from the bottom.
4. For badly undercooked rice, add a splash of water, cover tightly with foil and the lid, and bake at 300°F (150°C) for 15 minutes — the even ambient heat finishes it without scorching the base.

**Scorched bottom:** don't stir it in. Spoon the good rice off the top, leaving the crust, and soak the pot with warm water and a little baking soda.

## Step 8: Cool and store it fast — this one is a safety issue

Cooked rice is a documented food-poisoning risk, and the mechanism is worth knowing.

*Bacillus cereus* spores survive cooking. If cooked rice sits warm — between 40°F and 140°F (4°C and 60°C) — the spores germinate and multiply, and reheating does not reliably destroy the toxin they produce. This is the "fried rice syndrome" behind most rice-related illness.

**The rules:**

- **Cool fast.** Spread rice on a wide tray or shallow container so it cools within an hour, then refrigerate.
- **Refrigerate within 2 hours** of cooking, and don't leave rice in a warm pot on the counter overnight.
- **Eat within 2 days.** Reheat once, until steaming hot all the way through, and do not reheat the same portion twice.
- **Cold rice is better for frying** — the surface dries out in the fridge, so the grains stay separate instead of steaming into a paste.

Rice has a storage rule and a cooking rule, and only one of them is about time. For the temperature side, including why 165°F is the number for reheating, see [safe cooking temperatures](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/safe-cooking-temperatures/).

## Why This Works

Rice cooking is a hydration problem with a timing constraint. The grain needs to absorb a specific amount of water, and it needs to do so before the outside turns to starch paste.

The ratio sets how much water is available. Too little and the interior stays hard while the outside cooks; too much and the grain ruptures and releases starch into standing water, which is the mushy result.

Rinsing removes the loose surface starch that otherwise gelatinizes and glues grains together. That is why the rinse count goes up for varieties where separate grains are the goal.

The lid does two jobs: it traps steam, which is the actual cooking medium above the water line, and it keeps the temperature even. Opening it releases both, which is why lid-lifting correlates so strongly with uneven results.

Resting is about equilibration. At the end of cooking, the bottom of the pot is wetter and hotter than the top. Ten minutes off the heat lets capillary action and residual steam even that out — the same reason a steak rests.

And the cooling rule follows from spore biology: the danger zone is a temperature range where *B. cereus* multiplies, and the time spent there is what determines risk. Fast cooling shortens that window; slow cooling in a sealed pot maximizes it.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Using the same water ratio for every rice | Varieties absorb very different amounts | Follow the table by type |
| Stirring the rice while it cooks | Releases starch, produces gluey rice | Leave it alone; fluff after resting |
| Lifting the lid to check | Loses steam and heat; uneven cooking | Trust the timer, look through a glass lid |
| Going straight from heat to plate | Bottom is soggy, top is dry, grains break | Rest 10 minutes off the heat, lid on |
| Rinsing only once for sushi rice | Surface starch stays, rice turns gluey | Rinse 5–6 times until nearly clear |
| Scrubbing grains against each other | Breaks grains, uneven cooking | Swirl gently with your fingers |
| Leaving cooked rice out overnight | *Bacillus cereus* multiplies in the danger zone | Cool fast, refrigerate within 2 hours |
| Reheating the same rice twice | Toxin is not destroyed by reheating | Reheat once, eat within 2 days |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the correct rice to water ratio?**
It depends on the variety: long-grain white 1:1.5, medium-grain 1:1.25, short-grain 1:1.1–1.2, brown rice 1:2–2.25, wild rice 1:3. Volume ratios, measured with the same cup.

**Why is my rice always mushy?**
Usually too much water for the variety, or stirring during cooking, or both. Rinsing less than you should also contributes, because loose surface starch gelatinizes and glues the grains.

**Do you have to rinse rice before cooking?**
For separate, fluffy grains, yes — 2–3 rinses for long-grain and 5–6 for sushi rice. It is optional for risotto, where the surface starch is desirable.

**How long does white rice take to cook?**
About 12–15 minutes after the water comes to a boil, with the lid on and the heat at its lowest, plus 5–10 minutes resting off the heat.

**Why does my rice burn on the bottom?**
Thin pots, heat that is too high, or too little water. Use a heavy-bottomed pot, keep the flame at a bare simmer, and use a heat diffuser if your stove runs hot.

**Can you cook rice without a lid?**
Not well. Steam above the water line is what cooks the top layer; without a lid it stays hard while the bottom overcooks.

**How long can cooked rice stay in the fridge?**
Two days, and reheat it only once. Cool it quickly and refrigerate within two hours of cooking — warm rice is a *Bacillus cereus* risk.

**Why is restaurant rice so much better?**
Two things you can copy: they soak basmati before cooking, and they rest it off the heat before serving. Neither requires special equipment.

**Does soaking rice reduce cooking time?**
Yes, particularly for brown rice, where 30 minutes of soaking can save 5–10 minutes and produce a more even texture. For white long-grain it mainly improves the separation of the grains.
