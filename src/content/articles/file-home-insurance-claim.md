A home insurance claim is the largest single financial interaction most households ever have with an insurer, and it is usually filed by someone having the worst week of their year. That combination — high stakes, low preparation, and a deadline — is why claim payouts vary so widely for identical damage.

The mechanics are not mysterious. An adjuster is answering three questions: did a covered event occur, is the damage consistent with that event, and what does it cost to put the house back. Everything you do in the first day either supports those answers or creates doubt about them. This guide is the sequence that supports them.

## The Three Questions the Claim Actually Turns On

**1. Was the cause covered?** Policies are written as covered perils with named exclusions. Water damage from a burst supply line is usually covered; water that seeped in gradually over months from a failed seal is usually not, because it falls under the maintenance exclusion. Same wet drywall, opposite outcomes, and the difference is entirely in what caused it and when.

**2. Is the damage consistent with the reported cause?** Adjusters are trained on patterns. A [ceiling stain that is a uniform brown ring](/fix-it/home-repair/water-stain-on-ceiling/) with no sagging reads as a slow leak. A ceiling that failed suddenly reads as a burst above it. If your account says the pipe burst this morning and the drywall shows three months of staining, the file gets flagged — and a flagged file slows down or shrinks.

**3. What does restoration cost?** This is a scope question, not an argument. The adjuster prices line items from a estimating database; you win on scope by documenting items that were missed, not by arguing about the ones that were priced.

Everything below serves one of those three.

## Step 1: Stop the Damage, Then Keep the Receipts

Almost every policy contains a duty to mitigate: you are required to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, and you are generally reimbursed for reasonable costs of doing so. This is the one part of a claim where spending money quickly is right.

What counts as mitigation: shutting off the water at the main, calling an emergency plumber at 2 am for an active leak, tarping a damaged roof, extracting standing water, running dehumidifiers, moving furniture out of a wet room, boarding a broken window.

What to keep: every invoice, every receipt, and a note of what you did and when. Photograph the receipts. Mitigation costs are usually reimbursed outside the deductible, but only if they are documented and reasonable — a $600 emergency dry-out is routine; $4,000 of equipment rental without explanation is not.

Mitigation is also the moment to find the source, because a second leak in the same wall will not be covered as part of this claim — [the moisture checklist](/fix-it/maintenance/find-moisture-problems-early/) covers the three places it usually hides.

One caution: mitigation is not renovation. Do not start replacing drywall or flooring before the adjuster has seen the damage. Drying and stabilizing, yes. Rebuilding, no.

## Step 2: Document Before You Clean

This is the single highest-leverage hour of the entire claim. Once the water is extracted and the debris bagged, the evidence is gone, and the claim becomes your verbal account against the adjuster's experience.

Shoot in this order:

1. **Wide shots of every affected room** from each doorway, showing the whole space and what is in it.
2. **Medium shots of each damaged area**, including a reference object (a door frame, an outlet) so scale is obvious.
3. **Close-ups of the damage itself** — the split in the pipe, the waterline on the drywall, the buckle in the floor.
4. **A shot of the cause**, before it is repaired. The failed fitting, the cracked supply line, the tree limb, the hail on the ground next to the damaged shingles.
5. **Serial and model plates** of any damaged appliance, plus its age if you know it.
6. **A slow video walkthrough** narrating what you see, room by room. Video carries the timeline and the spatial relationship in a way stills do not.

Check that your phone's location and timestamp are on. Photos with metadata carry more weight than photos without it, and adjusters increasingly look. If you have any pre-loss photos of the same rooms — from a listing, a holiday, a renovation — dig them out. They establish condition before the event, which is exactly what a "pre-existing damage" denial attacks.

## Step 3: Read Three Parts of the Policy Before You Call

You do not need to read the whole contract. You need three sections, in this order.

**The declarations page.** Dwelling limit (Coverage A), other structures (B), personal property (C), loss of use (D), and your deductible — including whether the deductible is a flat dollar amount or a percentage of the dwelling limit. A 2% wind/hail deductible on a $450,000 house is $9,000 out of your pocket before anything is paid.

**The exclusions.** Scan for water, earth movement, neglect, mold, and ordinance or law. Two things worth checking specifically: whether you have a **water backup endorsement** (sump pump and sewer backup are excluded by default on most policies) and whether you have **matching coverage** for siding and roofing, which many policies limit or exclude.

**The duties after loss section.** This is the list of things you must do, in the order the policy requires, including prompt notice, protection of property, and a sworn proof of loss within a stated period. Missing a duty is one of the few ways to turn a valid claim into a weak one.

## Step 4: Report It the Same Day, and Say Less Than You Think

Call the claims number, not just your agent, and note down: the claim number, the date and time reported, the name of the person who took it, and the name and phone number of the assigned adjuster. You will refer to these notes repeatedly.

What to say: what happened, when it happened, what is damaged, and what you have done to stop further damage.

What not to say:

- **Do not speculate about the cause** ("I think the roof has been bad for years"). Speculation about long-term deterioration hands the insurer a maintenance exclusion.
- **Do not characterize age or condition** ("it was old anyway"). It devalues the item in the file before anyone has looked at it.
- **Do not estimate the damage** with a number. Your number becomes the anchor, and if the real scope is higher, you spend the rest of the claim climbing from it.
- **Do not guess at dates.** "Sometime last week" invites the question of why you waited.

If the loss involves a fire department, police, or a contractor who attended, get their report numbers now, while the people who wrote them still remember.

## Step 5: Do Not Throw Anything Away Yet

Contents claims are where households lose the most money quietly. The rule that protects you: **photograph everything before it leaves the house**, and keep the item itself until the adjuster has seen it or released it in writing.

If you must dispose of something for health reasons — saturated carpet, ruined food, sewage-contaminated material — photograph it in place with something for scale, cut a sample if practical, and record the quantity and the replacement price. Keep a written log with dates.

The one exception: if an item is a health hazard and the insurer is unreachable, dispose of it, document it thoroughly, and tell the adjuster you did so and why. Reasonableness wins that argument.

Not throwing anything away is the right instinct for the first week. For how long each document is genuinely worth keeping, see [which home records to keep](/organize/closet/home-records-retention/).

## Step 6: The Adjuster Visit

You will meet one of two kinds of adjuster. A **staff adjuster** works for the insurer. An **independent adjuster** is a contractor hired by the insurer during high-volume events; they are paid by the insurer and often have a large caseload, which means the visit may be short.

What actually matters in the visit:

- **Be there.** Never let a contractor meet the adjuster alone, and never let the adjuster walk the house unsupervised. The scope is written from this visit.
- **Walk it in the same order you documented it.** Have your photos ready on a tablet or phone so you can point to what the water looked like before extraction.
- **Point out what is not visible.** The floor under the vanity, the insulation in the wall cavity, the subfloor under the tile. Adjusters measure what they can see; hidden damage needs to be raised explicitly and often probed.
- **Ask for the scope in writing** before the visit ends, and ask when it will be sent. A verbal scope is not a scope.
- **Do not sign anything at the visit.** Especially not a "direction to pay," an assignment of benefits, or a contractor's authorization. Signing at the visit is how homeowners lose control of their own claim.

## Step 7: ACV, Replacement Cost, and the Second Check

Understand these three terms before you read the offer, because the difference between them is usually thousands of dollars.

**Actual Cash Value (ACV)** is replacement cost minus depreciation for age and condition. A ten-year-old water heater does not have a ten-year-old value; it has a value based on its remaining life.

**Replacement Cost Value (RCV)** is what it costs to buy the same item new today, without depreciation.

**Recoverable depreciation** is the gap between the two, and on an RCV policy it is paid as a second check — but only after you have replaced the item and submitted the receipt. This is the most commonly forfeited money in home claims: households take the ACV check, never replace the item, and never claim the difference.

Practical consequences:

- If your policy is RCV, you will usually get an ACV payment first. That is normal, not a lowball.
- Keep every receipt for replacement purchases, and submit them with the request for the depreciation release.
- Ask, in writing, what the **deadline** is for claiming recoverable depreciation. It is often one or two years from the date of loss, and it is not always volunteered.
- For contents, most policies pay ACV up front and RCV on replacement. If you are not going to replace a $400 coat, you are entitled to its ACV, not its replacement value — know which one you are claiming.

## Step 8: Read the Scope Line by Line

The scope of loss is the itemized repair estimate. Read it against your own photos, in this order:

**Quantities.** Square footage of drywall, linear feet of baseboard, number of shingles. Under-measured quantities are the most common and most easily corrected error, and they cost real money.

**Matching.** If half the siding is replaced, does the scope include matching the rest? Older policies often exclude matching; newer ones may include it. Check the endorsement.

**Code upgrades.** Bringing the repair up to current code — arc-fault breakers, tamper-resistant outlets, upgraded flashing — is usually excluded unless you have **ordinance or law** coverage. Ask whether it is on the policy and whether the scope reflects it.

**Loss of use.** If the house is uninhabitable, this covers hotel, meals above normal grocery spending, laundry, and pet boarding. Limits are typically a percentage of the dwelling limit or a time period (often 12 months). Ask what the limit is and how to claim it, because it is almost never offered proactively.

**Debris removal, permits, and dumpsters.** Frequently missing from initial scopes.

**Contents schedule.** Room by room, item by item. This is where you compare your inventory against their list.

## Step 9: When the Offer Is Wrong

Work up the chain, in this order, and do not skip steps.

**1. Ask the adjuster to reconsider, in writing, with specifics.** "The scope shows 120 sq ft of drywall in the living room; the room measures 210 sq ft of affected wall. Photos 12 through 18 show the extent." Specific, photographed, quantified objections get corrected more often than complaints.

**2. Ask for a re-inspection.** A second look with a supervisor or a different adjuster is routine and cheap for the insurer.

**3. Get your own contractor's estimate.** A licensed contractor's itemized estimate in the same format is the strongest counter-evidence, because it speaks the adjuster's language. Many contractors will provide one for free in exchange for the chance at the work.

**4. Use the appraisal clause.** Most policies include one: if you and the insurer disagree on the *amount* of loss (not on coverage), each side hires an appraiser, and an umpire resolves differences. It is a contractual right, it costs a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars, and it is rarely used — which is why it works.

**5. Consider a public adjuster, carefully.** A public adjuster works for you and typically charges 10% to 15% of the settlement. Worth it for large, complex, or disputed claims; almost never worth it for a $6,000 kitchen floor. Check licensing with your state insurance department, and never sign with one who knocks on your door the day after a storm.

**6. Complain to your state insurance department.** Free, on the record, and it changes timelines. Insurers track complaints.

## What Adjusters Actually Look For

Knowing the checklist lets you answer it before it is asked.

- **Consistency between the reported cause and the physical evidence** — pattern of damage, waterlines, burn marks, hail-spatter marks on soft metals.
- **Timeline evidence** — photo metadata, contractor invoices, neighbor reports, utility records.
- **Maintenance history** — service records for the roof, the HVAC, the plumbing. A maintenance exclusion is easier to apply when there is no history at all.
- **Prior claims** at the property, including claims by previous owners in some states.
- **Whether the loss is still ongoing** — an active leak that was never fixed reads as negligence.
- **Reasonable replacement costs** — retail receipts, comparable models, not aspirational upgrades. Claiming the commercial-grade range when you owned the base model is the fastest way to lose credibility on the whole schedule.

## What NOT to Do

- **Do not sign an assignment of benefits** with a restoration contractor. It transfers your claim rights to them, and you lose control of the settlement.
- **Do not accept a "we'll handle the insurance for you" offer** without reading what you are signing. Some contractors are excellent at this; some use it to inflate the scope and leave you liable for the difference.
- **Do not inflate the contents claim.** Every item on the schedule may be compared to photos. Inflated claims convert a cooperative adjuster into an adversarial one, and in the worst case into a fraud referral.
- **Do not miss the proof of loss deadline**, and do not let a contractor's delay become your missed deadline.
- **Do not repair before the scope is agreed** unless it is emergency mitigation, and then photograph everything first.
- **Do not assume flood is covered.** Water rising from outside is excluded from standard homeowners policies and requires a separate policy.

## Fix the Cause Before You File Again

One claim is an event. Two claims at the same address in a short period can affect renewal and premium, and a repeated cause is the strongest argument an insurer has for non-renewal. Before you close the file, fix whatever allowed the loss: replace the failing supply line, clear the gutters, correct the grading, add the water backup endorsement. Premium consequences of claims are covered in more detail in the guide to [lowering a homeowners premium without cutting coverage](/save-money/bills/lower-homeowners-insurance-premium/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Will filing a claim raise my premium?**
Usually yes for an at-fault or weather claim, and the increase can follow the property rather than the owner in some states. Small claims below roughly two to three times your deductible are often worth paying yourself — run the arithmetic before you file.

**How long does a claim take?**
Simple claims settle in two to four weeks. Claims involving contractors, concealed damage, or a coverage dispute routinely run three to six months, and appraisal can add more. Ask for the adjuster's expected timeline in writing at the first contact.

**What if the damage is below my deductible?**
The insurer owes nothing, but reporting it still creates a claim record. For damage clearly below the deductible, pay it yourself and keep the receipts.

**Can I choose my own contractor?**
Yes. You are not required to use the insurer's preferred contractor, and you should be cautious about a contractor whose estimate exactly matches the adjuster's — that usually means the scope was written by one party.

**Is a denied claim final?**
No. Ask for the denial in writing with the policy language cited, then request reconsideration, use appraisal for amount disputes, and escalate to your state insurance department. Denials are frequently reversed when the cause of loss is documented properly.

**Do I need to replace everything to get the full payout?**
On an RCV policy, the depreciation portion is released only when you actually replace the item and submit the receipt. If you do not replace, you keep the ACV and forfeit the rest.

**What about mold that appeared after the leak?**
Most policies cap or exclude mold, often at $10,000, and some exclude it entirely unless the underlying water damage was covered and reported promptly. Speed of drying is the variable you control.

**Should I use a public adjuster for a $15,000 claim?**
At 15%, that is $2,250 of your settlement. Justified only if the offer is clearly low and the dispute is real. Get a second contractor estimate first; it is cheaper and often sufficient.

## Bottom Line

Stop the damage, photograph everything before you clean, read the declarations and exclusions, report the same day, and say only what you know. Then read the scope line by line, ask for what is missing, and do not cash the depreciation check and forget the second half of it. Most of the gap between a good claim outcome and a bad one is documentation done in the first 24 hours — the negotiation comes later and matters less.
