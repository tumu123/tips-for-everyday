Cold air coming from an outlet is real, and it is worth fixing — but the fix is not what most people assume, and doing it the wrong way creates an actual hazard.

**The outlet is not the source; it is the exit.** The air you feel is outside air that has entered the wall cavity somewhere else — usually at the top or bottom of the wall, at the sill plate, or through penetrations — and is finding its way indoors through the hole the electrical box makes in the drywall.

**The stack effect explains why it is noticeable in winter.** Warm air rises and escapes at the top of the house, which lowers pressure near the floor, which pulls cold outside air in at the bottom. Any opening in an exterior wall becomes an intake. Outlet boxes on exterior walls are among the most numerous openings there are.

The good news: sealing them is a small job. The important part is doing it without creating a fire risk or blocking ventilation the house actually needs.

## Step 0: Confirm It Is Air Leakage, Not Just a Cold Surface

Put your hand near the outlet and then hold a lit incense stick or a smoke pencil beside it.

**If the smoke drifts inward, air is leaking.** If the smoke rises undisturbed, you are probably feeling a cold surface — the drywall and the plate are colder than the room, and your hand is sensing that, not airflow.

Cold surfaces feel drafty but are not drafts. Testing with smoke takes five seconds and prevents you from sealing every outlet in the house for no reason.

Do this on a cold, windy day for the clearest result.

## Step 1: Identify Which Outlets Matter

Only outlets on **exterior walls** leak outside air. Outlets on interior walls connect to interior cavities and cannot be a source of outside air.

**Prioritize by count, not by sensation.** A barely-noticeable leak at fifteen outlets is more total loss than an obvious one at two. Walk the exterior walls and count how many boxes there are — outlets, switches, and any other wall penetrations.

Because these openings are so numerous, this is one of the higher-value [air sealing](/fix-it/maintenance/seal-gaps-doors-windows/) jobs in a house relative to how little it costs.

## Step 2: Turn Off the Circuit Before You Touch Anything

Go to the panel and switch off the breaker for the circuit.

**Verify with a non-contact voltage tester before removing any plate,** and test the tester on a known-live outlet first so you know the tester itself works. A tester with a dead battery reads "safe" on everything.

Remove the wall plate. Do not remove the device itself unless you are comfortable with electrical work, and do not pull the outlet out of the box — you do not need to for this job.

## Step 3: Fit a Foam Gasket

Foam gaskets are pre-cut pieces of closed-cell foam sized to fit behind the wall plate, with holes for the device and the plate screws.

Remove the plate, press the gasket onto the device face, and reinstall the plate through the gasket's screw holes. Trim to fit if needed.

**These exist specifically for this purpose and are the right first choice.** They seal the gap between the plate and the wall, which is where most of the air comes through, and they do not require you to open the box or touch wiring.

They are inexpensive and install in about a minute per outlet.

## Step 4: Seal the Box-to-Drywall Gap

The gasket only handles the plate. There may also be a gap between the electrical box and the drywall around it.

With the plate off, look at the edge of the box where it meets the wall. If you can see a gap, seal it with a thin bead of caulk.

**Use caulk, not expanding foam, in this location.** Low-expansion foam is appropriate for larger gaps around the box in the cavity, but you cannot see what you are doing inside the wall, and standard high-expansion foam can deform a plastic box or push a device out of alignment.

For a visible gap bigger than a few millimetres, a small amount of low-expansion foam designed for windows and doors is acceptable, applied sparingly.

## Step 5: Do Not Block What Needs to Stay Open

This is the part that gets skipped, and it matters.

**Do not seal around recessed light fixtures unless they are rated for contact with insulation.** Many are not, and sealing them creates a fire hazard. Look for the IC rating on the fixture.

**Do not block combustion air openings.** If you have a gas furnace, water heater, or fireplace in the space, it needs combustion air. Sealing up a house tightly without checking this is genuinely dangerous — it can cause backdrafting and carbon monoxide problems.

**Do not block bathroom and kitchen exhaust paths.** Sealing leaks is good; sealing required ventilation is not. Your range hood, bath fan, and dryer still need to move air.

If the house has any combustion appliance, this is the point at which a conversation with a professional is worth having before you tighten the house further.

## Step 6: Reinstall and Verify

Refit the plate. Restore the breaker.

Re-run the smoke test. The smoke should no longer drift inward.

**Also verify the outlet still works** with a plug-in tester or a lamp, and check that the plate sits flat and is not bowed by the gasket.

## Step 7: Address the Source, Not Just the Symptom

Sealing the outlet stops the air coming in at that point. It does not stop air entering the wall cavity.

The air will find another exit — or, if you have sealed enough exits, it simply stops entering as readily because the pressure path is broken. This is why air sealing works cumulatively: sealing many small leaks produces a result greater than the sum of individual ones.

**The highest-value companion jobs are the big openings:** the sill plate where the wall meets the foundation, the top plate where the wall meets the attic, and any penetrations for plumbing, wiring, or vents through the exterior shell.

If you are doing this as part of a broader effort, an energy audit gives you the sequence.

## Why This Works

The mechanism is the stack effect, and understanding it shows both why this works and where its limits are.

In heating season, warm air escapes through leaks high in the house. That lowers the pressure low in the house. Outside air then enters through every available opening low in the structure — including the holes that electrical boxes make in exterior-wall drywall. Sealing those holes removes the intake path, so less outside air is drawn in and less warm air is pushed out.

This is also why the job is worth doing per-unit-cost. Each individual leak is small, which makes any single one feel not worth fixing. But a typical house has dozens of these penetrations, and the total is a meaningful share of the air changes per hour that your heating system has to pay for.

The safety limits follow from the same principle. You are deliberately reducing uncontrolled air exchange in a house that may contain combustion appliances and that certainly contains electrical devices. Reducing leakage is only safe if the intentional ventilation and combustion air paths remain intact — which is exactly why step 5 is part of the job rather than a footnote.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not seal outlets on interior walls.** They cannot be a source of outside air, and you will have wasted the effort.

**Do not work on a live circuit.** Switch off the breaker and verify with a working tester before removing a plate.

**Do not use standard expanding foam around an electrical box.** It can deform the box and push the device out of alignment. Use pre-cut gaskets and caulk for the visible gap.

**Do not seal recessed lights unless they are IC-rated.** Non-rated fixtures need clearance, and sealing them is a fire hazard.

**Do not block combustion air if you have gas appliances.** Tightening a house with combustion appliances in it needs that checked first.

**Do not seal required ventilation paths.** Exhaust fans and dryer vents must still work.

**Do not skip the smoke test.** A cold surface feels like a draft and is not one. Test before you seal.

## FAQ

**Is air from outlets actually a significant source of heat loss?**
Individually, no. Collectively, across dozens of penetrations, yes — it is one of the better-value air sealing jobs because the material cost is very low and the count is high.

**Are foam outlet gaskets safe?**
Yes. They are made for this, they sit outside the electrical box, and they do not contact wiring. They are the appropriate product.

**Can I just put tape over the outlets?**
Do not. Tape over an outlet is a hazard, leaves residue, and does not seal properly. Use a gasket behind the plate.

**Why do I feel it more in winter than summer?**
The stack effect is strongest when the indoor–outdoor temperature difference is largest. The same leaks are there in summer; the driving force is weaker.

**Will sealing these cause moisture problems?**
Sealing a reasonable number of leaks generally improves comfort without creating moisture issues in most houses. If you seal the house very tightly and have persistent humidity or combustion appliances, mechanical ventilation becomes more important — which is a separate decision.

**Do I need to seal every outlet in the house?**
Only exterior-wall penetrations matter, and interior ones are not worth doing. Focus on exterior walls.

**What about switch plates on exterior walls?**
Same treatment. They are another hole in the same drywall.

**Is this the same as insulating the wall?**
No. This seals air leakage; insulation slows conductive heat transfer. They are different problems and both matter, but sealing air is usually cheaper and more effective per dollar.
