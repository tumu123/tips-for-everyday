Curtains sit in the worst position in the house: a vertical surface directly in the path of every open window, at the point where indoor warm air meets outdoor cold air. That is where condensation forms, where cooking grease drifts, and where ultraviolet light breaks down dye for six hours a day. A curtain in a sunny window can accumulate more airborne soil in a year than a floor mat does in three.

They also get washed less than anything else in the house, usually because of one sentence on the care label: **dry clean only**.

That sentence is worth understanding before you pay $12 to $18 a panel for something you can usually do at home. In most cases it is a disclaimer, not a fabric fact.

## What "Dry Clean Only" Actually Means

Care labels are legal protection for the manufacturer, not a chemistry lesson. A brand that puts "dry clean only" on a polyester panel is protecting itself against a customer who washes it hot, tumbles it dry, and then complains that it shrank. The label costs them nothing and shifts the risk to you.

The useful question is not what the label says but what the fabric is. Four things actually make a curtain unsafe to wash:

| Condition | Why it fails in water |
|---|---|
| A bonded or coated backing (blackout, thermal) | The coating is glued on. Agitation and heat delaminate it into a cracked, peeling sheet |
| Stiffened pleats or a glued heading (Roman shades, some pleated panels) | The stiffener is water-soluble or heat-set; the pleats collapse permanently |
| Mixed dye lots in one panel | One colorway bleeds onto the other, and the bleed is not reversible |
| Trim that is not colorfast (contrast piping, tassels, velvet bands) | The trim bleeds onto the main field |

If none of those apply, the panel is a piece of fabric, and fabric can be washed. The exception list is short, and it is about construction rather than fiber.

## Step 1: Identify the Fiber, Then Pick the Method

The fiber decides the water temperature, the agitation, and — most importantly — whether the panel can be dried.

| Fiber | Shrinks in water? | Machine wash? | Dryer? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Polyester, nylon, acrylic** | Barely (under 1%) | Yes, cold, gentle | Low heat only | The easiest category. Most ready-made curtains are this |
| **Cotton** | Yes, 2–5% untreated | Yes, cold, gentle | No | Shrinkage is mostly heat; hanging damp recovers most of it |
| **Linen** | Yes, 3–8% | Hand wash or gentle | No | Wrinkles by design; do not fight it |
| **Cotton-poly blend** | 1–3% | Yes, cold | Low | The most forgiving natural-look option |
| **Viscose / rayon** | Yes, and it loses strength when wet | Hand wash only | No | Heavy when wet; support it, never wring |
| **Silk** | Yes, and it water-spots | Hand wash only | No | See [the delicates guide](/home-hacks/laundry/wash-delicates-wool-silk/) |
| **Wool** | Felts — irreversible | No | No | Do not wash. Brush and steam |
| **Blackout / thermal coated** | Depends on the base cloth | Spot clean or hand wash, no agitation | No | Machine washing is what kills the coating |

Two ambiguous cases worth naming. **"Polyester linen-look"** is polyester with a textured weave — treat it as polyester, not linen, and it will not shrink. **"Cotton duck"** is a heavy, tightly woven cotton that shrinks more than ordinary cotton, and it is common in tab-top panels, so allow for 4–5% and hang it damp.

## Step 2: Run the Hem Test Before Anything Else

Every panel has a hem allowance folded to the back — usually 2 to 4 inches of the same fabric, sewn where nobody sees it. Cut a one-inch square from the inside of that allowance and do this:

1. Soak it in a cup of warm water with a drop of dish soap for 30 minutes.
2. Blot it dry, then lay it flat and let it air-dry.
3. Measure it against the original. Note any change in length and any color that ran into the water.

This costs ten minutes and answers the three questions the label will not: does it shrink, does it bleed, does the coating survive water. If the sample shrinks more than 3%, plan to hang the panels damp and stretch them back to length while wet. If the water turns color, hand wash that panel alone.

The same test tells you whether the panel is dry-clean-only in practice. Coated blackout fabric will show a wrinkled, stiffened, or separated backing within an hour.

## Step 3: Take Them Down Properly

Order matters, because a panel that goes back up wrong is worse than a dirty one.

1. **Photograph the heading before you unhook it.** Pleat hooks, rings, and pinch-pleat folds are easy to reassemble wrong and hard to describe.
2. **Vacuum both sides** with the upholstery brush, top to bottom. This is not optional. Dust that goes into the wash becomes mud in the fibers, and mud is much harder to rinse out than dust is to vacuum up.
3. **Remove every hook, ring, and clip.** A single metal hook left in will rust, snag, and can crack a drum.
4. **Number the panels** with a safety pin on the back hem if they are not interchangeable. Panels fade at different rates depending on sun exposure, and swapping them shows.

## Step 4: Wash

Never wash more than one panel at a time unless the machine is large and the panels are sheer. A wet cotton panel is heavy, and an unbalanced load is what tears headings. The same capacity logic governs every bulky textile in the house, and [washing a duvet at home](/home-hacks/fabrics/wash-duvet-or-comforter/) is decided by drum volume before any technique matters.

- **Water:** cold for anything with cotton, linen, viscose, or a coated backing. Cool or warm is fine for pure polyester.
- **Cycle:** gentle or delicate, low spin — 400 to 600 rpm. A high spin sets creases that need ironing to remove.
- **Detergent:** a tablespoon of mild liquid detergent. Not powder, which can leave undissolved granules in a cold wash, and not fabric softener, which coats the fiber and makes the panel hang stiffly and attract dust faster.
- **Bag it:** a mesh laundry bag for sheer panels prevents the header tape from snagging on the drum.
- **Never mix with towels.** Towels shed lint, and lint on a dark curtain is visible from across the room.

**Hand washing viscose or silk:** fill the tub with cool water and a capful of mild detergent, lower the panel in folded rather than bunched, press it under the water rather than stirring, and let it soak for ten minutes. Viscose loses about half its strength when wet, so lift it with both hands supporting the middle — never by one corner.

## Step 5: The Part That Prevents Shrinking

Shrinkage in cotton and linen is a two-part reaction: water relaxes the fiber, and **heat** contracts it. Most of the shrinkage people blame on washing actually happens in the dryer.

So:

- **Do not tumble dry.** Not on low, not "just for ten minutes." Heat is the mechanism.
- **Do not wring or twist.** Roll the panel in a bath towel and press down on it. The towel takes the bulk of the water without distorting the weave.
- **Hang it back on the rod while still damp.** This is the recovery step. A damp cotton panel weighs several pounds, and its own weight pulls the weave back to length as it dries. Most of a 3% shrink comes back this way.
- **Smooth the hems downward** with your palms while wet, working from the top. Do not pull sideways or you will stretch the width instead.
- **Let it dry fully hanging.** A fan on the floor speeds this considerably and prevents the musty smell that comes from a panel dried against a wall.

If a panel has already shrunk from a previous wash, the recovery is the same: rewet it thoroughly, hang it damp, and weight the bottom hem lightly. Cotton will come back 1 to 3%. Once it has been through a hot dryer, most of that is gone permanently.

## What to Do About Blackout and Thermal Panels

These are the ones genuinely at risk, and the risk is not shrinkage — it is delamination of the acrylic foam or coated backing.

- **Spot clean first.** Most blackout panel soil is at the leading edge and the bottom hem, where hands and floors touch it.
- **If it must be washed**, hand wash in cool water with no agitation, no soaking beyond ten minutes, and hang immediately without wringing.
- **Never machine wash a foam-backed panel.** The foam crumbles into the drum and the panel comes out with a patchy, cracked back that no longer blocks light.
- **Steam instead of washing** for odor and dust. A garment steamer on the face fabric lifts most surface soil and kills the musty smell without water penetrating the backing.

## Drying, Steaming, and Getting Them Back Up

Hang the panels while damp and let gravity do the pressing. Once dry, most fabrics need nothing more.

For cotton and linen, a steamer is far better than an iron — you cannot iron a hanging panel evenly, and taking it down to iron defeats the purpose. Steam vertically from the top down, holding the head an inch off the fabric, and let the weight of the panel pull the creases out.

For pleated headings, steam the pleats back into shape with your fingers rather than pressing them flat. A flat-ironed pleat looks worse than a wrinkled panel.

Rehang in the reverse order you took them down, matching the photograph. Check that all panels hang to the same length at the floor — if one is an inch short, it is either a different fabric lot or it shrank, and neither is fixed by pulling.

## How Often Is Actually Necessary

The honest answer is less often than the cleaning-industry chart suggests, and more often than most people do it. Window coverings, blinds, and upholstery all sit on different schedules, which is what the [full cleaning frequency chart](/home-hacks/cleaning/cleaning-frequency-chart/) is for.

| Room | Interval | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Bedroom, low traffic | 12 months | Dust and skin cells only |
| Living room, street-facing | 6 months | Road dust and pollen settle on the face |
| Kitchen or open-plan adjacent | 3 to 4 months | Aerosolized cooking grease, which yellows over time |
| Bathroom | 3 months | Humidity and condensation |
| Any room with a smoker | 2 to 3 months | Tar deposits, which washing does not fully remove |
| Any room with a pet that leans on them | 4 to 6 months | Oils and hair at the bottom third |

Between washes, vacuum the panels monthly with the upholstery brush. That single habit roughly doubles the interval, because most of what accumulates is loose particulate that has not yet bonded to the fiber. Coverings that never come down need a different approach, and [cleaning blinds without taking them down](/home-hacks/cleaning/clean-blinds-without-removing/) is the same dust problem from the other side.

If the only problem is a stale smell, do not wash. Take the panel down, hang it outside in shade for an afternoon, and steam it. Odor is volatile; it leaves with airflow.

## When Washing Is the Wrong Answer

Three situations where the panel should be replaced rather than cleaned:

- **Sun damage.** The leading edge is faded and the fabric tears with light pressure. UV has broken the polymer chains, and no wash restores them.
- **Dry rot on old lining.** The lining crumbles to powder when rubbed between your fingers. That is hydrolyzed acetate, and it will disintegrate in water.
- **Yellowed grease in a kitchen.** Polymerized cooking oil bonds to the fiber and turns yellow with heat. It does not come out with detergent, and it comes back within weeks if it comes out at all.

For everything else, the sequence is the same: read the construction rather than the label, run the hem test, wash cold and gently, and let gravity do the drying. The panels that survive longest are the ones that were never put in a dryer.

## FAQ

**Can I wash curtains that say dry clean only?**

Usually yes, if the panel has no bonded backing, no glued pleats, and no non-colorfast trim. Run the hem test first. The label is a liability disclaimer more than a fiber property, and most ready-made curtains are polyester, which washes without shrinking.

**Why did my curtains shrink after washing?**

Almost always because of the dryer, not the wash. Cotton and linen shrink when heat contracts relaxed fibers. Wash cold, roll in a towel instead of wringing, and hang the panel damp so its own weight pulls it back to length.

**Can I put curtains in the dryer?**

Only pure polyester, and only on low heat. Cotton, linen, viscose, silk, wool, and anything with a blackout or thermal coating should never see the inside of a dryer. The heat sets shrinkage and cracks the coating.

**How do I wash blackout curtains without ruining them?**

Hand wash in cool water for no more than ten minutes with no agitation, then hang immediately without wringing. Never machine wash a foam-backed panel — the backing crumbles and stops blocking light. For routine refresh, steam the face fabric instead.

**Can I wash curtains with the hooks left in?**

No. Metal hooks rust, snag the fabric, and can crack the washer drum. Remove every hook, ring, and clip, and photograph the heading first so you can reassemble the pleats correctly.

**Do I need to vacuum curtains before washing?**

Yes. Dust that goes into the wash turns into mud in the weave, and mud is much harder to rinse out than dust is to vacuum off. Vacuum both sides with the upholstery brush, top to bottom, before the panel goes anywhere near water.

**How do I get wrinkles out of curtains without an iron?**

Hang them damp and let gravity pull the creases out, then steam vertically from the top down with a garment steamer held an inch off the fabric. Taking a panel down to iron it usually puts more creases in than it removes.

**Do curtains need to be washed if they only smell musty?**

No. Odor is volatile — take the panel down, hang it in shade outdoors for an afternoon, and steam it. Washing a panel that is only stale adds wear without solving anything the air did not.

## Bottom Line

Identify the fiber and the construction, not the label. Run the hem test before committing a whole panel. Wash cold, gently, one panel at a time, with a tablespoon of mild liquid detergent and no softener. Then let gravity dry it on the rod. Heat is what shrinks curtains, and the dryer is where the heat is.
