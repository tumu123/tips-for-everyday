On the first warm afternoon in late September, the south-facing wall turns black. Thousands of small black-and-red insects, each about half an inch long, packed along the foundation line, up the siding, around the window frames, and on the warmest part of the door threshold. They are sluggish, easy to sweep, and back the next afternoon.

This is the annual boxelder bug gathering, and it produces more unnecessary pesticide use than almost any other household insect, because the visual alarm is enormous and the actual threat is close to zero. Boxelder bugs do not bite, do not sting, do not chew wood, do not damage insulation, do not breed indoors, and do not carry disease. They stain fabric when crushed, they smell faintly when disturbed, and their carcasses feed other pests. That is the complete list of what they cost you.

So the honest guide is mostly about restraint — with two situations that genuinely justify action. The seasonal timing that governs outdoor insect movement in general is covered in [how to keep bugs out of the house in summer](/seasonal/seasons/keep-bugs-out-of-house-summer/); this is the autumn case, and the sealing work that both problems share is in [how to seal gaps around doors and windows](/fix-it/maintenance/seal-gaps-doors-windows/).

## Why They All Appear on One Wall

The behavior is a sun-tracking response, not an infestation.

Boxelder bugs feed in summer on the seeds and soft tissue of boxelder trees, and less commonly on silver maple, ash, and a few fruit trees. As days shorten and nights cool, they stop feeding and start looking for a place to spend the winter. Their cue is warmth, and the warmest surface on most houses in the afternoon is the south or west wall, especially masonry, dark siding, or anything that holds heat after the sun drops.

They gather there in the afternoon and disperse at night. Then, as the nights get colder, they begin working into any narrow gap they can find — under siding, behind a shutter, around a window frame, through a weep hole in brick, along a door sweep that no longer touches the threshold. Once inside a wall cavity or an attic, they are out of the weather and out of sight until spring.

That is the one genuinely useful fact in this whole story: **they do not breed indoors.** There is no host plant, no seed, nothing to eat inside a wall. Every bug you find inside in January is a bug that went in and stayed, and every bug you find on a February windowsill is one that woke up and is trying to leave. The population indoors is always a subset of the population outside, and it shrinks over the winter rather than growing.

## What They Actually Damage

Worth stating plainly, because the spray advertising does not.

- **Fabric and walls.** A crushed boxelder bug leaves a reddish-brown stain that is difficult to remove from light textiles, upholstery, and painted surfaces. This is the real cost of an indoor invasion — not the insects, the marks.
- **Nothing structural.** No wood damage, no wire damage, no insulation damage.
- **Nothing agricultural indoors.** No houseplants are at risk; they are not a greenhouse pest.
- **An olfactory nuisance.** Disturbed bugs release a mildly unpleasant odor. It is nothing like a stink bug.
- **A secondary pest invitation.** Accumulated dead boxelder bugs behind a baseboard or in a window frame are a protein source for carpet beetle larvae and dermestids. This is not a reason to panic; it is a reason to vacuum up the dead ones rather than let them pile up.

## The Decision: Treat or Ignore

The two situations that justify action:

1. **A repeat invasion of the interior** — not a few bugs on a wall in October, but dozens inside the living space every season, year after year, indicating a real entry path that should be sealed anyway for heating and moisture reasons.
2. **A host tree immediately adjacent to the house** — a boxelder or silver maple whose branches overhang the roof, producing a population concentrated on one elevation. Removing or pruning that tree reduces the source rather than treating the symptom.

The situations that do not justify action:

- Bugs on the outside wall in the afternoon. That is normal autumn behavior for a native insect.
- A handful indoors in October. Vacuum them.
- Bugs on a windowsill in February. They are trying to get out. Let them, or vacuum.
- Bugs on the sunny side of a tree trunk in the yard. Leave them.

Homeowners spend real money on perimeter sprays every September for a bug that will be gone in six weeks and whose indoor population cannot reproduce. The spray does not even kill the population — it kills the ones present on the surface at application, and the next warm afternoon brings more from the trees.

## The Window That Actually Matters: September and October

If you are going to do anything outdoors, timing is what makes it work.

**Late September through mid-October**, before the bulk of the population moves into the walls, is the only period when exterior work affects how many end up inside. After they are in the cavity, no exterior treatment reaches them.

What is effective then:

- **Wash the gathering off the wall.** A hose with a spray nozzle knocks them down; a bucket of soapy water (a tablespoon of dish soap per gallon) kills what it hits. This is a physical removal that must be repeated, but it interrupts the accumulation without any pesticide on your house.
- **Fix the entry paths.** Caulk around window frames, seal the gap where the siding meets the foundation, replace worn door sweeps and the weatherstripping at thresholds, screen the weep holes in brick veneer, and check the utility penetrations. This work is worth doing regardless of the bugs, and it is the same route used for [sealing gaps around doors and windows](/fix-it/maintenance/seal-gaps-doors-windows/).
- **Deal with the tree.** If a boxelder or silver maple overhangs the roof, prune it back or have it removed. This is the source control, and it is the only measure with a multi-year effect.
- **Treat the tree's trunk band if the population is severe.** Boxelder bugs congregate on the sunny side of the trunk before moving to the house. A professional can treat that band; a homeowner with a hose can knock them off repeatedly. Do not spray the whole tree — the bugs are on the seed-bearing female trees, and a broad application harms pollinators for a marginal gain.

## What to Do With the Ones Already Inside

Inside, the method is removal without crushing.

- **Vacuum them up**, then empty the canister outdoors. Put a nylon stocking over the hose end if you want to avoid the smell inside the machine.
- **Sweep them into a bucket of soapy water.** The soap breaks surface tension and they drown quickly, which avoids both the stain and the odor.
- **Do not squash them on a wall or a curtain.** The stain is the main damage they do.
- **Do not spray indoors.** An aerosol in a living room for an insect that cannot reproduce there is an exposure with no benefit, and it leaves residue on surfaces you touch.
- **Seal the interior gap you can see.** If they are coming through a window frame, that frame has a gap. Caulk it and the flow stops.

In late winter, when the survivors wake up and head for the light, you will see them on windowsills and around light fixtures. That is the exit, not a new infestation. Open the window or vacuum.

## How They Differ From Stink Bugs

These two get confused constantly, and the difference changes what you should do.

| Feature | Boxelder bug | Brown marmorated stink bug |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Elongated oval, flat-backed | Shield-shaped, wider at the shoulders |
| Color | Black with red-orange margins | Mottled brown with white banding on the antennae |
| Behavior when disturbed | Runs, may fly short distance | Drops, or flies; releases a strong odor |
| Smell when crushed | Faint, mildly unpleasant | Strong and persistent |
| Indoor activity in winter | Mostly dormant, moves to light in spring | Active intermittently all winter, flies to lights |
| Damage | Stain only | Stain; also a serious agricultural pest outdoors |

The practical consequence: stink bugs are more active indoors through the winter and are worth trapping deliberately — a foil roasting pan with soapy water and a small light in a dark room collects them. Boxelder bugs mostly sit still, so removal by vacuum is sufficient.

## Why Spraying the Exterior Wall Is the Wrong Move

Three reasons, in order of how much they matter.

**It does not change the population.** The bugs on your wall are a small fraction of the bugs on the trees. Killing the ones present on a Tuesday does not affect the ones arriving Wednesday.

**It is temporary by chemistry.** Residual insecticide on siding breaks down under ultraviolet light and rain. The effective window on a sun-exposed wall in September is measured in days to a couple of weeks, which is shorter than the invasion period.

**It creates the second problem.** Dead insects on a wall and in window frames attract carpet beetle larvae and other dermestids, which do damage textiles. Trading a nuisance for a textile pest is a bad exchange.

There is a legitimate use for a professional application: a barrier treatment around the foundation and entry points in early autumn, in the specific case where a house has had repeated interior invasions and the sealing work is not yet complete. That is a targeted, time-limited use, not an annual ritual.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not fog the attic.** If they are in the wall cavity, fog does not reach them and the residue is permanent.

**Do not spray the host tree on your own.** Broad-spectrum spray on a mature tree for a nuisance insect is a poor trade, and the bugs are concentrated on female seed-bearing trees rather than spread throughout.

**Do not crush them indoors.** The stain is the only real damage they cause.

**Do not assume spring sightings mean a new infestation.** They are waking up and leaving. The population indoors declines over winter.

**Do not treat a wall because it looks alarming.** Density on an exterior wall is not a measure of interior risk. The measure is how many get inside.

## FAQ

**Do boxelder bugs bite or sting?**

No. They have mouthparts for piercing plant tissue and seeds and cannot bite skin. They do not sting and they do not transmit disease. An itchy spot after handling them is irritation from the fluid they release, not a bite.

**Why are there so many on one side of the house?**

They follow warmth. The south or west elevation holds heat longest in the afternoon, and the bugs congregate there before working into gaps as nights cool. The concentration is a sun-exposure effect, not an indication that the wall is infested.

**Do they breed inside the house?**

No. They feed on boxelder and maple seeds outdoors, and there is nothing for them to eat or lay eggs on inside a wall cavity. Every indoor bug came from outside and the indoor population only declines over winter.

**How do I get rid of them permanently?**

You cannot remove an insect that lives on trees in your neighborhood. You can reduce how many get inside by sealing gaps, removing or pruning a host tree that overhangs the roof, and washing the gathering off the wall in early autumn. Permanent elimination is not a realistic goal; a large reduction is.

**What are the red stains on my curtain?**

Crushed boxelder bugs. Their body fluid is red-brown and stains light fabric. Vacuum or sweep them rather than crushing them, especially near curtains, upholstery, and painted walls.

**Are boxelder bugs the same as stink bugs?**

No. Stink bugs are shield-shaped, mottled brown, and release a strong odor when disturbed. They are also more active indoors through the winter. The treatments differ: boxelder bugs need sealing, stink bugs benefit from indoor traps.

**Do I need an exterminator for boxelder bugs?**

Usually not. A perimeter application in early autumn has a limited place if a house has a history of interior invasions and the sealing is not finished, but for the typical case on the exterior wall, washing them off and sealing the gaps does more and costs less.

## Bottom Line

Boxelder bugs on the south wall in October are a native insect doing what it does every year, and the list of things they actually damage is one item long: fabric they get crushed against. They do not breed indoors, so the ones you find inside are a shrinking subset that came in through a gap you can seal. Fix the gaps, wash the afternoon gathering off the wall while it is happening, and deal with a host tree that overhangs the roof. Skip the annual perimeter spray. It does not reduce the population, and the dead bugs it leaves behind feed the carpet beetles that do real damage.
