**First, distinguish this from a different problem.** If the door *rubs or jams as you close it*, that's a fitting problem, not a latching problem — see [How to Fix a Sticking Door](/fix-it/home-repair/fix-sticking-door/). If it makes noise when it swings, see [How to Fix a Squeaky Door Hinge](/fix-it/home-repair/fix-squeaky-door-hinge/).

This guide is for the specific case where **the door closes fine but won't stay shut** — the latch bolt doesn't enter the strike plate hole, or it enters and springs back out.

In the large majority of cases, this is not a problem with the latch itself. It's that the latch bolt and the hole in the frame **no longer line up**.

---

## Step 0: Find Out What Moved

The door latch and the strike plate were aligned at some point. Something shifted. Finding what determines the fix.

**Do this quick test:**

1. **Close the door slowly** and watch the latch bolt as it meets the frame
2. **Note where it lands relative to the hole** — too high, too low, or not far enough in

| Observation | What moved | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Latch hits the frame above or below the hole | Door has sagged, or the frame has settled | Adjust the strike plate, or fix the hinge |
| Latch enters the hole but springs back out | Strike plate is too far out; bolt isn't catching | Move the strike plate inward |
| Latch won't extend at all, or feels gritty | Latch mechanism is dirty or binding inside the door | Clean and lubricate the latch |
| Latch enters but the door still pushes open | Strike plate hole is worn, or the plate is too far out | Adjust or shim the plate |
| Door has to be lifted or pushed to latch | hinge wear or a settling frame | Hinge repair (see below) |
| Only happens in humid months | Seasonal wood movement | Adjust with the season in mind |

**The most common cause by far is a door that has sagged slightly on its hinges** over years of use, dropping the latch bolt below the hole. Gravity does this slowly and it goes unnoticed until one day the door stops latching.

---

## Step 1: Mark Exactly Where the Latch Lands

This removes the guesswork, and it's the step that makes the rest work.

1. **Cover the strike plate** and the latch bolt with lipstick, chalk, or a marker
2. **Close the door** and let the latch contact the plate — don't force it
3. **Open the door** and look at the mark

You'll see exactly where the bolt is hitting relative to the hole. That tells you which direction to move the plate and roughly how far.

**This takes one minute and eliminates trial and error.** Without it, people file the plate in the wrong direction and make the problem worse.

---

## Step 2: Adjust the Strike Plate

For a misalignment of a few millimetres, moving the plate is the whole fix.

**Small adjustment (1–3 mm):**

1. **Loosen the strike plate screws** slightly
2. **Tap the plate** in the direction it needs to move, using a hammer and a block of wood as a buffer
3. **Retighten** and test
4. **Repeat** until the latch catches cleanly

**Larger adjustment (or if the screws won't hold):**

1. **Remove the plate**
2. **Fill the old screw holes** with glued matchsticks or golf tees, cut flush — this gives the screws something to bite into
3. **Reposition the plate** per your mark
4. **Drill new pilot holes** and reinstall
5. **Test**

**If the plate needs to move inward** (latch doesn't reach far enough), the fix is a **shim behind the plate**. Cut a thin piece of cardboard or use a wooden shim, place it behind the strike plate, and reinstall. This moves the catching surface outward toward the latch without moving the plate's screw positions.

**If the hole itself is too small or worn,** file the opening in the direction of your mark. Remove the plate, file a little, test, and repeat — metal is easy to remove and impossible to add.

---

## Step 3: Fix a Sagging Door at the Hinges

If the door has dropped, adjusting the strike plate is treating the symptom. Fixing the hinge addresses the cause — and it's often easier than it sounds.

**First: tighten everything.** Open the door and check every hinge screw, top to bottom. Loose hinge screws are the most common cause of sag, and simply tightening them frequently solves the problem.

**If the holes are stripped** (the screw turns without gripping), the hole needs filling:

1. **Remove the screw**
2. **Insert a glued matchstick or golf tee** into the hole, break it off flush
3. **Let the glue set** for a few minutes
4. **Reinstall the screw** — it now has material to grip

**The upgrade that actually prevents recurrence:** replace the top hinge's short screw with a **long screw (2.5–3 inches)** that reaches through the door frame into the wall stud behind it. This anchors the hinge to structural framing rather than just the thin door jamb, and it pulls the door back up.

1. **Remove one screw from the top hinge** — the one that goes into the frame, not the door
2. **Drive the long screw** in its place
3. **Watch the door lift** as the screw draws in
4. **Test the latch**

This single screw is often enough to correct years of sag, and it's the most underused fix in this entire guide.

**If the hinge itself is bent or worn,** replacing it is inexpensive and straightforward — swap one at a time so the door stays hung.

---

## Step 4: Deal With a Sticky Latch

If the latch bolt moves sluggishly or won't extend, the mechanism needs cleaning — but not oiling.

**Do not use oil or WD-40 on a latch.** Oil attracts dust and eventually forms a sticky varnish that makes the problem worse. Use a **dry lubricant**: graphite powder or a PTFE spray.

1. **Extend the latch** and wipe the bolt clean
2. **Apply dry lubricant** to the bolt and into the mechanism
3. **Work the handle** several times to distribute it
4. **Wipe off the excess**

**If the latch is stiff from paint,** carefully clean around it with a utility knife along the edge of the latch plate, then lubricate.

**If the door handle itself feels loose or the latch doesn't fully retract when you turn it,** the issue is inside the lockset, and replacing the latch assembly is usually faster than repairing it.

## Seasonal and Structural Causes

**Humidity.** Wood absorbs and releases moisture, and doors swell in humid months. A door that latches in winter may not in summer. If this is your pattern, adjust the strike plate with a little extra clearance, or adjust it seasonally.

**Foundation or frame movement.** If the gap around the door is uneven and getting worse, or if several doors in the house are affected, the frame may be moving rather than the door. That's a structural question worth having assessed.

**A door that swings open on its own** is a different issue — the frame is out of plumb, meaning the hinge side isn't vertical. Check with a level.

---

## When to Call a Professional

- **The frame is visibly out of square** and getting worse
- **Multiple doors are affected** — suggests structural movement
- **The hinge mortises are damaged** or the wood around them is split
- **You've adjusted everything and it still won't latch** — the latch or lockset may need replacing
- **It's an exterior door with security implications** — a door that doesn't latch securely is a real concern

## Why This Works

**Misalignment is a geometry problem, and the mark makes it visible.** Guessing at which direction to move a strike plate produces a lot of filing in the wrong place. A transfer mark from the latch shows the exact offset in one step.

**Doors sag because hinge screws pull out of the jamb, not because hinges wear out.** The jamb is thin trim board with limited holding power, and the top hinge carries the most load. A long screw that reaches the stud behind transfers that load to actual framing — which is why one screw can correct years of drop.

**Dry lubricants work where oils fail.** Latch mechanisms are exposed to dust, and oil plus dust forms a gummy residue that eventually seizes the mechanism. Graphite and PTFE lubricate without attracting particles.

**Adjusting the plate treats symptoms; fixing the hinge treats causes.** Moving a strike plate works and is often sufficient, but if the door is sagging, the underlying drop will continue. Doing both — reposition the plate and anchor the top hinge — produces a fix that lasts.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Filing the plate without marking | Removes metal in the wrong place | Mark the contact point first |
| Assuming the latch is broken | Replacing hardware for nothing | Check alignment — it's usually that |
| Using oil on the latch | Attracts dust, eventually seizes | Dry graphite or PTFE |
| Overtightening hinge screws | Strips the hole further | Fill with a glued matchstick first |
| Only adjusting the strike plate on a sagging door | The door keeps dropping | Also anchor the top hinge with a long screw |
| Forcing the door to latch | Damages the latch and frame | Find the misalignment |
| Ignoring an out-of-plumb frame | Door swings on its own | Check with a level |
| Leaving an exterior door unlatched | Security risk | Prioritize fixing it |

## Frequently Asked Questions
**Why does my door close but not latch?**
The latch bolt and the strike plate hole are no longer aligned — usually because the door has sagged slightly on its hinges.

**How do I know which way to move the strike plate?**
Mark the latch with lipstick or chalk, close the door, and look at where it contacts the plate. That shows the exact direction and distance.

**Will tightening the hinge screws fix it?**
Often yes. Loose hinge screws are the most common cause of sag, and tightening them frequently resolves latching problems immediately.

**What's the long screw trick?**
Replacing one screw in the top hinge with a 2.5–3 inch screw that reaches through the jamb into the wall stud. It pulls the door back up and prevents future sag.

**The screw just spins and won't grip. What now?**
The hole is stripped. Fill it with a glued matchstick or golf tee, let it set, then reinstall the screw.

**Should I oil a sticky latch?**
No. Use dry graphite or PTFE. Oil attracts dust and makes latches worse over time.

**Why does my door only fail to latch in summer?**
Humidity makes wood swell. Adjust the plate with slightly more clearance, or accept seasonal adjustment.

**When is this a structural problem?**
When the frame is out of square, the gap is uneven and worsening, or several doors are affected.
