Spring storms differ from winter storms in a way that changes what you prepare for. Winter brings snow load and ice. Spring brings **wind, hail, and water** — and it brings them with far less warning, because convective storms form and arrive within hours.

The damage profiles are also different. Wind damage starts at the roof edge and any unanchored object. Hail damage is concentrated on the roof, siding, and any horizontal surface. Water damage comes from rain driven sideways into places rain never normally reaches.

---

## Step 1: Inspect the roof edge, seals, and flashing — from the ground

Use binoculars. Getting on a roof to check for damage is how most ladder injuries happen, and it isn't necessary for the initial pass.

1. **Look at the ridge line** for lifted or missing shingles. Ridge caps are the first thing wind takes.
2. **Check the rake and eave edges** for missing pieces, which propagate inward.
3. **Inspect the flashing** around the chimney, vents, and any skylight. Flashing is where most roof leaks originate — not the field of the roof.
4. **Look for cracked or missing sealant** at the pipe boots and vent collars. A failed boot is a small, cheap fix that becomes a ceiling stain.
5. **Check the gutters for loose hangers.** A gutter that has pulled loose on one side comes down in a windstorm and damages whatever is below.
6. **Look at the fascia for soft or stained wood**, which indicates an existing leak.
7. **Check the chimney cap and any vents on the roof** for corrosion and secure attachment.

**If you find small issues, fix them now.** Roof sealant for cracked seals and loose flashing, and a couple of replacement shingles, cost very little. A leak discovered during a storm costs far more.

## Step 2: Fix the water paths before heavy rain

Spring storms drop large volumes of rain quickly, and drainage that handles a normal rain can fail under a burst.

1. **Clean the gutters and clear the downspouts.** Spring pollen and winter debris are both factors; a partial clog becomes a full one under heavy flow.
2. **Check the downspout extensions** — water must discharge 4 to 6 feet from the foundation.
3. **Verify the grading** slopes away from the house.
4. **Test the sump pump** by pouring water into the pit. It will be needed exactly when the power might also be out, so confirm it works now.
5. **Consider a battery backup for the sump pump** if your basement floods. Storms producing heavy rain also produce outages, and a pump without power is no pump.
6. **Clear window well drains** if you have them.
7. **Check the foundation for cracks** and seal them. Driven rain finds hairline cracks that ordinary rain doesn't.
8. **Look at where the ground is bare or eroded** — areas that flood repeatedly need grading or a drainage solution, not annual sandbags.

## Step 3: Secure everything outside

Anything loose becomes a projectile in wind, and projectile damage is not covered as generously as people assume.

1. **Bring in or anchor:** patio furniture, grills, trash bins, planters, garden ornaments, and kids' toys.
2. **Secure trampolines.** These are the most-replaced outdoor item after storms, and they travel. Anchor them or dismantle them if heavy weather is forecast.
3. **Take down umbrellas, awnings, and hanging planters.**
4. **Check fence posts** for rot at the base and reinforce loose posts. Fences fail as a run, not as individual panels.
5. **Secure or store the grill and propane tank** — a detached tank is genuinely dangerous.
6. **Move vehicles away from trees** and into a garage or carport if possible.

## Step 4: Deal with the trees

Falling branches cause more home damage in spring storms than anything else, and it's the most preventable.

1. **Remove dead or diseased trees** near the house. A dead tree is a known hazard.
2. **Prune branches overhanging the roof** to at least 10 feet of clearance.
3. **Remove limbs that overhang power lines** — for this, always call the utility. Never work near energized lines.
4. **Thin dense canopies** so wind passes through instead of catching. A dense crown acts like a sail, and the failure is at the trunk.
5. **Check for split trunks or included bark** on large trees near the house. These are structurally weak points and should be assessed by an arborist, not pruned casually.
6. **Cut back shrubs and trees touching the siding** — they hold moisture and give wind leverage.

## Step 5: Protect the vulnerable openings

1. **Check that all windows close and latch properly.** A window that doesn't latch fully is far more likely to break or be forced open.
2. **For storm shutters or panels,** confirm the hardware is present and the panels fit. Finding out they don't fit during a warning is too late.
3. **Check the garage door.** A garage door is the largest opening in most homes and a common failure point in wind. Check the tracks, rollers, and that the door is rated for wind in your area.
4. **Reinforce the garage door** if your area gets high winds — most doors can be strengthened with a simple bracing kit.
5. **Inspect door hinges and deadbolts** on exterior doors. Wind-driven rain through a poorly fitting door damages floors and framing.
6. **Check for gaps around exterior doors** and add weatherstripping, which serves double duty for drafts.

## Step 6: Prepare the utilities and the plan

1. **Trim or remove branches near power lines** — utility's job.
2. **Consider a whole-house surge protector** if you have significant electronics. Power outages and restoration spikes both damage equipment.
3. **Restock storm supplies:** flashlights, batteries, a battery-powered radio, water, non-perishable food, a first aid kit, and medications.
4. **Charge power banks and devices** when a storm is forecast.
5. **Store important documents in waterproof containers** or, better, in digital form — see [How to Organize Paperwork at Home](/organize/closet/organize-paperwork-at-home/).
6. **Know where your utility shutoffs are:** water, gas, and electricity. Being able to shut off gas quickly matters after a storm.
7. **Have a meeting point and an out-of-area contact** agreed in advance. Local phone networks congest during storms; a distant contact is easier to reach.

The four-hour, eight-hour, and twenty-four-hour marks each break something different — [preparing for a power outage](/seasonal/seasons/prepare-for-power-outage-home/) covers all three.

Wind pressure and storm surge are separate problems — [preparing a house for hurricane season](/seasonal/seasons/prepare-home-for-hurricane-season/) covers both.

## Step 7: Document everything before the season

This is the step people skip and regret.

1. **Photograph the exterior** — all four sides, the roof (from the ground if needed), and the garage door.
2. **Photograph the interior** room by room, including valuables.
3. **Keep receipts** for major purchases and improvements in one place.
4. **Review your insurance coverage** before the season: does it cover wind, hail, and water damage? Are the deductibles you can live with?
5. **Note that flood damage is almost always a separate policy** from homeowners insurance. If you have any flood risk, this matters enormously — most people discover it after the storm.

## Step 8: When a warning is issued

- **Move inside and stay away from windows.** Most storm injuries are from glass and flying debris.
- **Close all interior doors** to compartmentalize pressure and reduce damage if a window breaks.
- **Unplug sensitive electronics** if you have time.
- **Fill bathtubs and containers with water** if you're on a well or expect power loss.
- **Avoid the basement during tornado warnings** if it's under a large span — go to an interior room on the lowest floor instead, away from windows.
- **After the storm, photograph any damage before cleaning up**, for insurance purposes.
- **Don't touch downed lines**, and don't assume a line is dead because the neighborhood is dark.

## Why This Works

Spring storm damage follows three distinct physical mechanisms, and each prevention targets one specifically.

Wind damage is a pressure problem. Wind creates positive pressure on the windward side and negative pressure on the leeward side and over the roof, and the combination lifts roofing materials from the edges. This is why ridge caps and rake edges fail first — they're the most exposed and have the least overlap. Securing the edges and maintaining a continuous roof covering addresses the mechanism. Unanchored outdoor objects matter because the kinetic energy of a projectile scales with the square of its speed; a light object lifted by wind becomes dangerous.

Hail damage is energy transfer. Hailstones strike with energy determined by mass and velocity, and the damage is mechanical: granule loss on shingles, cracked vinyl siding, and dented gutters and vents. The prevention is different — it's about the condition of the materials. Aged, brittle shingles crack from impacts that newer ones survive, which is why hail damage is so much worse on an old roof.

Water damage from storms comes mostly from **lateral** rain, not vertical. Rain driven sideways enters gaps that shed normal rain perfectly well: window frames, door seals, vent openings, and hairline foundation cracks. This is why drainage maintenance alone isn't enough, and why sealing the building envelope matters as much as clearing gutters.

The sump pump battery backup deserves its own explanation. The weather that produces basement flooding is the same weather that produces power outages — heavy rain with high wind. A sump pump on grid power fails exactly when it's needed, which is why a battery backup isn't redundancy so much as completing the system.

Tree failure under wind follows from canopy drag. A dense canopy presents a large surface area to the wind, and the mechanical load transfers to the trunk and root plate. Thinning the canopy reduces the drag and lets wind pass through, which is why thinning is more effective than shortening. Included bark — where two trunks grow together with bark trapped between them — creates a structural weak point that fails suddenly under load, and it can't be fixed by pruning.

Documentation matters because insurance claims are decided on evidence of prior condition. Photographs establish what existed before the storm and rule out pre-existing damage, which is often the point of dispute. Reviewing coverage in advance matters because flood damage and wind damage are handled differently, and most homeowners discover the gap afterward.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Climbing on the roof to inspect | Leading cause of ladder injuries | Use binoculars from the ground |
| Ignoring small roof seal cracks | They become leaks under driven rain | Seal them before the season |
| Leaving downspouts discharging at the foundation | Basement flooding | Extend 4 to 6 feet out |
| Relying on a sump pump with no backup | Outages come with the same storms | Add a battery backup |
| Leaving trampolines and furniture unanchored | They become projectiles and travel far | Anchor or store before the season |
| Pruning near power lines yourself | Electrocution risk | Call the utility |
| Topping trees instead of thinning | Doesn't reduce wind drag; causes weak regrowth | Thin the canopy instead |
| Assuming standard insurance covers flood | Flood is almost always a separate policy | Check your coverage before the season |
| Not photographing the house beforehand | Claims become disputed as pre-existing | Document all four sides and interior |
| Standing near windows during a warning | Glass injury is the most common storm injury | Interior room, away from windows |
| Touching a downed line after the outage | Lines can be energized from another source | Stay back and call the utility |
| Cleaning up damage before photographing | Loses evidence for the claim | Photograph first, then clean up |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What should I do to prepare my house for spring storms?**
Inspect the roof edges and flashing from the ground and seal small cracks, clear gutters and downspouts, secure or store all outdoor objects, prune overhanging branches, test the sump pump with a battery backup, and document the exterior with photographs.

**How do I prevent wind damage to my roof?**
Maintain the roof covering continuously — replace lifted or missing shingles and ridge caps promptly, and keep flashing sealed. Wind damage starts at the edges and propagates inward, so edge maintenance matters most.

**Does homeowners insurance cover storm damage?**
Wind and hail damage are typically covered. Flood damage is almost always excluded and requires a separate policy. Review your coverage before storm season, not after.

**How do I protect my house from hail?**
The main factor is the condition of your materials. Aged, brittle shingles crack from impacts that newer ones survive, so a maintained roof fares far better. Impact-resistant shingles and siding help if you're replacing anyway.

**Should I stay in my basement during a tornado warning?**
Only if it's fully underground and not under a large open span. Otherwise go to an interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows, ideally under a sturdy table. Avoid rooms with large windows in any case.

**How much clearance should branches have above my roof?**
At least 10 feet. Also remove dead limbs entirely and thin dense canopies so wind passes through rather than catching in them.

**Do I need a sump pump battery backup?**
If your basement has ever flooded, yes. Power outages come from the same storms that bring heavy rain, and a pump without power provides no protection.

**What should I photograph before storm season?**
All four sides of the exterior, the roof as visible from the ground, the garage door, and each room of the interior including valuables. Keep the photos somewhere accessible from your phone, not only on the home computer.
