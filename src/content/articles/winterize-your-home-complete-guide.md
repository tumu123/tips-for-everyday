Winterizing is usually presented as a checklist, but the items aren't independent. The order in the guide below follows the three ways a home actually fails in winter, because fixing them out of order wastes effort.

**The three failure modes:**

1. **Frozen pipes.** Water expands about 9 percent on freezing, and the burst is discovered at thaw. The most expensive common winter failure.
2. **Ice dams.** Heat escaping into the attic melts snow on the roof; it refreezes at the cold eaves and forces water under the roof covering.
3. **Heat loss.** Air leakage moves heat out far faster than conduction through insulation.

Notice that all three share a cause: **uncontrolled heat and water movement.** Winterizing is the process of controlling both.

---

## Step 1: Start with air sealing — always first

Everything else is less effective until this is done. Air sealing stops convective heat loss, which bypasses insulation entirely.

1. **Find the leaks.** On a cold, windy day, feel along the base of exterior walls, around windows and doors, and at the top of the foundation. Or use a flashlight in the attic during daylight — you'll see light where air moves.
2. **Seal exterior window and door frames** with caulk, inside and out.
3. **Weatherstrip all exterior doors** so they seal on all four sides, not just the bottom.
4. **Replace worn door sweeps** with ones that actually contact the threshold.
5. **Seal utility penetrations** — pipes, cables, and vents passing through exterior walls.
6. **Seal the attic hatch or access door**, then insulate the back of it.
7. **Seal the rim joist area** in the basement. This is a major leak path and rarely sealed.
8. **Seal around recessed lights and ceiling penetrations** in the attic floor, using the correct covers for the fixture type.

**A note on foam:** most expanding foams are not fire-rated and shouldn't be used around heat-producing fixtures. Use the appropriate product, and never foam around a chimney flue.

## Step 2: Insulate where it matters

After sealing, insulation works.

1. **Check the attic first.** In cold climates, the attic is where the most heat is lost. If you can see the joists, you're under-insulated.
2. **Add attic insulation to the recommended R-value** for your climate zone, and keep it clear of soffit vents so airflow isn't blocked.
3. **Insulate the attic hatch or access door** with a rigid foam panel.
4. **Insulate the rim joist and basement walls** if they're uninsulated.
5. **Insulate exposed pipes** in the attic, garage, crawlspace, and any exterior wall cavity — foam pipe sleeves for straight runs, and heat cable on known problem runs.
6. **Insulate water heater tank and pipes** if the tank is in a cold space. A tank blanket pays for itself quickly on an older heater.

**Important:** attic insulation only prevents ice dams when combined with **ventilation**. Insulation keeps heat out of the attic; ventilation removes whatever heat gets in. Ice dams require both, plus air sealing to stop the leaks that let heat in. If ice dams recur, this three-part combination is the fix — not roof work.

## Step 3: Protect the plumbing — the highest-stakes step

A burst pipe is the most expensive common winter failure, and prevention is cheap.

1. **Identify the vulnerable pipes.** Exterior walls, unheated basements and crawlspaces, attics, garages, and any pipe running along an exterior sill.
2. **Insulate all exposed pipe** on those runs.
3. **Install heat cable on the runs most likely to freeze** — typically the ones nearest an exterior wall. Use a cable with a built-in thermostat and plug it into a GFCI outlet.
4. **Seal gaps where cold air reaches pipes.** A pipe in an exterior wall with an unsealed opening behind it freezes faster than one in a warm cavity. This is where air sealing and plumbing work overlap.
5. **Disconnect exterior hoses and shut off, then drain the interior valve** for exterior faucets. Do the interior shutoff — the faucet alone isn't enough.
6. **Know where your main shutoff is** and test that it turns. This is what limits damage if a pipe does burst.
7. **Insulate the water heater and any exposed lines** in cold spaces.
8. **For extended absences in cold weather,** shut off the water, drain the system, or leave the heat at a minimum of 55°F (13°C). Never turn the heat off completely.

For the full method including what to do if a pipe has already frozen, see [How to Prevent Frozen Pipes](/fix-it/maintenance/prevent-frozen-pipes/).

## Step 4: Deal with the roof and attic heat

This is the ice dam sequence, and it must be done in order.

1. **Air seal the attic floor** (Step 1). This stops warm air entering the attic.
2. **Add insulation** (Step 2) to reduce conductive transfer.
3. **Check ventilation:** soffit and ridge vents clear, no insulation blocking the soffit. Adequate intake and exhaust ventilation keeps the roof deck cold.
4. **Clear any existing ice dams carefully** — never chop at them with an ice pick, which damages shingles. Use a roof rake from the ground for snow, and treat ice with a calcium chloride product designed for roofs.
5. **Address the cause, not the symptom.** Repeated ice dams mean the attic is warm, and the fix is always sealing, insulation, and ventilation.
6. **Consider a roof rake** for heavy snow loads, especially on low-slope roofs and over unconditioned areas. Snow load is a structural concern on older roofs.

## Step 5: Windows and doors

Windows are the largest visible loss, and also the most overstated.

1. **Check and repair weatherstripping** on operable windows.
2. **Lock the windows.** A locked window compresses the seal; an unlocked one leaks measurably.
3. **Caulk around the frames**, inside and out.
4. **For single-pane windows you can't replace,** install insulation film. It works by creating a trapped air layer. It does nothing for the frame leaks, so air seal first.
5. **Use heavy curtains or thermal blinds**, and close them at night. Note that on sunny days, leaving south-facing windows uncovered gains more than the night loss costs.
6. **For doors,** add a draft stopper at the bottom if the sweep can't be adjusted. For sliding doors, check the seal between the panels.

Be skeptical of claims about how much window treatment saves. Air leakage and attic insulation are consistently bigger factors — see [How to Save on Heating Costs in Winter](/save-money/bills/save-on-heating-costs-winter/) for which changes actually pay.

## Step 6: Prepare the heating system

1. **Replace the furnace filter.** Do this at the start of the season and check monthly during peak use.
2. **Schedule a professional service** if it's been over a year.
3. **Test the system on a mild day** so you find problems before the first cold night.
4. **Vacuum vents and returns.**
5. **Check the flue and clear any obstruction.** Also clear snow from exterior vents after storms — a blocked high-efficiency furnace vent can cause the system to shut down or cause CO buildup indoors.
6. **Reverse ceiling fans to clockwise on low speed** to push warm air down.
7. **Set a programmable thermostat schedule.** Setback during sleeping and work hours is the largest single heating saving, and it costs nothing after setup.
8. **Check CO detectors** and replace batteries. CO risk rises with heating season.

## Step 7: Exterior and outdoor items

1. **Shut off and drain exterior faucets, disconnect hoses.**
2. **Drain the irrigation system** and blow out lines.
3. **Clean gutters** so meltwater and rain drain instead of freezing in place.
4. **Check that downspouts discharge away** from the foundation.
5. **Store or cover outdoor furniture**, and bring cushions indoors.
6. **Drain fuel from small engines** or add stabilizer; store the mower.
7. **Service the snow equipment** and store ice melt where it's accessible.
8. **Trim branches overhanging the house** — ice load brings them down.
9. **Protect tender plants** with mulch or wraps.

## Step 8: Prove it holds with a cold-weather test

Winterizing is a set of assumptions. Verify them on the first genuinely cold day.

1. **Walk the house and feel for drafts** at every exterior wall, window, and door.
2. **Check the pipes on the coldest exterior wall** — if they feel very cold, add heat cable.
3. **Measure room temperatures** with a thermometer. Large differences between rooms indicate air leaks or duct problems.
4. **Look in the attic after the first snow** for uneven melting patterns, which reveal where heat is escaping.
5. **Check the attic for frost** on the roof sheathing, which indicates moist air entering from the house.
6. **Note anything you need to fix** and do it before the coldest stretch. Adjustments are much easier now than in January.

## Why This Works

Air sealing comes first because of how heat actually leaves a house. Conductive loss through insulation is governed by the temperature difference and the R-value — it's predictable and unavoidable. Convective loss moves air itself, carrying heat directly from indoors to outdoors, and it bypasses insulation completely. A single unsealed gap can lose more heat than a substantial insulated area retains, which is why energy audits consistently rank air sealing first.

Ice dams are a direct consequence of attic temperature. In a properly built and sealed attic, the roof deck stays close to outdoor temperature, snow melts only from the sun, and meltwater drains off the roof. When warm air leaks into the attic, the deck warms above freezing, snow melts from underneath, and water runs down to the cold eave where it refreezes. The ice grows into a dam, and subsequent meltwater has nowhere to go but under the shingles. That's why the fix is air sealing plus insulation plus ventilation — and why roof work doesn't help.

Frozen pipes follow from the expansion coefficient of ice. Water is unusual in that its solid phase is less dense than its liquid phase, so freezing expands it about 9 percent. Inside a rigid pipe, that expansion generates force that splits copper and PVC. The key insight is that the burst happens at the freeze but the leak appears at the thaw — a split pipe stays sealed by the ice plug until it melts. That's also why insidious damage goes unnoticed for weeks.

Insulating pipes works by slowing heat transfer from the water to the surrounding cold air, which buys time. It does not generate heat. Heat cable is different — it adds energy and is the correct solution for runs that will be exposed to sustained sub-freezing air. Combining both (insulation over heat cable) is the strongest protection.

Window film works on a simple principle: a trapped air layer. Still air is a poor conductor, so an air gap between the glass and the room reduces conductive loss. It does nothing for the frame and sash leaks, which are convective. That's why the sequence is seal-then-film, and why film alone produces disappointing results in a leaky house.

Testing after the first cold day matters because winterizing failures are invisible until they're stressed. A gap that doesn't leak at 40°F may leak substantially at 10°F, because the pressure differential driving infiltration scales with the temperature difference. The first genuinely cold day is the diagnostic opportunity.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Insulating before air sealing | Convective loss bypasses the insulation | Seal first, then insulate |
| Chopping ice dams with a pick or chisel | Destroys shingles and creates a leak | Roof rake for snow; roof-safe ice melt for ice |
| Fixing ice dams by redoing the roof | The cause is attic heat, not the roof | Air seal, insulate, and ventilate the attic |
| Leaving hoses connected | Traps water in the bib and splits the pipe inside the wall | Disconnect, shut off the interior valve, drain |
| Turning the heat off when away in winter | Pipes freeze | Keep at 55°F minimum, or drain the system |
| Counting on window film alone | Doesn't address frame and sash leaks | Air seal first |
| Foaming around a chimney flue or heat fixture | Fire risk; wrong material | Use the correct fire-rated product, keep clearance |
| Blocking soffit vents with insulation | Kills attic ventilation and makes ice dams worse | Maintain a clear air channel at the soffits |
| Ignoring exterior furnace and dryer vents after snow | Blocked vents cause shutdown and CO risk | Clear vents after every significant snowfall |
| Skipping the CO detector check | CO risk rises with heating season | Test and replace batteries |
| Assuming a new furnace needs no filter attention | Filters load fastest in peak season | Check monthly |
| Trusting a thermostat schedule you never reviewed | Default setbacks often don't match your routine | Set it to your actual schedule |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the first thing to do when winterizing a home?**
Air seal. Seal window and door frames, utility penetrations, the attic hatch, and the rim joist. Air leakage bypasses insulation and is consistently the largest heat loss in most homes, so sealing first makes everything else more effective.

**How do I stop ice dams on my roof?**
Air seal the attic floor, add insulation, and ensure soffit and ridge vents are clear and unblocked. Ice dams form because heat escapes into the attic and melts snow from underneath. Roof work doesn't fix it.

**What temperature should I leave the heat at when away?**
At least 55°F (13°C). Below that, pipes in exterior walls are at risk. For extended absences, shut off the water and drain the system, or have someone check the house.

**Do window insulation kits actually work?**
Yes, for what they do — they create a trapped air layer that reduces conductive loss through the glass. They don't fix frame and sash air leaks, so seal those first or the film's benefit is small.

**How do I prevent pipes from freezing?**
Insulate all exposed pipe on vulnerable runs, add heat cable to the worst ones, seal the gaps that let cold air reach them, disconnect and drain exterior faucets, and keep the thermostat at 55°F or above.

**Is it worth insulating the attic if it's already partly insulated?**
Yes, if you can see the joists. Most homes in cold climates are under-insulated, and attic insulation is among the highest-return improvements — but air seal first or much of the benefit is lost.

**What should I do about my water heater for winter?**
Insulate the tank and any exposed hot water lines if the heater is in a cold space, and consider a tank blanket. It pays back quickly on older heaters and reduces standby loss.

**When is the best time to winterize?**
Before the first freeze, while you can still work outside comfortably and products like caulk will cure. Doing it during the first cold snap means doing it under pressure, often at higher cost.
