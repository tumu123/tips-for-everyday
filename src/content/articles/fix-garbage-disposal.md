**Read this before anything else: never put your hand or fingers into a garbage disposal.** Not briefly, not "just to check," not even when it's switched off. Use tongs or pliers, and disconnect the power before reaching in. The impeller can move unexpectedly, and disposal injuries are severe.

With that established: most "broken" disposals aren't broken. Two conditions account for nearly all failures, and both take minutes to resolve.

---

## Step 0: Identify the Symptom

| Symptom | Most likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Completely silent, no sound at all** | No power, or the overload protector has tripped | Check the breaker and the reset button |
| **Hums or buzzes but doesn't grind** | Impeller is jammed | Free the jam with the hex wrench |
| **Runs but drains slowly** | Clog downstream in the drain line | Clear the trap or the drain |
| **Runs, then stops on its own** | Overheating from overload or a jam | Let it cool, then reset and clear the jam |
| **Leaks from underneath** | Failed seal, loose connection, or a cracked housing | Repair or replace |
| **Smells bad but works fine** | Buildup on the impeller and chamber | Clean it |

**The hum is diagnostic.** A humming disposal has power and a motor trying to turn — it's mechanically blocked. That's a jam, and it's the most common single failure.

When water appears under the sink only after the disposal runs, the leak is in the unit itself — [fixing a leaking garbage disposal](/fix-it/plumbing/fix-garbage-disposal-leaking/) narrows down which joint it is.

---

## Step 1: Check Power and the Reset Button

Do this first; it's the cheapest possible fix.

**Check the circuit breaker.** A disposal has its own breaker or shares one. If it has tripped, reset it once. If it trips again immediately, there's an electrical problem — stop and call an electrician.

**Then find the reset button.** Nearly every disposal has a small red or black button on the **underside of the unit**, near the center. It's an overload protector that pops out when the motor overheats.

1. **Make sure the switch is off**
2. **Lie a cloth or mat down** — you'll be working under the sink
3. **Press the reset button** firmly until it clicks and stays in
4. **Turn the switch on** and test with cold water running

**If the button won't stay in,** the motor is still too hot. Wait 10–15 minutes and try again. Repeated immediate tripping means a persistent jam or a failing motor.

**Note:** if the disposal recently ran for a long time or ground something hard, the overload trip is doing its job. That's protection working, not failure.

---

## Step 2: Free a Jammed Impeller

If it hums, the impeller plate can't rotate. Freeing it is straightforward.

**The right tool:** a hex wrench — usually the one that came with the unit, typically 1/4 inch. Many people store it by taping it to the side of the disposal or under the sink. If yours is lost, a standard 1/4 inch hex wrench works on most models.

**The procedure:**

1. **Turn off the switch** and, better, the breaker
2. **Look at the very bottom center of the disposal** — there's a hexagonal hole
3. **Insert the wrench** fully into the hole
4. **Work it back and forth** — clockwise and counter-clockwise — with firm pressure. You're trying to break free whatever is wedged
5. **You'll feel it release** and start rotating freely through a full circle
6. **Remove the wrench**
7. **Restore power,** press reset if needed, and run cold water while switching on

**If you don't have a hex wrench,** a broom handle or a wooden spoon pushed against the impeller from the top can sometimes free it. Angle it against the impeller plate and lever gently. This works but the wrench is better.

---

## Step 3: Remove the Jam With Tongs (Power Off)

If the wrench doesn't clear it, something is wedged in the chamber and needs to come out.

**Safety first:**
- **Turn off the breaker**, not just the wall switch. Verify with a non-contact voltage tester
- **Never use your hand**

**The procedure:**

1. **Shine a flashlight** into the chamber through the sink opening
2. **Look for the object** — common culprits are fruit pits, shell fragments, bones, bottle caps, utensils, and fibrous vegetable material
3. **Use long-handled tongs or needle-nose pliers** to grasp and remove it
4. **Work deliberately.** If it's wedged tightly, the hex wrench from Step 2 may free it enough to grab
5. **Once removed,** use the wrench again to confirm full rotation
6. **Restore power** and test with cold water

**If you can't see or reach the obstruction,** don't keep digging. That's the point to call a plumber — they can remove the unit and clear it properly.

## Step 4: Clear a Downstream Clog

If the disposal runs but the sink drains slowly, the blockage is past the disposal.

This is a drain problem, not a disposal problem, and removing the trap is usually the fix. The procedure is the same as any sink — see [how to unclog a slow drain](/fix-it/plumbing/unclog-slow-drain/) for the full steps.

**One caution:** never use chemical drain cleaner in a disposal. It can damage the unit's seals and it creates a hazard when the disposal runs.

The drain cycle is time-limited rather than level-sensed, so a partial clog leaves water behind every time — [a dishwasher that will not drain](/fix-it/appliances/dishwasher-leaves-water-in-bottom/) covers where that clog sits.

## What Can and Cannot Go In

Prevention is worth more than any repair here, and the prohibited list is where most damage originates.

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| **Fibrous vegetables** (celery, onion skins, corn husks, artichoke) | Fibres wrap around the impeller and jam it |
| **Coffee grounds** | Accumulate into a dense sludge in the drain |
| **Grease, fat, oil** | Solidifies in the chamber and the drain |
| **Bones** | Damage the impeller and can jam the plate |
| **Fruit pits** | Jam the impeller directly |
| **Shellfish shells** | Very hard; damage the grinding components |
| **Pasta, rice, potatoes** | Expand with water and form a paste |
| **Eggshells** (disputed) | The membrane wraps the impeller; the shells add grit to the drain |
| **Non-food items** | Utensils, packaging, twist ties — obvious but common |
| **Expandable foods** | Anything that swells with moisture |

**Safe in moderation:** small soft food scraps, citrus rinds in small amounts, ice cubes (useful for cleaning).

**The rule of thumb:** the disposal is for the small residue left on plates, not for meal preparation waste. Most of what people put in it belongs in the bin or the compost.

## Cleaning and Deodorizing

A smelly disposal usually has buildup on the impeller plate and the chamber wall. Cleaning solves what air fresheners only mask.

**Method A — ice and coarse salt:**
1. **Fill the chamber with ice cubes** and a handful of coarse salt
2. **Run cold water** and switch on
3. **The ice and salt scour** the impeller and chamber surfaces
4. **Run for 30 seconds**

**Method B — baking soda and vinegar:**
1. **Pour baking soda** into the chamber
2. **Add a splash of vinegar** and let it foam for a few minutes — the foam reaches areas you can't
3. **Flush with hot water** (with the disposal off for the final flush, or running for better contact)

Use these as separate steps rather than simultaneously for maximum effect — the fizzing is mechanically useful, but combining them neutralises much of the cleaning action.

**Method C — citrus:** grind small pieces of lemon or lime rind with cold water. It's genuinely effective at deodorizing, and much better than any scented product.

**Clean the splash guard** too — the rubber flaps underneath collect residue and are a common odour source that people miss. Scrub them with a brush and dish soap.

## When to Replace

| Situation | Replace? |
|---|---|
| Reset trips repeatedly with no jam | Likely — the motor is failing |
| Grinding or screeching from the motor | Usually yes |
| Leaking from the bottom of the housing | Yes — internal seals have failed |
| Unit is over 10–12 years old and failing | Replace rather than repair |
| Motor runs but nothing grinds | The impeller or drive has failed — usually replace |
| Cracked housing | Replace immediately |

**The economic calculation:** a service call plus repair often costs a significant fraction of a new mid-range unit. If the unit is more than about ten years old, replacement is usually the better value — and modern units are quieter.

## Why This Works

**The overload protector exists precisely for this scenario.** The motor draws high current when stalled, which generates heat. The thermal cutout interrupts power before the windings are damaged. Resetting it after the motor cools restores operation — which is why a tripped disposal is usually fine rather than broken.

**A jam is a mechanical block, and the hex wrench converts your leverage directly to the impeller shaft.** The bottom-center hex socket is there specifically to let you turn the shaft by hand. Rocking it back and forth breaks the wedge because the obstruction is usually pinched rather than bonded.

**Fibrous material jams by wrapping, not by wedging.** Celery, corn husks, and onion skins wrap around the rotating plate and bind it against the housing. Cutting the power and physically removing the wrap is the only reliable fix — which is why prevention matters so much on this appliance.

**Odour comes from a surface film, so scouring beats masking.** Residue coats the chamber and the impeller, and the film holds odour. Abrasive cleaning with ice and salt removes it; fragrance only overlays it.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Putting your hand in the chamber | Severe injury | Tongs or pliers, with power off |
| Only switching off the wall switch | Unit can still be energised | Turn off the breaker and verify |
| Running it while jammed | Overheats the motor, trips the overload | Free the jam first |
| Pressing reset repeatedly without clearing a jam | Trips again immediately | Clear the obstruction first |
| Using chemical drain cleaner in a disposal | Damages seals; hazardous when it runs | Mechanical clearing only |
| Putting fibrous vegetables in | Wraps the impeller | Compost or bin them |
| Pouring grease in | Solidifies and blocks | Collect and bin it |
| Grinding hard objects | Damages the impeller | Bin them |
| Running it without water | Overheats and jams | Always run cold water |

## Frequently Asked Questions
**Why does my disposal hum but not grind?**
The impeller is jammed. Turn off power, then use a hex wrench in the hole on the bottom to work the shaft back and forth until it frees.

**Where is the reset button?**
On the underside of the unit, near the center — a small red or black button. Press it firmly until it clicks in, after the motor has cooled.

**Can I put my hand in to remove something?**
No. Never. Use tongs or pliers, and disconnect power at the breaker first.

**Why does my disposal smell?**
Residue film on the impeller and chamber. Grind ice with coarse salt, then citrus rind, and scrub the rubber splash guard.

**Are coffee grounds okay?**
Not really. They accumulate into a dense sludge that clogs drains over time. Compost them instead.

**My disposal leaks from the bottom. Is it fixable?**
Usually not worth repairing — that indicates failed internal seals. Replacement is typically the answer.

**How long should a disposal last?**
Typically 8–12 years with reasonable use. Heavy use and hard objects shorten that substantially.

**Should I run hot or cold water?**
Cold. Cold water keeps grease solid so it can be ground and flushed, whereas hot water melts it so it re-solidifies downstream.
