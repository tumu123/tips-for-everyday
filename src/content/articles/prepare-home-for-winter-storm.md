A winter storm is not one hazard. It is three, arriving at different times, and the prep for each is different.

**Weight** comes first — snow accumulating on the roof, then rain falling on that snow, then a refreeze that turns it into a load the roof was never designed to carry. **Water** comes second, as ice dams push meltwater under the shingles and into the ceiling. **Cold** comes last and stays longest, in the form of a house that cannot hold heat and pipes that freeze when the furnace stops.

The household that prepares for "a storm" generally prepares for the wind and the roads. The damage, almost every time, comes from the other three.

## Step 1: Understand what your roof is actually rated for

Roof snow load design values in the United States run from about 20 pounds per square foot in mild regions to 50 or more in mountain areas. To translate that into snow:

- 1 inch of water equivalent weighs **5.2 pounds per square foot**.
- Fresh dry snow is roughly 15 to 20 inches per inch of water; wet snow is closer to 10 to 1.
- So 20 inches of wet snow is about 2 inches of water, or roughly **10 psf** — a fraction of the design load.

That is why most roofs do not collapse from a single storm. The failures come from accumulation: two feet of old snow that has settled and densified, plus a foot of wet snow on top, plus rain soaking through, plus drifts against a valley or a dormer that concentrate load three or four times higher than the flat-field average.

The practical rule: **start removing snow at 18 inches of depth, or at 6 inches of wet snow**, and never let it build past the point where you cannot safely reach it.

## Step 2: Get the roof edge right before the first snow

Ice dams form at the eave, and the eave is where you can still do something in October.

- **Clear the gutters completely.** A gutter full of leaves freezes solid, and the ice backs up under the first course of shingles. This is the single highest-value fall task for winter damage.
- **Check the flashing at the roof-wall junction** and around the chimney. A gap that leaks in October becomes an ice-filled gap in January.
- **Trim branches within 6 feet of the roof.** Ice-loaded branches are the most common cause of storm damage that is not snow load at all.
- **Note where the roof valleys are.** Those are the drift zones, and they are where you will need to rake first.

Do the ladder work now, in dry weather. Ladder use in freezing conditions is how most winter storm injuries happen, and a fall from 10 feet in January is far worse than the same fall in September.

## Step 3: Stop ice dams at the source, not with a heat cable

An ice dam is a symptom. The cause is warm air leaking from the house into the attic, heating the roof deck above 32�F, melting the snow, and sending water down to the eave where it refreezes against the cold overhang.

The fix, in order of effectiveness:

1. **Air-seal the ceiling plane.** Every recessed light, plumbing penetration, attic hatch, and top plate. This is the step that actually works, and it is the one people skip.
2. **Insulate to the current code level** for your zone — typically R-49 to R-60 in cold climates. Insulation without air sealing does less than half the job.
3. **Ventilate the attic properly.** Soffit intake and ridge exhaust, both unobstructed. Blocked soffit vents are common after a re-insulation and they turn a cold roof warm.
4. **Rake after every storm, as a backstop.** A roof rake with a 20-foot handle, used from the ground, removes the fuel before the dam forms.

Heat cable is the last resort. It works on a narrow band of roof and does nothing for the rest, it costs real money to run, and it fails silently when a section dies. Treat it as a patch for a specific trouble spot, not a solution.

## Step 4: Protect the pipes that will freeze first

Pipes burst not at the ice, but between the ice plug and a closed faucet — pressure builds as the ice expands and the water has nowhere to go. That means the damage appears far from the freeze.

Rank your risk by location, not by guesswork:

- **Exterior walls**, especially kitchen and bathroom sinks on a north or west wall.
- **Unheated crawlspaces**, garages, and attics with plumbing.
- **Hose bibs and any pipe within 8 inches of an exterior wall.**
- **Any pipe in a cabinet against an exterior wall**, where the cabinet door insulates the cold in.

Before the first hard freeze: disconnect hoses, shut off and drain outdoor spigots, insulate exposed pipe in the crawlspace, and seal the rim joist. In the storm itself, open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, let a pencil-thin stream run from the farthest faucet, and keep the thermostat at 55�F minimum even when away.

Know where the main shutoff is, and test that it turns. A pipe that bursts at 2 a.m. with a shutoff that has not moved in nine years is a flood, not a repair.

## Step 5: Plan for heat loss, not just power loss

A winter storm takes the power out in roughly one in three events, and the house then has to survive on its own heat. Two numbers decide whether that is uncomfortable or destructive:

- **Outdoor temperature.** At 20�F, an unheated house with decent insulation stays above freezing for 24 to 48 hours. At 0�F, pipes in exterior walls can freeze within 6 to 8 hours.
- **Your alternative heat source.** A wood stove, a propane heater rated for indoor use, or a generator that can run the furnace blower. Not the oven, not the gas range, not a charcoal grill.

The furnace blower is the detail people miss. A generator that runs the fridge but not the furnace leaves you with cold food and a freezing house. A licensed transfer switch, sized for the blower and a few circuits, is worth more than a bigger generator.

Install a **battery-powered carbon monoxide alarm** on every level before the season. Generator and space heater use during storms is the reason CO poisoning cases spike, and the alarm is the only thing that catches it while people are still awake enough to act.

## Step 6: Stage the kit by failure, not by checklist

Shopping lists are useless without a model of what fails. Build four groups instead:

**Warmth.** Blankets, sleeping bags rated for the temperature, hats, and a way to heat one room rather than the house. Pick the room now — south-facing, plumbing on interior walls, closable with a door.

**Water.** One gallon per person per day for three days, plus one for pets. Two 5-gallon containers fill a bathtub in advance if a warning is issued.

**Light and information.** Headlamps beat candles by a wide margin — open flame plus a storm plus a dark house is a fire. A battery or hand-crank radio, and a battery bank charged to full before the storm.

**Medicine and documents.** Seven days of prescriptions, a copy of insurance and ID in a waterproof bag, and cash in small bills. Card readers fail when the power and the network both do.

## Step 7: Clear the right things during the storm

Shoveling the driveway is the visible task. Three less visible ones matter more:

- **Clear the furnace and dryer vents outside.** A buried direct-vent furnace exhaust pushes carbon monoxide back into the house. This kills people every winter, and it takes two minutes to prevent.
- **Keep one exterior door and a path to the street clear.** If the door freezes shut or snow blocks it, you cannot leave and emergency services cannot enter.
- **Clear around the gas meter and any hydrant**, gently, with a broom rather than a shovel. A meter that ices over can stop registering, and a hit meter is a leak.

Rake the roof after each storm rather than waiting for accumulation. It is easier, safer, and it prevents the dam instead of dealing with it.

## Step 8: Handle the car and the road before you need them

- **Full fuel tank** before the storm. A half tank can freeze at the fuel line in extreme cold, and stations lose power too.
- **Kit in the car**: blanket, boots, gloves, a bag of sand or kitty litter, a small shovel, a phone charger, and a candle-free light.
- **Never drive into standing water or over an unplowed drift.** Both hide depth, and both are how cars get stranded within a mile of home.
- **Tell someone the route** if you must travel, and check in on arrival.

## Step 9: Use the warning window correctly

When a winter storm warning is issued 24 to 36 hours out, the order that matters:

1. Fill prescriptions and fuel the car.
2. Charge every battery bank and device.
3. Set the thermostat 2�F higher than normal — the house is a thermal battery.
4. Fill water containers and the bathtub if you are on a well.
5. Move anything that can blow or freeze into the garage.
6. Confirm the CO alarm works and the flashlights are where you think they are.

Do not spend the window shopping. Most of what you need, you already own; the stores are the worst place to be in the last 12 hours.

## Step 10: Inspect in the right order afterward

After the storm clears, check in this sequence — it follows the way damage actually propagates:

1. **Vents and flues** — clear them before running the furnace or water heater.
2. **Ceilings and attic** — look for new stains, which mean an ice dam has already leaked.
3. **Exterior walls and foundation** — check for drifting snow against the house and any new crack.
4. **Roof from the ground** — missing shingles, sagging, or ice build-up at the eave.
5. **Pipes** — open the cabinets, look for bulges, and check the meter for movement with everything off.

Photograph anything you find before you fix it. Storm claims go much more smoothly with dated photos taken the same day.

## Why This Works

The prep is organized around the three mechanisms that actually damage houses in winter — load, water, and heat loss — rather than around the weather forecast. Each step interrupts a specific chain: air sealing stops the melt that makes the dam, raking removes the load before it compounds, and the shutoff plus a 55�F thermostat bounds the damage a freeze can do.

The sequencing matters as much as the content. Everything that requires a ladder or dry conditions is done in fall; everything that requires judgment happens in the warning window; everything that requires inspection happens after the vents are clear. That order is what keeps a storm from turning into a repair.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much snow can a roof hold?**

Most residential roofs are designed for 20 to 50 pounds per square foot, and 20 inches of wet snow is only about 10 psf. The risk is accumulation and drift, not a single snowfall. Start raking at 18 inches of settled snow, or 6 inches of wet snow, and pay attention to valleys and dormers where drifts concentrate.

**What is the fastest way to prevent ice dams?**

Stop warm air from reaching the roof deck: air-seal every ceiling penetration, insulate to code, and keep soffit and ridge vents open. As a backstop, rake the roof after each storm so there is no snow to melt and refreeze. Heat cables treat the symptom on a narrow strip.

**At what temperature do pipes freeze in a house?**

Pipes in exterior walls can begin freezing when outdoor temperatures stay below 20�F for several hours, and within 6 to 8 hours at 0�F. An unheated house with reasonable insulation holds above freezing for 24 to 48 hours at 20�F. Keep the thermostat at 55�F minimum and open cabinet doors on exterior walls.

**Should I turn off the water if I lose heat?**

If you are leaving or the house will drop below freezing, yes — shut off the main and drain the lines by opening the lowest faucet and every fixture. If you are staying and have any heat source, keep a pencil-thin stream running at the farthest faucet instead, which relieves pressure if ice forms.

**How do I keep a room warm without power?**

Pick one interior room with no plumbing on exterior walls, close it off, and heat it with a properly rated indoor propane or kerosene heater with a CO alarm present. Hang blankets over doorways and windows. Never use a gas oven, a charcoal grill, or an unvented generator indoors.

**Is it safe to use a generator during a winter storm?**

Only outdoors, at least 20 feet from doors, windows, and vents, with the exhaust pointed away. Never in a garage, even with the door open. Use a transfer switch or heavy cords rated for the load, never backfeed through an outlet. Install battery-powered CO alarms on every level.

**How long does food last in a power outage during winter?**

A closed refrigerator holds temperature for about 4 hours; a full freezer for 48 hours, a half-full one for 24. In winter you can use the outdoors as a freezer for a short period, but only for sealed items and only while the temperature stays below 40�F — and never in sunlight or where animals can reach it.

**What should be cleared first after a snowstorm?**

Vents and flues — furnace, water heater, dryer — then one door and a path out, then the gas meter. Driveway and walkways come after. A buried furnace vent forces exhaust back into the house, which is the fastest way for a snowstorm to become a poisoning.
