**Almost every article about DIY versus hiring decides based on difficulty, and difficulty is the wrong criterion.** Difficulty tells you whether you *can* do something. It doesn't tell you whether you *should*, because the jobs that ruin houses are not the hard ones — they're the easy ones done slightly wrong.

Replacing a toilet seat is trivially easy and cannot go badly. Replacing a toilet's wax ring is also easy, and done slightly wrong it leaks into the floor for six months. Both are "easy." Only one is safe to attempt without experience.

The right question is: **what does the failure cost?** That single reframe sorts almost every household repair into a decision you can make in thirty seconds.

---

## The Rule

Classify each job by the cost of getting it wrong, not by the skill involved.

| Tier | Cost of a mistake | Examples | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green** | Aesthetic, or fixed by redoing it | Painting, caulking, replacing a seat, unclogging a drain | Try it. Worst case is time |
| **Yellow** | Water damage, cosmetic rework, a mid-size bill | Faucet cartridges, toilet internals, drywall patching, appliance parts | Do it if you can describe the fix before starting |
| **Red** | Structural, fire, gas, flood, injury, or a permit violation | Gas lines, panel work, spring and cable work, structural changes | Hire. Always |

**Green means the mistake is visible and recoverable.** If the paint is uneven, you paint again. If the caulk is ugly, you peel it off. The material is cheap and the failure is self-announcing.

**Yellow means the mistake is invisible and expensive.** A leaking wax ring looks fine. A badly seated supply line looks fine. The failure shows up weeks later as a soft floor or a swollen cabinet. Yellow jobs are the ones where a two-minute YouTube clip is genuinely not enough — you need to understand the failure mode, not just the sequence of steps.

**Red means the mistake is dangerous or illegal.** Not "hard." Dangerous. Gas work, electrical panel work, anything involving stored mechanical energy, and structural changes all belong here regardless of how simple the task looks.

---

## The Five Things That Make a Job Red

Everything in the red tier shares one of these properties. If a job has one, the answer is no.

**1. Pressurised gas.** Natural gas and propane work requires a licence in most jurisdictions, and for good reason: the failure mode is an explosion, and the leak is odourless until a mercaptan threshold is exceeded. There is no version of this that is safe to learn.

**2. The electrical panel or a new circuit.** Replacing a switch or an outlet is green. Opening the panel, adding a breaker, or running a new circuit is red. The panel is the one place in a house where a mistake can kill the next person to touch it — including an electrician who later assumes it was done correctly.

**3. Stored mechanical energy.** Garage door torsion springs, extension springs, and cables. A wound spring holds enough energy to break bone and has killed people. [Garage door diagnosis](/fix-it/appliances/garage-door-wont-open/) is a fine DIY task; the spring is not.

**4. Structure.** Anything that carries load: removing a wall, cutting a joist, enlarging a window opening, adding a beam. The failure mode is slow and invisible until it isn't.

**5. Water inside a wall.** Not water in a fixture — water in an assembly. Cutting into a supply line, soldering copper, moving a drain. The failure is a flood that runs for hours while nobody is home, and it's the most expensive common household accident there is.

## The "Can You Describe the Fix" Test

For yellow jobs, there's a quick check that predicts success better than confidence does.

**Before starting, describe out loud what you're going to do, in order, including what could go wrong and what you'll do if it does.**

If you can do that, the job is probably within reach. If you find yourself saying "and then I'll figure it out," stop — that's the point where a $40 handyman visit becomes a $400 repair.

Applied to a real example: replacing a toilet's internals.

- **Green-tier answer:** "I'll shut the water off, flush to empty the tank, unscrew the old valve, put the new one in, adjust the fill height, and turn the water back on." That's fine — the failure is a running toilet, which is visible and cheap.
- **Yellow-tier example, same appliance:** "I'll pull the toilet, scrape the old wax ring, set a new one, and reset the bowl." Missing from that description: the flange height, the bolt alignment, and the fact that a wax ring compressed twice has to be replaced. That's a job where the failure is a slow leak into the floor — invisible for months.

**The test works because it forces you to confront the failure modes, not just the steps.** Videos show steps. They rarely show what happens when a fitting is cross-threaded.

## Permits and Insurance

Two practical constraints that decide jobs independently of skill.

**Permits.** Most jurisdictions require permits for structural changes, new electrical circuits, gas work, water heater replacement, and deck construction. The permit exists so the work gets inspected — and it matters at resale, because unpermitted work can hold up a sale or void insurance.

**Insurance.** A homeowner policy typically covers accidental damage from a sudden event, and typically does not cover damage from work you did incorrectly or over time. If a DIY supply line lets go and floods the basement, the adjuster will ask who did the work. The answer changes the outcome.

**The practical rule:** if the job requires a permit, the permit requires an inspection, and the inspection requires someone licensed to sign off. That sequence answers the question for you.

This rule is about consequences rather than difficulty, which is the same test [pricing out and doing it yourself](/save-money/big-decisions/cost-to-hire-out-vs-diy/) applies to the money — saved labour divided by hours spent.

## How to Pick Someone When You Do Hire

Since the red tier is where the money goes, it's worth knowing how to spend it.

**Get three quotes for anything over about $1,000.** The spread is often 40%, and the cheapest is frequently cheap for a reason.

**Ask for the licence number and verify it.** Every jurisdiction publishes a lookup. This takes two minutes and eliminates the most common form of fraud.

**Check insurance, specifically.** General liability and workers' compensation. If a worker is injured on your property and the contractor has no workers' comp, the claim can reach your homeowner policy.

**Put the scope in writing**, including what's excluded. Most disputes are about exclusions that were never discussed.

**Never pay more than a third up front**, and never pay in full before the work is inspected and complete. Deposit-heavy requests are the signature of the contractors who disappear.

**For trades specifically:** an electrician, a plumber, and an HVAC technician are separate licences in most places. A "handyman" who offers all three is worth checking carefully.

## Why This Works

**The cost of the mistake, not the difficulty, is what varies by orders of magnitude.** Replacing a toilet seat wrong costs five minutes. Replacing a wax ring wrong costs a subfloor. The tasks look equally simple; the downside differs by a factor of a thousand. Sorting by downside is the only classification that reflects actual risk.

**Invisible failures define the yellow tier.** Green failures announce themselves — the paint is uneven, the drain is still slow. Yellow failures don't: a mis-seated supply line, a compressed wax ring, caulk over wet substrate. All look correct at the moment of completion and fail weeks later. That delay is what makes experience worth paying for.

**Red jobs are red because the failure is not recoverable.** A gas leak, an overloaded circuit, a released spring — there's no partial failure mode and no second attempt. Every other tier has a mistake you can see and redo.

**Permits encode the same judgment.** They exist for the categories where inspection matters, which is a useful external signal: if a jurisdiction decided this category needs checking, that's a strong hint it's not a learning opportunity.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Judging difficulty by the video | Videos skip the failure modes | Judge by the cost of getting it wrong |
| Opening the electrical panel to "just add a breaker" | Fire, electrocution, uninsurable work | Hire a licensed electrician |
| Adjusting a garage door spring | Severe injury or death | Hire a door technician |
| Working on gas because "it's just a connection" | Explosion, carbon monoxide | Licensed only, no exceptions |
| Skipping a permit to save time | Resale problems, insurance denial | Check the requirement first |
| Starting a yellow job without a plan B | A small leak becomes a large repair | Describe the failure modes first |
| Choosing the lowest quote | Often the least insured, least licensed | Verify licence and insurance |
| Paying in full up front | No leverage if the work is incomplete | Third up front, rest on completion |
| Treating a repeating breaker trip as a nuisance | The breaker is reporting a real fault | Investigate the circuit |
| Assuming homeowners insurance covers bad DIY | It usually doesn't | Know the exclusion before you start |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What home repairs should I never DIY?**
Gas work, electrical panel work, garage door springs and cables, anything structural, and soldering supply lines inside a wall. Each has a failure mode that is dangerous or catastrophically expensive.

**Is it cheaper to DIY or hire?**
DIY is cheaper on materials but not always on outcome. A yellow job done wrong costs more than the professional would have. Green jobs are almost always worth doing yourself.

**Do I need a permit to replace a water heater?**
In most jurisdictions, yes — and it usually requires a licensed installer. Check before buying the unit.

**How much should a handyman charge?**
Rates vary widely by region, but the useful comparison is against the cost of the damage a bad job causes, not against other handymen.

**Can I do my own electrical work?**
Replacing a switch, outlet, or light fixture on an existing circuit is usually permitted for homeowners in many jurisdictions. Anything involving the panel or a new circuit is not.

**What if I start a job and get stuck?**
Stop before damaging anything, and document what you've done. A professional called at that point costs far less than one called after a flood.

**Is it worth fixing an appliance yourself?**
For parts under about $100 and machines under 8 years old, usually yes. The information is available and the parts are accessible. Anything involving 240 V or gas needs the power or gas off first.

**How do I know if a contractor is legitimate?**
Licence number you can verify, liability and workers' comp insurance, a written scope, and a payment schedule that doesn't front-load.

## Bottom Line

Sort by the cost of the mistake, not the difficulty. Green jobs have visible, cheap failures — do them. Yellow jobs have invisible failures — do them only if you can describe what could go wrong. Red jobs have unrecoverable failures — hire, every time, regardless of how simple the task looks.

That rule resolves almost every case in about thirty seconds, and it's more reliable than confidence, which is the least trustworthy input in this whole decision.
