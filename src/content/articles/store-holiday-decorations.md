Decorations are used for four weeks and stored for forty-eight. Almost all the damage happens in those forty-eight weeks — tangles, broken bulbs, crushed ornaments, yellowed trees, and mice nests.

The storage problems are predictable, and so are the fixes. They come down to **three rules**: keep things off the floor, keep them out of extreme temperature swings, and keep like with like.

---

## Step 1: Unpack and triage before you pack

Packing happens in January when you're tired. Do a fast triage first so you're not storing broken things.

1. **Test every strand of lights** and discard the dead ones. Storing dead strands means rediscovering them next year.
2. **Inspect ornaments and discard broken ones.** Glass shards hidden in a bin cut fingers next December.
3. **Check for anything chewed, moldy, or damp.** Never store these — they contaminate everything else.
4. **Separate by type**, not by room: lights, ornaments, tree components, wreaths and garlands, linens, outdoor items, and small accessories.
5. **Note what you need to replace** so you can buy it in the off-season or in early fall, when selection is better.

## Step 2: Store lights on spools, never in bags

Tangled lights are the single biggest time cost of decorating, and the cause is always loose storage.

1. **Wind each strand onto a cord spool, cardboard cutout, or a piece of rigid cardboard notched at both ends.**
2. **Or wrap each strand around a clothes hanger**, then hang the hangers together in a closet.
3. **Or use a zip bag per strand**, with the plug and socket ends taped so they don't tangle with the rest.
4. **Or coil loosely into a circle** and secure with a twist tie, taping the two ends together — this is the fastest method and works well enough.
5. **Label each strand** with its length and whether it's indoor or outdoor rated.
6. **Store in a dry, indoor location.** Outdoor-rated lights still shouldn't sit in a damp shed; the sockets corrode.

**What not to do:** stuffing strands loose into a bag. It takes four seconds to pack and forty minutes to untangle.

Four ignition sources in one room compound — [holiday safety checks for lights, trees, candles and heaters](/seasonal/holidays/winter-holiday-safety-home-checks/) covers each.

## Step 3: Pack ornaments by fragility, not by color

Ornaments break in storage for exactly one reason: they touch each other or the container.

1. **Wrap each fragile ornament individually** in acid-free tissue. Acidic paper discolors and can react with painted or metallic finishes over months.
2. **Use divided trays or the original boxes** for glass and heirloom ornaments. This is the highest-value investment of the whole process.
3. **Pack the tree top, largest, and heaviest ornaments at the bottom** of the container, small and light on top.
4. **Leave no empty space** — ornaments that can shift will shift. Fill gaps with shredded paper.
5. **Shatterproof ornaments** can be packed together loosely, but keep metal hooks separate; they scratch finishes.
6. **Store hooks in a separate zip bag**, not attached to ornaments. Attached hooks stress the cap and tangle.
7. **Label each container** with a contents list, and mark any that are fragile or "do not stack."

## Step 4: Store the artificial tree without killing it

An artificial tree fails in storage through crushing, damp, and dust.

1. **Use the original box** if you have it — it's designed for the shape.
2. **Otherwise use a tree storage bag** with a rigid bottom, sized to the tree.
3. **Disassemble section by section**, and for hinged or pre-lit trees, keep sections intact; do not compress the branches.
4. **Bag each section separately** so branches don't hook into each other.
5. **Never store a tree in a damp basement, garage, or attic.** Moisture causes the metal frame to rust and the "needles" to degrade and yellow.
6. **Keep it away from extreme heat** — an attic that reaches 130°F in summer will embrittle plastic needles and can discolor lights.
7. **Wrap the tree base and stand separately**, and keep the hardware in a labeled bag taped to the bag.
8. **For pre-lit trees,** check for cut or pinched cords before storage and coil the light leads so they don't get crushed between sections.

## Step 5: Handle wreaths, garlands, and fabric items

1. **Wreaths:** store in a dedicated wreath box or a sturdy bag hanging on a hook. Wreaths stored flat or under other boxes arrive next year deformed.
2. **Garlands:** coil loosely and store on a spool like lights. Untangle now, not in December.
3. **Tree skirts and stockings:** wash if needed, dry fully, then store with holiday linens. Use acid-free tissue between embroidered or beaded pieces so they don't snag.
4. **Table linens:** fold with tissue between folds to avoid permanent creases, and store flat.
5. **Santa suits, costumes, and fabric decor:** these are the classic mouse target. Store in a hard-sided bin, not a bag.
6. **Never store fabric in a sealed plastic bag.** It traps moisture and can cause mildew, and it also attracts pests more than a hard bin does.

## Step 6: Sort the outdoor decorations

1. **Inflatable decorations:** clean, dry completely, then store with the stakes and tethers in a labeled bag. Never store inflatables damp — they mildew and the blower motor corrodes.
2. **Light stakes and clips:** keep in a single labeled bag. These get lost more than anything else and are annoying to rebuy.
3. **Extension cords:** coil on spools, and check for damage before storing. Discard cords with cracked insulation.
4. **Outdoor timers and controllers:** store indoors; the electronics don't survive damp.
5. **Lighted figures:** wrap in bubble wrap, and store in a rigid container so the wire frame isn't crushed.

## Step 7: Build storage that works next year

The container system determines whether this is a 2-hour job or a 20-minute job.

**Container choice**

- **Use rigid bins with lids** for anything breakable or fabric. Cardboard collapses, absorbs moisture, and invites pests.
- **Clear bins** for items you dig through; **opaque uniform bins** if the storage area is visible.
- **Match bin sizes within a system** for stacking efficiency.
- **Never use bags** for breakables, fabric, or anything an animal can chew through.

**Location**

- **Off the floor**, on shelving or pallets. Concrete wicks moisture year-round.
- **Interior storage preferred.** Attics and garages experience extreme temperature swings; basements are damp. A climate-stable closet is best, and worth the space it takes.
- **Never against exterior walls**, the coldest and dampest surface in a garage.
- **Avoid direct sunlight** if the storage area has windows — UV degrades plastic and fabric.

**Organization**

- **Group by category**, not by room: all lights together, all ornaments together.
- **Label every container** on the visible side and on the lid, listing contents.
- **Photograph the packed layout** and keep the photo on your phone. It answers "where did I put the village pieces" without opening anything.
- **Keep an inventory list** in your phone notes — including what needs replacing.
- **Store the oldest or least-used items in the hardest-to-reach spots.**

## Step 8: Avoid the classic storage mistakes

| Mistake | What it causes |
|---|---|
| Loose lights in a bag | Tangles that cost an hour |
| Ornaments packed touching each other | Chips and breaks |
| Newspaper wrapping | Ink transfers permanently to finishes |
| Damp basement or hot attic storage | Rusted frames, yellowed trees, degraded plastic |
| Sealed plastic bags for fabric | Mildew |
| Cardboard boxes on concrete | Moisture wicking, pests, collapse |
| Storing anything damp | Mold that spreads through the whole bin |
| No labels | Opening every bin to find one item |
| Attached hooks in storage | Scratches and tangles |
| Storing houseplants or food in the same area | Attracts pests to the decorations |

Decorations spend eleven months a year in whatever space the house has left over. For the storage conditions that decide whether they survive it, see [how to store things in a basement](/organize/home-systems/basement-storage-without-ruining/).

## Why This Works

Most decoration damage is mechanical or biological, and both are preventable by the same three rules.

The mechanical damage is contact. Ornaments stored loose in a bin rub against each other every time the bin moves, and painted and metallic finishes abrade quickly. Individual wrapping or divided trays eliminate contact. Lights stored loose interlock into knots because the strands have hundreds of attachment points — spooling constrains the strand to a single winding, which cannot tangle.

Paper choice matters chemically, not just physically. Newsprint is acidic, and acid migrates into materials it touches over months, causing discoloration and accelerating degradation of paints and metals. Acid-free tissue does not, which is why archival storage uses it. This is the same principle behind acid-free storage for photographs and documents.

Moisture damage has two paths. Direct damp from a humid space causes mildew and rust. Thermal cycling in an attic or garage is subtler: warm days drive moisture into materials and cool nights condense it. A tree stored in an attic that swings from 40°F to 130°F goes through hundreds of these cycles, which rusts the frame and embrittles the branches. A stable closet avoids both paths, which is why location matters more than container quality.

The no-sealed-plastic rule follows from vapor movement. Any organic material holds some moisture, and sealing it traps that moisture against the surface in a warm microclimate. That's precisely the condition mildew needs. A hard bin with a lid is not airtight, so vapor can escape, while a bag is effectively sealed.

Pests are attracted by nesting material and by food residue. Fabric items, especially wool and cotton, are ideal nesting material, and decorations stored near food or in a space with rodent access get chewed. Hard-sided bins and elevation off the floor remove the access, and keeping food away from the storage area removes the attraction.

Finally, labeling and photographs address the retrieval problem rather than the damage problem — but retrieval is where most of the frustration comes from. A labeled system turns a search through six bins into a direct pull, and the photograph means you don't have to open anything at all.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Storing light strands loose in a bag | Tangles take far longer than spooling | Spool, hanger, or coil-and-tie each strand |
| Wrapping ornaments in newspaper | Acidic ink transfers and stains finishes | Acid-free tissue |
| Packing ornaments touching each other | Rubbing abrades paint and chips glass | Individual wrap or divided trays |
| Storing in a hot attic or damp basement | Rust, yellowing, embrittlement, mildew | Stable interior closet |
| Using sealed plastic bags for fabric | Traps moisture, grows mildew | Hard bins with lids |
| Cardboard boxes on a concrete floor | Wicks moisture, attracts pests, collapses | Rigid bins elevated on shelving |
| Storing decorations without testing lights first | You store dead strands and rediscover them | Test and discard before packing |
| Leaving hooks on ornaments | Scratches finishes and tangles | Store hooks separately |
| No labels or inventory | Opening everything to find one item | Label containers and keep a phone inventory |
| Storing wreaths flat under other boxes | Deforms the shape permanently | Dedicated wreath box, hung |
| Storing inflatables while damp | Mildew and corroded blower motors | Dry completely, store indoors |
| Keeping no record of what needs replacing | You rebuy duplicates or miss items | List it while packing |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do I stop Christmas lights from tangling in storage?**
Wind each strand onto a spool, a notched piece of cardboard, or a clothes hanger. Alternatively, coil each strand into a loose circle and secure it with a twist tie, taping the two plug ends together. Never store strands loose in a bag.

**What's the best way to store fragile ornaments?**
Wrap each one individually in acid-free tissue and pack them in divided trays, largest and heaviest at the bottom. Fill empty space so nothing shifts, and store hooks separately.

**Can I store an artificial Christmas tree in the attic or garage?**
It's not ideal. Both spaces swing in temperature, which rusts the frame and embrittles the branches, and garages are damp. A climate-stable interior closet is much better if you have the space.

**Should I use plastic bags or bins for decorations?**
Bins with lids, not sealed bags. Bags trap moisture against fabric and organic materials, which causes mildew. Hard-sided bins also keep pests out.

**How do I keep mice out of stored decorations?**
Use hard-sided bins with lids, store off the floor on shelving, keep the storage area free of food, and seal any openings into the space. Fabric items like Santa suits and stockings are the most commonly chewed.

**Is newspaper okay for wrapping ornaments?**
No. Newsprint is acidic and the ink transfers to painted and metallic finishes over months. Use acid-free tissue paper instead.

**How should I store wreaths?**
In a dedicated wreath box or a hanging bag, never flat under other boxes. Compressed wreaths stay deformed and don't recover.

**When should I buy replacement decorations?**
During the off-season or in early fall. Selection is better and prices on last-season stock are lower. Keep a list of what you discarded while packing so you don't rebuy the wrong things.
