In October they come to the sunny side of the house, and by November they are inside — in the window frames, behind the curtains, on the back of the bathroom mirror, in the lamp shade. Unlike the boxelder bugs that arrive at the same time, these do not go quiet. A brown marmorated stink bug that has settled into a wall cavity will wake up on any mild day all winter, fly to a light, and bump around the room.

That difference changes the method. Boxelder bugs on a wall are a nuisance you can largely ignore; stink bugs indoors through a whole winter are worth actually excluding, because their activity is continuous and their main defense — a strong, persistent odor released when disturbed or crushed — makes handling them unpleasant in a way that compounds the problem.

There is also a second cost that gets lost in the indoor conversation: this insect is a serious agricultural pest, and if you keep a vegetable garden or fruit trees, the population in your yard is doing real damage to them, not to your house. The autumn-shelter behavior these bugs share with boxelder bugs is covered in [boxelder bugs on the south wall](/pest-control/insects/boxelder-bugs-fall-invasion/); this guide is the case where exclusion is genuinely worth the work.

## They Are a Farm Pest Wearing an Indoor Costume

Brown marmorated stink bugs arrived in the United States from East Asia in the late 1990s, first identified in Pennsylvania, and spread through the mid-Atlantic and beyond. They feed by piercing fruit, pods, and leaves and sucking out the contents — apples, peaches, peppers, tomatoes, corn, soybeans, beans. The damage appears as dimpled, corky spots on fruit and "cat-facing" deformity on tomatoes.

They do not bite people, do not sting, do not chew wood, do not damage insulation, do not breed indoors, and do not transmit disease. What they do indoors is exist loudly: they fly to lights, they land on you, and they release an odor when threatened that lingers on fabric.

So the honest framing is: exclude them because the winter-long activity is annoying and because the ones in your walls are the same population that will be in your garden next summer. Eradication is not the goal and is not achievable.

## Step 1: Separate Them From the Look-alikes

Several shield-shaped bugs show up on and in houses in autumn. Getting the species right tells you whether to bother.

| | Brown marmorated stink bug | Boxelder bug | Kudzu bug |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Shield, broad shoulders | Elongated oval, flat-backed | Rounder, boxy, very convex |
| Color | Mottled brown with white banding on the antennae and the edge of the abdomen | Black with red-orange margins | Olive-brown to dark brown |
| Size | About 5/8 inch | About 1/2 inch | About 1/4 inch, smaller than a pea |
| When disturbed | Flies or drops, and smells strongly | Runs, may fly a short distance, faint smell | Flies readily, can stain and irritate skin |
| Indoors in winter | Active on mild days, flies to light | Mostly dormant, moves to light in spring | Tends to stay near the entry |

The antennae are the fastest field mark: brown marmorated stink bugs have distinct light and dark bands on the last two segments, like a tiny barber pole. Boxelder bugs have solid dark antennae.

One caution about the kudzu bug, which is common in the Southeast: it can cause a mild skin irritation and it stains fabric, and it is smaller than people expect, so it gets dismissed as a beetle.

## Step 2: Count What Is Actually Inside

Before you commit to work, establish the scale.

Walk the rooms with a flashlight in the afternoon and look in the places they settle:

- The channels of window frames and the track of a sliding door
- Behind curtains and blinds, especially on south and west windows
- Behind pictures, mirrors, and wall clocks
- In the folds of a lamp shade and inside light fixtures
- Behind baseboards that have a gap at the top
- In the corners of a ceiling where two walls meet
- Behind the cover plate of an exterior-wall outlet

Count what you find and note where. Ten to twenty in a season across a whole house is a normal infiltration through imperfect sealing. Hundreds, concentrated near one window, means there is a route at that window worth fixing.

This count is also your baseline. After sealing, the same walk in February should produce far fewer.

## Step 3: Build the Light Trap — It Is the Only Thing That Reliably Works Indoors

This is the practical core of the whole guide, and it costs about the price of a takeout meal.

The principle: stink bugs are strongly attracted to a bright light against a dark background. If the only light in a dark room is above a pan of soapy water, they fly to it and land in the water, where the soap breaks the surface tension and they drown rather than float.

**Build it:**

1. Take a disposable aluminum foil roasting pan, roughly 9 by 13 inches.
2. Fill it with an inch of water and add a generous squeeze of dish soap — a tablespoon or so. Stir gently; you do not want suds, you want the water's surface tension broken.
3. Clip a small LED lamp to the rim of the pan, pointing straight down at the water, or stand a small lamp centered over it.
4. Place the pan on the floor or a low table in the room where you see the most activity.

**Run it:**

- Do this in the evening, in a room you can darken completely. Close the curtains, turn off every other light, and leave the room.
- Leave it three to four hours, or overnight if the room is not a bedroom.
- Empty it into the toilet or outdoors each morning and refill. A pan left for days grows a smell of its own.
- Repeat nightly for two to three weeks in late autumn, when the indoor population is highest.

The trap catches the ones that are already inside and active. It does not touch the ones sealed in a wall cavity — nothing does, short of opening the wall, which is not worth it for this insect.

A note for anyone with pets or small children: an open pan of water on the floor is a hazard. Put it on a counter or a table out of reach, or run it only in a closed room.

## Step 4: Seal the Autumn Entry Points, Because That Is the Actual Fix

Trapping indoors is a cleanup. Sealing is what reduces next year's population.

Do this work in late September and early October, before the bulk of the movement. The general route is in [how to seal gaps around doors and windows](/fix-it/maintenance/seal-gaps-doors-windows/); the autumn-specific items are:

- **Window frames**, especially on south and west elevations. Run a bead of caulk along the exterior trim and check the interior stops. A window that has never been caulked at the exterior sill is a common route.
- **Door sweeps and thresholds.** A visible gap under an exterior door is an open invitation for anything that walks.
- **Weep holes in brick veneer.** These are intentional, and plugging them causes moisture problems — but they can be screened with a weep hole vent cover, which is the correct fix.
- **Utility penetrations** — the AC line set, the dryer vent, the meter base, the cable entry. Seal with an exterior-grade sealant or a purpose-made boot.
- **Siding gaps and the joint where siding meets the foundation.** A bead of sealant along that line closes hundreds of small openings.
- **Exterior wall outlets and light fixtures.** Foam gaskets behind the cover plates are inexpensive and effective.
- **The attic.** Check that soffit vents are screened and that the ridge vent is intact. An attic is a large, warm, undisturbed volume, and a population that gets in there will drop into the living space all winter.

The fall maintenance route that puts this alongside gutter and roof work is in [how to prepare your home for fall](/seasonal/seasons/prepare-home-for-fall/), and the summer version of the same exclusion work is in [keeping bugs out of the house](/seasonal/seasons/keep-bugs-out-of-house-summer/).

## Step 5: Stop Crushing Them

This is not squeamishness. Crushing a stink bug releases the odor compound from the thoracic glands, and the smell persists on a curtain, a wall, or a rug for days. It also stains light fabric.

Better handling, in order:

- **Vacuum them.** Use a hose attachment and empty the canister outdoors immediately. Some people put a nylon stocking over the hose end to make disposal cleaner.
- **Sweep them into a container** with a piece of paper or a cup, and flush them.
- **Pick them up with a tissue** and drop them in soapy water.
- **If one does get crushed**, ventilate the room and wash the surface with a dilute dish soap solution rather than scrubbing, which spreads the compound.

Do not swat them against a wall. It is the most reliable way to make a small problem into a smell that lasts a week.

## Step 6: Handle the Spring Exit, and Know When the Nest Is Done

In March and April, the survivors that overwintered in your walls wake up and head for the nearest light, which is usually a window. They are trying to get out.

You will find them on windowsills in numbers, and it looks like a new infestation. It is not — it is the exit. Open the window, or trap them at the sill, or vacuum them. There is no treatment that improves on letting them leave.

The same is true outdoors. A paper wasp or yellowjacket nest from last year is abandoned and will not be reused; stink bugs do not build a nest at all, so there is nothing to remove.

By late spring the indoor population is zero regardless of what you do, and the problem resets in September. That cycle is why the sealing work in Step 4 is the only intervention with an effect that lasts longer than one season.

## Why Indoor Spraying Is a Waste

A residual spray applied to a windowsill or a baseboard kills the bugs that walk across it. The population behind the drywall does not walk across it, and the population outside keeps arriving.

Worse, spraying a surface that stink bugs use as a route has a rebound effect: dead and dying bugs on a sill, day after day, are a collected food source for carpet beetle larvae and other dermestids, which do actual damage to textiles. The general caution about dead insects as a secondary food source is in [carpet beetles in the house](/pest-control/insects/carpet-beetles-in-house/).

Exterior barrier treatments around the foundation do have a place in a severe, repeated infestation, applied by a professional in early autumn. That is a targeted use, not a weekly chore, and it does not replace sealing.

## The Garden Problem Is the Real One

If you have fruit trees, cane fruit, peppers, tomatoes, or beans, the stink bugs in your house are a small sample of the population feeding on them.

What actually reduces the population:

- **Inspect fruit and pods weekly** from June onward and remove infested ones rather than leaving them to drop and shelter the next generation.
- **Check the underside of leaves** for the barrel-shaped eggs, laid in clusters of about 28, and remove them when you find them.
- **Row cover on brassicas and beans** during the flight periods works better than any spray, because it excludes rather than kills.
- **Avoid broad-spectrum garden sprays.** They kill the parasitic wasps and tachinid flies that do more against stink bugs than any homeowner product.
- **Remove host trees near the house** where that is practical — particularly tree of heaven, which is a preferred host — and prune branches away from the roofline.

For the house itself, none of this is necessary. It is only worth the effort if you garden.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not fog the house.** A fogger does not reach wall cavities, leaves residue on kitchen surfaces, and produces no lasting reduction.

**Do not seal the bugs inside.** If they are already in the wall, sealing the exterior in December traps them where they are. Seal in early autumn, before the movement, or accept the spring exit.

**Do not crush them.** The odor is the whole reason this insect is memorable.

**Do not rely on repellent sachets.** Mothballs, dryer sheets, lavender, and cedar have no reliable effect on stink bugs and some are a vapor exposure you do not need in a bedroom.

**Do not use an insect light zapper.** These kill moths and beneficial insects in large numbers and are far less effective against stink bugs than the simple pan-and-lamp trap, which catches them by drowning rather than electrocuting.

**Do not assume a spring sighting is a new infestation.** They are leaving. Let them.

## FAQ

**Why are there so many stink bugs in my house in the fall?**

They are looking for shelter to overwinter. In late September and October they gather on warm exterior walls and work into any narrow gap — window frames, siding, soffits, utility penetrations. Once inside a wall cavity they are protected and will become active on mild days all winter.

**What is the best way to get rid of stink bugs indoors?**

A light trap: a foil pan of soapy water with a small lamp over it, in a darkened room, run overnight. It catches the active indoor population. It does not reach the ones in the walls, so pair it with exterior sealing before the next autumn.

**Do stink bugs bite?**

No. They have piercing mouthparts for plant tissue and cannot bite or sting a person. An itchy spot after handling one is contact irritation from the defensive fluid, not a bite.

**Are stink bugs harmful to the house?**

No structural damage, no wood chewing, no nesting, no disease. The costs are the odor, the staining on fabric, the annoyance of continuous winter activity, and the fact that the same population damages garden crops.

**How are they different from boxelder bugs?**

Stink bugs are shield-shaped and mottled brown with banded antennae; boxelder bugs are elongated black with red-orange margins. Stink bugs stay active indoors all winter and fly to light; boxelder bugs are mostly dormant and are active in spring. Stink bugs also smell far worse when disturbed.

**Do I need an exterminator?**

Usually not for the indoor nuisance. Sealing plus a light trap handles it. A professional exterior barrier treatment in early autumn has a place in a severe repeated infestation, but it does not replace sealing the entry points.

**Will they go away on their own?**

Yes, by late spring — the indoor population is zero once the overwintering survivors leave. The problem returns each September unless the entry points are sealed.

## Bottom Line

These are the autumn bugs that do not go quiet, so exclusion is worth doing properly. Seal the window frames, door sweeps, utility penetrations, and siding gaps in late September, before the movement. For the ones already inside, run a foil pan of soapy water under a single lamp in a dark room overnight, for two or three weeks, and vacuum rather than crush. Skip the fogger and the windowsill spray — neither reaches a wall cavity, and the dead bugs left behind feed carpet beetles. In spring, open the window and let them out, because that is what they are trying to do.
