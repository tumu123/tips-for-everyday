Property tax is the largest recurring tax most households pay — a line worth reviewing once a year alongside the rest of [household spending](/save-money/shopping/track-household-spending/) — and it is calculated from a number that a government office estimated — often with a model, occasionally with a drive-by, and rarely with anyone walking through the house. That number is not final. It is an opinion, and in most jurisdictions you are entitled to disagree with it on the record, once a year, within a window.

The reason almost nobody does is not that the process is hard. It is that the notice arrives looking like a bill, and a bill feels like a fact. The households that appeal are not doing anything unusual; they are reading the same notice and checking two things.

## The Two Numbers on the Notice

Every assessment notice contains them, though the labels vary:

**The assessed value** — what the jurisdiction says your property is worth for tax purposes. In some states this equals market value; in others it is a percentage (the assessment ratio, sometimes 25%, 40%, or 100%).

**The tax rate** — a millage rate, levy, or per-$100 rate applied to the assessed value. Multiply and you get the tax.

A common and expensive confusion: **you can only appeal the value, not the rate.** If your tax bill rose because the town raised the rate, an appeal will not help — that is a budget vote, and it is handled at the ballot box. If it rose because your assessed value jumped, an appeal is exactly the right tool.

Second confusion: **an increase is not automatically wrong.** If every house in the neighborhood went up 12% and yours went up 12%, the assessment is probably defensible. The appeal case is not "it went up," it is "it is assessed higher than comparable properties" or "it is assessed above what it would sell for."

## Step 1: Find Your Deadline, Which Is Short

Appeal windows are usually 30 to 90 days from the date on the notice, and they are strict. Some jurisdictions require the appeal in writing; some require a specific form; some require it online. Miss the window and your next chance is next year — the tax you pay in the meantime is not refunded retroactively in most places.

Find three things before anything else:

- **The deadline**, from the notice or the assessor's website.
- **The procedure** — informal review first, or straight to a board hearing? Many jurisdictions have a quick informal review with an assessor, which resolves a large share of cases without a hearing.
- **The evidence rules** — what the board accepts, and whether you must submit evidence before the hearing (many require it, and refuse to consider anything introduced at the hearing).

Put the deadline in your calendar with a two-week buffer, because gathering comparable sales takes longer than people expect.

## Step 2: Check Your Own Record First

Before you argue about value, check that the record describes your house correctly. Assessor databases are built from permits, aerial imagery, and occasional inspections, and they carry errors that are invisible until you look:

- **Square footage.** Finished area above grade, basement area, and whether a finished basement was counted as living area.
- **Bedroom and bathroom count.** A three-bedroom house recorded as four is a material error.
- **Lot size.** Especially where parcels were split or combined.
- **Year built and effective age.** Renovations are sometimes recorded as additions.
- **Garage, pool, outbuildings, decks.** A shed recorded as a garage, or a demolished pool still on the roll.
- **Exemptions.** Homestead, senior, veteran, disability, and agricultural exemptions are frequently not applied because they require an application the owner never filed. In many states a homestead exemption alone is worth several hundred dollars a year.

A factual correction is the easiest possible appeal: it does not require an opinion about value, just a document showing the record is wrong. Pull your closing documents, the appraisal from your purchase, and any permit records.

## Step 3: Build the Comparable Sales Case

If the record is accurate, the appeal becomes an argument about value, and value is argued with comparable sales — sales of similar properties, near your property, close in time.

**How comparable, and how close:**

- **Three to five sales** is the useful range. More is not better if they are weak.
- **Within 6 to 12 months** of the assessment date. The assessment date is often January 1 of the tax year, not the date on the notice — check.
- **Within roughly half a mile** in a dense area, up to a couple of miles in a rural one.
- **Similar in living area** (within about 20%), age, bedroom and bathroom count, lot size, and construction type.
- **Arm's-length sales only** — no foreclosures, estate sales to family, or quitclaim transfers.

**Where to find them:** the assessor's own public records (best, because the board trusts its own data), county deed and transfer records, and the major property portals for an initial screen. Verify every sale you intend to use against the recorded deed or the assessor's sales list, because portal data is often stale or wrong about sale type.

**How to present them:** a one-page table with address, sale date, sale price, living area, year built, beds and baths, lot size, and the adjustment you would make for each difference. Then a short paragraph: "These five sales average $X per square foot. My property is assessed at $Y per square foot. The assessment is Z% above the market indicated by the sales the county itself recorded."

Per-square-foot comparison is the argument boards understand best. It is not perfect — it ignores condition — but it is simple, checkable, and hard to dismiss.

## Step 4: The Evidence That Strengthens a Weak Case

Comparable sales do most of the work, but three other categories carry weight when the sales are ambiguous.

**An independent appraisal.** $300 to $600 for a full appraisal, and it is the strongest single document you can bring. Justified when the gap is large — a $40,000 over-assessment on a property taxed at 2% is $800 a year, which pays for the appraisal in year one.

**Photographs and contractor estimates of defects.** A failed foundation, a roof at the end of its life, water intrusion, a busy road at the property line, a shared driveway, or a former commercial use next door. Condition adjustments are legitimate and are the main reason two identical floor plans are worth different amounts. A contractor's written estimate to cure a defect is far better than your description of it.

**The purchase price, if recent.** If you bought the house within the last year or two in an arm's-length sale, your price is usually the best evidence of value there is. Bring the closing statement. Assessors will often correct to the sale price on this alone.

**The income approach**, only if you own a rental. Rent rolls and expense statements support a value based on income, which can beat a sales comparison in a market with few comparable sales.

## Step 5: Decide Whether to Appear in Person

You can usually appear yourself, send a representative, or in some jurisdictions submit written evidence only. Appearing in person generally improves outcomes, for a mundane reason: the board sees a person, hears a specific argument, and can ask questions. A written submission with no one present is easier to deny.

If you appear:

- Bring **five copies** of everything — the board, the assessor's representative, and yourself.
- Open with the number, not the story: "The assessment is $412,000. Five comparable sales average $366,000. I'm asking for $366,000."
- Answer questions directly. If you do not know something, say so.
- Do not argue about the tax rate, the school budget, or fairness in general. The board's jurisdiction is the value.
- Keep it to five minutes. You will be interrupted and asked questions; that is normal and not hostility.

If you cannot attend, some jurisdictions allow a written submission or a phone appearance — check the notice. A contingent-fee appeal service typically charges 25% to 35% of the first year's savings; that is reasonable for a large commercial property and usually poor value for a house, where the process is a form, a table, and an afternoon.

## Step 6: Understand What You Are Actually Winning

A successful appeal reduces the **assessed value**, and the tax follows. A 10% reduction on a $400,000 assessment at a 2% effective rate is $800 a year, recurring, because the reduced value carries forward until the next reassessment.

What it does not do:

- **It does not lower your mortgage or [your insurance](/save-money/bills/lower-homeowners-insurance-premium/).** Some lenders require you to notify them of a changed assessment; it does not change your payment.
- **It does not automatically apply to future years** in every state. Some carry the corrected value forward; others reassess and can land back where they started. Note the date of the next reassessment.
- **It does not reduce the rate**, and a successful appeal by many homeowners can, in the long run, shift the levy onto the remaining base — a reason towns sometimes fight appeals harder than the individual numbers justify.

The one thing it can affect: if you are in a state where assessed value is capped or where exemptions phase out by value, a lower assessment can qualify you for relief you did not have before. Check the exemption thresholds at the same time.

## Common Reasons Appeals Fail

- **Arguing the tax is too high** rather than the value is too high. The board cannot help with the first.
- **Using listings instead of sales.** Asking prices are opinions; the board wants closed, recorded, arm's-length sales.
- **Comparables that are not comparable** — a smaller ranch two miles away in a different school district, or a distressed sale.
- **Missing the evidence deadline**, which most jurisdictions treat as fatal.
- **No adjustment for differences.** If your comparable has 400 more square feet, the board will notice, and the credibility of your whole table drops.
- **Emotional presentation.** Boards hear hundreds of these; the ones that win are the ones with a table.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it worth appealing if the increase is small?**
Do the arithmetic: reduction × effective tax rate = annual saving. Under about $200 a year, the time cost usually exceeds the benefit — unless the correction is a factual error, which is worth fixing because it compounds for as long as you own the house.

**Can I appeal every year?**
Usually yes, if the value changes or you have new evidence. Some jurisdictions limit repeat appeals without a change in circumstances, so check.

**Do I need a lawyer?**
No, for a house. The process is designed for owners acting for themselves. Lawyers are for commercial property or a coverage dispute that has already gone badly.

**What if I just bought the house at a price above the assessment?**
Then you probably should not appeal on value. If your purchase price is above the assessed value, the assessment is favorable and an appeal risks a correction upward, which some jurisdictions will make.

**Can the assessor raise my value if I appeal?**
In most places, no — the board cannot increase the assessment as a result of your appeal. In a few, it can. Ask before you file.

**How long does it take?**
Informal reviews resolve in weeks. Formal hearings typically take two to six months, and the reduction usually appears on the following tax bill or as a credit.

**Does the assessed value affect what I can sell for?**
No. Buyers look at the market, not the roll. A low assessment is a small selling point because it implies lower taxes, which is one more reason to fix errors rather than leave them.

## Bottom Line

Read the notice, find the deadline, check the record for factual errors, and pull three to five genuine comparable sales from the assessor's own data. Present it as a one-page table with per-square-foot math and a specific number you are asking for. Most appeals fail not because the assessment was right, but because the owner argued about the bill instead of the value, missed the evidence deadline, or brought listings instead of sales.
