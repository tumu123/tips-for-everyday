The bites are on your ankles, three or four in a row, and the itching starts about twenty minutes after you notice them. You have no cat. You have no dog. The house was empty for six weeks before you moved in, or the previous tenant had a dog, or a raccoon has been in the crawlspace, or the neighborhood cat sleeps on the porch step every afternoon. There is a flea problem, and none of the usual explanations apply.

This is not a rare situation. The cat flea, *Ctenocephalides felis*, is the dominant flea species in American homes, and despite the name it feeds happily on dogs, raccoons, opossums, foxes, and humans. A house does not need a resident pet to have fleas. It needs one animal to have passed through, once, at some point in the previous several months.

The reason an empty house can erupt into a biting problem the day you move in is the pupal stage, and understanding it changes everything about how you treat. The general sequence of identifying a source before treating it is set out in [the DIY pest control method](/save-money/bills/save-money-on-pest-control-diy/); fleas are the case where that principle matters most, because most flea treatment is aimed at the wrong life stage in the wrong place.

## Why the Problem Appears After the House Was Empty

A flea spends only part of its life on an animal. The adult is the only stage that bites, and it is roughly 5 percent of the population at any moment. The other 95 percent — eggs, larvae, and pupae — are in the environment: carpet fibers, floorboard gaps, the nap of a sofa, the dust under a bed, the padding beneath a rug.

Flea eggs do not stick to anything. They fall off the host within a few hours of being laid, which means wherever an animal rested, eggs rained down into the carpet. Larvae hatch in two to fourteen days and burrow away from light, down into the base of the carpet pile, along the seam where wall meets floor, and into the crevices between floorboards. They do not bite. They eat flea dirt — the dried blood in adult feces — and organic debris.

Then they spin a cocoon and become pupae, and this is where the timeline breaks. A pupa in a silk cocoon covered with carpet debris is almost invulnerable. It is not affected by most insecticides, because the cocoon does not absorb them. It does not need to feed. And it can wait.

A flea pupa can remain dormant in its cocoon for months — six months is routinely cited, and longer periods have been recorded in vacant buildings. What triggers emergence is a specific combination of cues: vibration, physical pressure, warmth, and a rise in carbon dioxide. In an empty house, none of those cues are present. The pupae sit. The day a family moves in, the floor vibrates with footsteps, bodies generate heat and carbon dioxide, and the whole dormant cohort hatches within hours.

This is why people describe fleas as having "appeared overnight" in a house that stood empty. They were there the whole time, waiting for a host to walk over them.

## Step 1: Confirm It Is Actually Fleas

Before treating, get the identification right, because the treatment for fleas is a lot of work and the wrong diagnosis wastes all of it.

The bite pattern is a useful clue but not proof. Fleas cannot fly; they jump, and they tend to bite the lowest parts of the body they can reach — ankles, the back of the calves, the waistband where clothing binds. Bites often appear in clusters of three or four. Mosquito bites are usually fewer and higher up; bedbug bites are often in lines on exposed skin during sleep, including arms and face.

Real confirmation comes from finding the insect or its waste:

| Evidence | Where to look | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Small dark insects, 1/8 inch, that jump when disturbed | Carpet edges, pet bedding, under furniture | Adult fleas |
| Dark grit that turns red-brown on a damp paper towel | Same areas, plus sofa seams | Flea dirt — digested blood |
| Tiny white oval eggs | Difficult to see; they fall into the pile | Active infestation upstream |
| Barely visible silk-covered debris at carpet base | Along baseboards, in floor gaps | Pupae in cocoon |

The damp paper towel test is the most reliable do-it-yourself check. Vacuum a square foot of carpet, empty the contents onto a white paper towel, add a drop of water. If dark specks bleed rust red, that is flea dirt, and it means adults have been feeding in that spot.

For a comparison with a pest that produces similar looking damage indoors, see [how to identify carpet beetles](/pest-control/insects/carpet-beetles-in-house/). Carpet beetle larvae chew fabric and leave cast skins; they do not bite, and there is no red-brown grit.

## Step 2: Locate the Source Before You Treat Anything

Treating the whole house because of bites on your ankles is the standard mistake. Fleas are not evenly distributed. They concentrate where an animal rested, and those spots are usually a small fraction of the floor area.

Walk the house with a flashlight and a pad of sticky notes, and mark every location where you find any of the evidence above. Then add the places an animal would have chosen if it had gotten in:

- The doormat and the first two feet of floor inside every exterior door
- A porch, stoop, or garage floor where a neighborhood cat or dog lies
- The crawlspace access and any gap where a raccoon or opossum could enter
- Under beds, under the sofa, and inside the frame of any upholstered furniture
- Closets where coats and bags are stored low
- The basement, particularly near a sump or a floor drain

If the previous occupant had pets, also check the trunk of a car and any upholstered furniture that came with the house.

Outdoors, fleas survive in shaded, humid spots: under decks, in leaf litter, along a fence line where animals travel. They do not survive long in open sun or on a mown, dry lawn. Your yard is usually not the source, but the shaded strip beside the foundation can be.

Where rodents are the likely carrier, the entry points are the same ones that matter for the rodents themselves. For that part of the problem, see [how to find where mice are getting in](/pest-control/rodents/mice-in-the-walls/).

## Step 3: Vacuum Like You Mean It, Every Day, for Two Weeks

Vacuuming is the single most effective non-chemical action against fleas, and it is not because it removes them — it does remove a lot, but the bigger effect is behavioral.

Vibration and pressure from a vacuum cleaner trigger dormant pupae to emerge. That sounds counterproductive. It is not: a pupa in its cocoon is nearly immune to everything. An adult flea on the carpet surface is vulnerable to vacuuming, to desiccation, and to any product you apply. Vacuuming converts your protected reservoir into exposed adults, and then removes them.

The practical rules:

- **Daily, for at least fourteen days.** One pass will not do it. The point is to keep interrupting the cycle faster than it can complete.
- **Use the beater bar on carpet.** The mechanical agitation is the trigger. A suction-only pass on hard floor is fine, but carpet needs the brush roll.
- **Concentrate on the marked hot spots**, and give each one slow, overlapping passes — roughly ten seconds per square foot rather than a quick sweep.
- **Use the crevice tool along every baseboard** and in floor gaps. Larvae develop there.
- **Seal and remove the bag.** A vacuum bag is a flea incubator. If your vacuum uses bags, bag the contents in a zip bag and put it outside immediately. If it is bagless, empty it into an outdoor bin and rinse the canister.
- **Vacuum the furniture**, including under the cushions, and the inside of the car if an animal has ridden in it.

Vacuuming is also the moment to deal with pet hair embedded in the carpet, which holds eggs and provides larval food. For that task specifically, see [how to get pet hair out of carpet and upholstery](/home-hacks/cleaning/remove-pet-hair-everywhere/).

## Step 4: Wash Everything That Can Go in a Machine

Flea eggs, larvae, and pupae do not survive a hot wash and a hot dryer. Anything that touches the floor or a resting animal should go through both.

- Bedding, throws, blankets, and slipcovers: wash at 140°F or the hottest setting the fabric allows, then dry on high heat for at least 30 minutes.
- Pet bedding, if there was any: this is often the single densest reservoir in the house, and it is frequently thrown out rather than washed. Washing at high heat works; if the item is beyond cleaning, seal it in a bag and remove it.
- Rugs and mats that fit in a machine: wash them. Rugs that do not: vacuum both sides and hang them in direct sun for a day.
- Shoes and slippers kept by the door: these are a common hidden reservoir. Wipe the insides and leave them in sun.

Dry heat matters as much as the wash. A hot dryer for 30 minutes kills all stages; line drying in cool weather does not.

## Step 5: Apply an Insect Growth Regulator, Not Just an Adulticide

This is where most DIY flea treatment fails, and the reason is the same one that makes foggers useless.

An adulticide — the spray that kills fleas on contact — reaches only the adults that are out in the open at the moment you apply it. That is roughly 5 percent of the population. The eggs, larvae, and pupae in the carpet are untouched, and the pupae will hatch over the following weeks, producing what looks like reinfestation.

An insect growth regulator (IGR) works differently. It does not kill adults. It mimics juvenile hormone, so larvae that contact it cannot complete the molt to the next stage, and eggs laid by treated adults do not hatch. The two common active ingredients are methoprene and pyriproxyfen. Applied to carpet and upholstery, an IGR remains active for months and breaks the cycle at the point where it is actually vulnerable.

The effective approach is both, used together:

1. Vacuum thoroughly — this triggers emergence and removes the bulk of the population.
2. Apply an IGR to all carpet, rugs, upholstery, and the cracks along baseboards, following the label exactly.
3. Allow it to dry completely and stay off the treated surfaces until it does.
4. Keep vacuuming daily. The IGR survives vacuuming on the fiber; you are removing adults, not the treatment.
5. Reapply only at the interval on the label, not sooner.

Do not combine a fogger with this. Foggers disperse a fine mist that settles on surfaces without penetrating carpet pile, reach nothing below the surface, and are a fire hazard in a closed room. They are the most common reason people conclude that "nothing works."

If there are pets in the house — even if the fleas did not come from them — the animal must be treated at the same time, or the house will be reinfested from the animal within days. That treatment is a veterinary decision, not a household one, and the safety point below is not optional.

## Step 6: Break the Outdoor Source

If the source is outside — a neighborhood cat's resting spot on the porch, a raccoon in the crawlspace, a shaded strip along the foundation — indoor treatment alone will not hold. Fleas will be carried back in on shoes and on the animal itself.

- **Stop the animal from resting against the house.** Remove the cushion, the doormat it sleeps on, or the crate. A cat will choose a new spot, ideally one farther from your door.
- **Clear the shaded, humid band along the foundation.** Rake out leaf litter, cut back ground cover, and let sun reach the soil. Flea larvae need moisture; dry, exposed soil does not support them.
- **Treat the shaded outdoor band** if it is a confirmed source, using a product labeled for outdoor flea control on lawns. Focus on the shaded perimeter, not the whole yard.
- **Deal with the rodent or wildlife entry.** A crawlspace that admits raccoons is a flea source and a much larger problem. Sealing it closes both.

## Verification: Expect Three Months, Not Three Days

The difficult number to accept is the timeline. Because pupae can stay dormant for months and emerge in waves, a single treatment round does not end a flea problem. What ends it is a monitoring routine that catches each wave.

Set up monitoring that does not depend on getting bitten:

- **White socks.** Walk slowly through the treated rooms in white athletic socks. Fleas jump onto the socks and are visible against white. Do this daily for the first two weeks, then twice a week.
- **A soap dish trap.** A shallow dish of water with a drop of dish soap, placed on the floor in a hot spot, catches fleas that jump at the reflection. Place it near a nightlight for best results.
- **Bite log.** Note the date of any new bites. Bites that stop and then restart two weeks later mean a second wave of pupae has hatched, which is expected — not a failure.

The problem is over when no fleas appear on the socks and no new bites occur for thirty consecutive days. In a house with a long dormancy period behind it, plan on three months of light monitoring, with an IGR reapplication per label, before you can call it finished.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not use a flea fogger or "bomb."** It does not penetrate carpet, reaches no larvae, and is a fire and inhalation hazard. It is the classic reason a household repeats treatment five times and still has fleas.

**Do not apply a permethrin product to a cat.** This is the most serious hazard in this entire subject. Permethrin and similar pyrethroids are highly toxic to cats — a dog-formulated flea product applied to a cat can be fatal, and cats are also exposed by close contact with a treated dog. Read the species on the label every time. When in doubt, the pet's flea treatment is a question for a veterinarian.

**Do not treat the whole house when you have identified two hot spots.** Flea products are pesticides. Treating 1,200 square feet because of a bite on your ankle is unnecessary exposure for no added benefit.

**Do not skip the vacuuming because you applied a product.** The vacuum is what converts the protected pupal reservoir into killable adults. The product cannot reach what the vacuum has not exposed.

**Do not assume the fleas came from your pet.** If there is no pet, look for the animal that passed through — a raccoon, an opossum, a squirrel in the attic, or a neighbor's cat. If a rodent is the carrier, the fleas will keep coming until the entry is closed.

## When to Call a Professional

Flea control is one of the few household pest problems where professional treatment is genuinely more effective than DIY, for a specific reason: the equipment. Professionals use growth regulators and residual products applied with pressure and agitation that reaches into the carpet base, plus they can treat crawlspaces and wall voids that a homeowner cannot reach safely.

Consider calling when:

- The infestation persists after two full months of daily vacuuming plus a labeled IGR application.
- The source is a crawlspace, wall void, or attic that you cannot safely access.
- There is a confirmed wildlife infestation — raccoon, opossum, or squirrel — because the animal must be excluded first, and that may be regulated.
- Anyone in the household has a severe reaction to bites, or a pet has developed flea allergy dermatitis, which requires veterinary care alongside the environmental work.

## FAQ

**How can I have fleas with no pets at all?**

The most common routes are a previous occupant's pet, a neighborhood cat or dog that rests against the house, a rodent or raccoon, or a dormant pupal population left in the carpet from months earlier. The cat flea is the dominant household species and feeds on many mammals, so a single animal passing through can seed a house.

**Why did the fleas appear the day I moved in?**

Because the pupae were already there. A flea pupa in its cocoon can stay dormant for six months or more in an empty house, waiting for vibration, warmth, and carbon dioxide. Footsteps on the carpet provide all three at once, and the dormant cohort hatches within hours of occupancy.

**How long does it take to get rid of fleas without an exterminator?**

With daily vacuuming plus an insect growth regulator, most infestations are controlled within four to six weeks. Full resolution — no fleas on a white sock and no new bites for thirty days — typically takes two to three months, because pupae hatch in waves.

**Do flea bombs work?**

No, not against an established infestation. A fogger deposits a mist on surfaces, does not penetrate carpet pile, and does not reach larvae or pupae at the base of the pile. It kills the exposed adults, which are about 5 percent of the population, and then the cycle continues.

**Can fleas live in a house with no carpet?**

Yes, but the population is smaller and easier to control. Eggs fall into floorboard gaps, larvae develop in dust along baseboards, and adults use upholstered furniture and bedding. Hard floors reduce the reservoir substantially but do not eliminate it.

**Are flea bites dangerous?**

Fleas can transmit pathogens, but in a typical American household the practical problems are intense itching, secondary infection from scratching, and flea allergy dermatitis in pets. The bites are more of a nuisance than a disease risk in most cases, but they should not be ignored in a house with pets or young children.

**Will vacuuming alone get rid of them?**

Rarely, but it is the most important single step. Vacuuming removes a large share of the population and triggers dormant pupae to emerge, which exposes them. Combined with an IGR and hot washing of textiles, it is usually sufficient. Alone, it tends to control rather than eliminate.

## Bottom Line

Fleas in a pet-free house are not a mystery and not a new arrival. They are a dormant pupal population that was triggered by the first footsteps on the carpet, or a live population maintained by an animal you have not identified yet. Find the hot spots before you treat anything, convert the reservoir with daily vacuuming, wash every textile in hot water and a hot dryer, and apply an insect growth regulator to break the larval molt rather than chasing adults. Then monitor with white socks for thirty days and expect the second wave. The waves are the mechanism, not a sign of failure.
