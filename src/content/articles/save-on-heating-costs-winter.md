Heating is typically the single largest energy expense in a cold-climate home — often 40–50% of the annual energy bill. That makes it the highest-leverage place to look for savings, but also the place where folk advice is most misleading.

The core principle: **your heater is replacing heat that left the house.** Every dollar saved comes from either slowing that loss, or paying less for each unit of heat. Nothing else matters.

---

## Where the Heat Actually Goes

Typical heat loss distribution in an older, unimproved home:

| Location | Share of loss |
|---|---|
| **Walls** | 25–35% |
| **Windows** | 10–25% |
| **Roof / attic** | 15–25% |
| **Air leakage (all sources)** | 15–25% |
| **Floors** | 10–20% |

Two things follow. First, **air leakage is a major, cheaply-fixable slice** that no amount of glass upgrade will address. Second, **the attic matters as much or more than windows** — which is why the usual "buy new windows first" advice is usually wrong.

**These are averages, and your house may not match them.** A 1950s house with uninsulated walls is a different problem from a 2005 house with poor attic insulation, and spending on the wrong one wastes the budget. Two cheap ways to find out where your heat actually goes:

- **A thermal camera or an infrared thermometer** shows cold surfaces directly. On a cold day, the difference between an insulated and an uninsulated wall is obvious within seconds, and so is a cold band where insulation has slumped or was never installed.
- **The incense or smoke test** finds air leakage. Move a lit incense stick along skirting boards, window frames, and ceiling penetrations on a windy day and watch the smoke. It bends where air is moving, which tells you exactly which gaps are worth sealing rather than which ones merely exist.

An infrared thermometer costs less than a single replacement window and answers the question that determines how the rest of the budget should be spent.

Snow load, ice dams, and heat loss are the three failures — [preparing for a winter storm](/seasonal/seasons/prepare-home-for-winter-storm/) covers them.

## The Ranked List

### 1. Seal air leaks (highest return, lowest cost)

Air leakage is often 15–25% of total heat loss, and weatherstripping costs very little. Focus on:

- Attic hatch and whole-house fan
- Exterior door bottoms and frames
- Window frame corners and seams
- Electrical outlets and switch plates on exterior walls
- Plumbing and cable penetrations
- Recessed light fixtures

**Find them before you seal them.** Drafts travel, and the spot you feel is rarely where air enters. Full method in [Cheap Ways to Insulate Windows and Doors for Winter](/save-money/bills/cheap-window-insulation/).

### 2. Lower the thermostat, and lower it when you're away

Heating demand scales roughly with the temperature difference between inside and outside. Lowering the setpoint reduces that difference directly.

A commonly cited guideline: each degree of setback over an eight-hour period saves on the order of 1% of heating cost. Actual savings depend on climate, insulation, and how long the setback lasts.

Suggested settings:

| Period | Setting |
|---|---|
| Awake and home | 68°F (20°C) or lower as comfortable |
| Asleep or away | 55–62°F (13–17°C) |

### 3. Add attic insulation

Heat rises, and an under-insulated attic is one of the largest single sources of loss. Adding insulation is a bigger project than weatherstripping but has a strong return in cold climates — and it keeps paying back every year, unlike a setback that depends on daily behavior.

### 4. Use the sun deliberately

1. **Open curtains on south-facing windows** during the day.
2. **Close them at dusk** to trap the gained heat.
3. **Keep north-facing curtains closed** all day.

This costs nothing and works immediately.

### 5. Reverse ceiling fans to winter mode

1. **Turn the fan off** and let it stop.
2. **Flip the direction switch** on the motor housing.
3. **Run on low** — the blades should pull air up, pushing warm air off the ceiling and down the walls.

**If you feel a breeze, it's wrong.** Winter mode is about circulation, not cooling.

### 6. Close off unused rooms — with a caveat

Closing vents in unused rooms saves money, but:

- Do not close more than a small fraction of total vents — it can strain the system
- Rooms with plumbing should stay warm to protect pipes

### 7. Seal the fireplace damper

An open damper is a large hole in the ceiling of your thermal envelope. If the fireplace is unused, keep the damper closed and consider an inflatable chimney balloon.

Most tanks ship at 140°F, and 120°F is safe for an adult household — [setting the water heater temperature](/save-money/bills/water-heater-temperature-setting-savings/) covers the trade-offs.

## Why This Works

**Heat loss is proportional to temperature difference.** The driving force is ΔT between inside and outside. Reduce your setpoint or improve the barrier, and you reduce the flow.

**Air exchange is expensive.** Every cubic foot of warm air that leaks out is replaced by cold air you must heat from scratch. Sealing stops the exchange entirely — which is why it outperforms upgrades that only address conduction.

**Free heat is cheaper than purchased heat.** Solar gain through south-facing glass is genuinely free; the only requirement is timing the curtains correctly.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Cranking heat to warm up faster | Furnaces heat at a fixed rate — it just overshoots | Set the target and wait |
| Turning heat off entirely when away | Risk of frozen pipes; reheat costs more | Set back to 55–60°F |
| Blocking radiators or vents with furniture | Traps heat, reduces circulation | Keep clearance |
| Using the oven to heat the house | Carbon monoxide risk, fire hazard | Use a space heater safely |
| Closing most of the vents | Can damage the blower and cause coil freeze | Close only a few |
| Ignoring the furnace filter | Reduces airflow, increases run time | Check monthly in heating season |
| Heating rooms nobody uses | Straight waste | Close the door and set back |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does lowering the thermostat really save money?**
Yes. Heating demand scales with the inside-outside temperature difference, so a lower setpoint directly reduces the heat your system must supply.

**Is it cheaper to leave heat at a constant temperature?**
Generally no. Setbacks save money, though the amount depends on how long the setback lasts and how well insulated the home is. Very long recovery periods in poorly insulated homes reduce the benefit.

**What temperature should I set in winter?**
Around 68°F while awake and home, and 55–62°F while asleep or away — adjusted to your comfort and health needs.

**Should I close vents in unused rooms?**
Only a few. Closing too many creates pressure problems that can damage the blower or freeze the coil.

**Do ceiling fans help in winter?**
Yes, when reversed to low speed. They push warm air off the ceiling back into the living space. If you feel a draft, the direction is wrong.

**Does a fireplace actually heat a house?**
Usually the opposite. Most of the heat goes up the chimney, and the fire draws additional warm room air with it.

**What is the single best heating investment?**
Air sealing, followed by attic insulation. Both reduce the load permanently; window replacement generally has a much slower payback.
