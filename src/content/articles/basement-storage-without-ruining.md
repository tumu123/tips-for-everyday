Basements earn their reputation from floods, but floods are not what ruins most stored belongings. Water that arrives in a visible inch is easy to understand and easy to claim for. What actually destroys things down there is a slow process with three separate mechanisms — vapour, wicking, and condensation — and all three are invisible until a box collapses or a coat comes out smelling of the floor.

The good news is that the countermeasures are physical and cheap: height, air, and the right container. The order matters, because a sealed bin sitting on a damp floor is worse than an open crate on a shelf.

## What Actually Ruins Things Down There

Three mechanisms, and they are often confused with each other.

**Bulk water** is the obvious one: a leak, a backed-up drain, a rising water table after heavy rain. It happens rarely and it is the only one people plan for.

**Water vapour** is the constant. Concrete and block walls are porous, and the ground outside is permanently damp in most climates. Vapour moves through the wall into the basement all year, and it does not need a crack to do it. This is why a basement can feel damp with no leak anywhere.

**Condensation** is the one that catches people out. Warm air holds more moisture than cool air. When humid summer air reaches a cool basement wall, floor, or pipe, it drops below its dew point and the excess water comes out of the air onto the surface. The floor slab is the coldest thing in most basements because it sits against the ground, which is why the bottom of a stored box gets soft while the top is fine.

Add two multipliers: cardboard is a sponge, and mould needs roughly 70 percent relative humidity at a surface to grow. A cardboard box on a concrete floor can sit above that threshold for months without anyone noticing.

## Step 0: Measure before you store anything

Two measurements and one photograph, all before the first box goes down.

1. **Relative humidity**, with a cheap hygrometer left in place for a day. Under 50 percent is comfortable for storage; 50 to 60 percent is workable with vigilance; above 60 percent means the room needs a dehumidifier before it needs shelving.
2. **The high-water mark**, if there is one. Look for tide lines on the wall, staining at the base of the furnace, or a water stain on a joist. If the floor has flooded before, it will flood again, and the plan has to start above that line.
3. **Photograph the empty space**, including where the pipes, meters, and shut-offs are. It saves a search later and it shows how much shelving actually fits.

While you are down there, look for active moisture sources — efflorescence (the white powder on masonry), rust on the bottom of metal legs, a musty smell concentrated in one corner. [Finding moisture problems early](/fix-it/maintenance/find-moisture-problems-early/) covers how to trace those to a cause instead of just dehumidifying around them.

## Step 1: Nothing sits on the floor

This is the rule that prevents most loss, and it has a number attached: **four to six inches of clearance.** That is enough to survive a minor water event, enough for air to move under the item, and enough that condensation on the slab does not reach the contents.

What works as a base:

| Base | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Plastic pallets | Low | Best option; does not absorb water, stacks, easy to clean |
| Metal shelving with legs | Moderate | Buy units with at least 4-inch legs, not flat-bottomed cabinets |
| Pressure-treated timber runners | Low | Adequate; check for rot annually |
| Breeze blocks and a board | Very low | Fine for one or two heavy items, not a system |
| Cardboard on the floor | Free | This is the failure case, not a base |

Never stack boxes directly on the slab, even briefly, and never store anything against a wall at floor level.

## Step 2: Build shelf tiers by risk

Storage in a basement is not a two-dimensional problem. Height is a risk gradient: the closer to the floor, the wetter the conditions, and the higher up, the drier and warmer.

| Height | Conditions | What goes there |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 6 inches | Flood path, condensation zone | Nothing — leave it empty, or only plastic crates of tools |
| 6 to 18 inches | Damp, splash risk | Heavy durable items: tools, paint cans, garden equipment, bottled water |
| 18 inches to 5 feet | Best zone, easiest access | Anything that matters: documents in bins, textiles, electronics, seasonal decor |
| Above 5 feet | Driest, hardest to reach | Light, rarely used, nothing heavy, nothing that needs lifting down |

The instinct is to put heavy things low and valuable things high, which is right — but the lowest shelf is a bad place for anything, including heavy things, if it is within reach of water. A metal shelf whose bottom shelf starts at 6 inches solves both problems at once.

Two structural rules: bolt tall shelving to the wall, and never store anything within 18 inches of the furnace, water heater, or electrical panel.

## Step 3: Keep air moving behind and around everything

Concrete walls stay close to ground temperature year-round, which makes them a condensation surface in summer. A box pressed against the wall wicks that moisture directly.

- Leave **2 inches** between shelving and walls, and never stack boxes against the wall itself.
- Keep a clear path down the middle of the room so air can move and so you can see a leak when it starts.
- Do not seal the room. Basements need some air exchange with the rest of the house; a closed, unventilated basement in summer is a humidity trap.

If the rim joist area (where the wood frame meets the foundation) is uninsulated and open, that is where warm humid air meets cold wood, and it is the most common place to find mould on stored items near the ceiling. Insulating and sealing that band is a bigger job, but it is the correct fix rather than a bigger dehumidifier.

## Step 4: Match the container to the contents

The container question has a right answer per material, and the answers differ.

| Contents | Container | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Paper, photos, documents | Sealed plastic bin plus desiccant | Paper absorbs moisture and stains permanently; pests eat it |
| Clothing, bedding, textiles | Sealed plastic bin, contents bone dry | Blocks damp and insects; must go in dry, not "almost dry" |
| Leather, shoes, wood furniture | Breathable cover, not sealed | Leather and wood need to release moisture; sealing damp leather grows mould |
| Electronics | Sealed bin, remove batteries, add desiccant | Corrosion is the killer, not water directly |
| Metal tools | Open crate or oiled and covered | Trapped moisture rusts faster than moving air |
| Christmas lights and decor | Labelled bins, sorted by room | See the decoration-specific method below |
| Mattresses and upholstery | Upright, wrapped, off the floor | Flat storage compresses and traps moisture underneath |

Three details that make the difference between a bin that protects and a bin that incubates:

- **Dry the contents first.** A bin seals in whatever moisture went in with the item. Anything that has been in a damp room for years needs to dry before it is sealed.
- **Use desiccant deliberately.** Silica gel packs are cheap and they work in a closed bin. They saturate, so replace or recharge them annually.
- **Do not use vacuum bags for everything.** They are excellent against moisture and insects, but long-term compression ruins pillows, down bedding, and anything with structure. They also hide damp: a sealed bag with a slightly damp jumper will grow mould quietly.

Cardboard boxes are the default and the worst option for a basement floor, but they are fine **on a shelf, above 18 inches, for items that are not moisture-sensitive**, provided they are not touching a wall. For anything valuable, plastic is not optional.

## Step 5: Control the air, not just the boxes

A dehumidifier is the single most effective tool in a basement, and most are set up in a way that wastes it.

- **Set it to 50 to 55 percent relative humidity**, not to its lowest setting. Lower than that costs a lot of electricity for no storage benefit and makes the space unpleasant.
- **Use a continuous drain.** A unit that fills a tank and switches off is a unit that stops working whenever you forget it. Run the hose to a floor drain or a condensate pump.
- **Keep it running in summer, not just when it feels damp.** The humid months are when the damage happens, and humidity is not perceptible at the level that ruins paper.
- **Do not ventilate in humid weather.** Opening basement windows on a warm, muggy day lets in more moisture than it removes. Ventilate when outdoor air is drier than indoor air — typically cool, dry autumn days.
- **Seal obvious air leaks** at the top of the foundation wall and around penetrations, so humid outdoor air has fewer ways in.

A cheap hygrometer next to the shelving tells you whether any of this is working. Without one, the dehumidifier setting is a guess.

## Step 6: Handle cold surfaces and temperature swings

Condensation forms on the coldest surface, not the dampest one. In a basement that is usually the floor slab, a metal shelf leg, or a cold-water pipe running across stored goods.

- **Insulate cold pipes** that run above storage; a dripping pipe over a box is a slow disaster.
- **Keep storage out of the direct line of any pipe**, and never directly under a bathroom or kitchen.
- **Watch the spring transition.** A cold basement and a warm, humid first warm week is the worst combination of the year: the walls and floor are still cold, the air is suddenly wet, and everything sweats. That is the week to check the dehumidifier, not July.

Freezing is the other seasonal risk, and it matters for liquids, paint, and anything with water in it. If the basement is unheated and can drop below freezing, [winter pipe precautions](/fix-it/maintenance/prevent-frozen-pipes/) apply to the stored paint tins as much as to the plumbing.

## Step 7: Pest-proof without poison

Basements are attractive to insects, mice, and occasionally worse, because they are dark, quiet, and full of nesting material.

- **Store textiles in sealed bins, not in bags on the floor.** This alone removes most rodent interest.
- **No cardboard on the floor, no paper bags, no open boxes of rags.**
- **Keep a 12-inch gap between storage and the wall** where practical, so there is no hidden runway.
- **Avoid baits inside the storage area.** Poison in a basement is a hazard to pets and children and does not fix the entry point. Seal gaps around pipes and the sill plate instead.
- **Check annually for droppings** behind and under shelves. If you find them, the entry point is the problem, not the shelves.

Cardboard is both shelter and food for several species. For the one that eats the paper itself, and the humidity rule behind it, see [what silverfish eat](/pest-control/insects/silverfish-what-they-eat/).

Boxes and bins are chosen partly by what can get into them. For the insect that gets into stored wool, and the inspection route that finds it, see [pest-proofing stored textiles without poison](/pest-control/insects/carpet-beetles-in-house/).

## Step 8: Label facing out, inventory on the door

A basement fails at retrieval, not at storage. When everything is in identical grey bins, the system is only as good as its labels.

- **Label the end of the bin that faces the aisle**, not the lid. You should be able to read every bin from the middle of the room.
- **Write the contents and the year.** "Winter coats — 2026" tells you whether the bin is worth opening.
- **Keep a one-page inventory on the back of the door**, listing bin numbers and rough contents. This is what stops the "it's somewhere in the basement" search.
- **Group by destination, not by category.** All the Christmas items in one zone, all the camping gear in another, all the archives in a third. Category systems collapse when a new item does not fit an existing heading.

## Step 9: Inspect twice a year

Ten minutes in spring and ten in autumn, on the same day as the smoke alarm check.

Look at: the bottom edge of every cardboard box, the wall behind the shelves, the floor under the lowest shelf, and the dehumidifier drain. Run a hand along a shelf leg — rust at the base means water has been there.

Anything that smells musty, has a soft corner, or shows a tide line gets opened immediately. Mould on stored goods is a surface problem if it is caught early and a replacement problem if it is not.

## What Never Goes in a Basement

Some categories are simply the wrong contents for the space, regardless of shelving.

- **Original documents** — deeds, certificates, birth certificates, tax records. Damp and pests are both fatal; these belong in a fireproof box upstairs.
- **Photographs and negatives**, printed. The emulsion layer is favourite mould food. Digital copies plus originals in a living space.
- **Musical instruments**, especially guitars and pianos. Wood moves with humidity, and a basement is the worst possible environment for it.
- **Paint and solvents** if the room can freeze, and anything flammable near the furnace.
- **Propane cylinders**, anywhere inside the house.
- **Mattresses stored flat on the floor** — they wick, compress, and grow mould underneath.
- **Electronics with batteries left in them.**
- **Anything you would be upset to lose and have not backed up.** A basement is a place for things you could replace.

Some storage decisions are about animals rather than damage. For the spider question specifically, and how to tell it from something that is not a spider, see [the case for leaving basement spiders alone](/pest-control/insects/spiders-in-the-basement/).

## FAQ

**What humidity level should a basement be kept at for storage?**

50 to 55 percent relative humidity. Below 50 is fine but costs more to maintain; above 60 puts paper, textiles, and wood at risk, and above 70 allows mould growth on surfaces. Measure it rather than judging by feel — a basement at 65 percent does not feel damp.

**Can I store cardboard boxes in a basement?**

Yes, on shelving at least 18 inches above the floor, off the walls, for items that are not moisture-sensitive. No, if the boxes are on the floor or against a wall, or if the contents are documents, photos, or textiles. Plastic bins cost little more and remove the risk entirely.

**How high should basement shelving be off the floor?**

Six inches minimum for the lowest shelf, 18 inches for anything that matters. A shelf that starts at floor level is a shelf that will eventually hold water.

**Why do my stored boxes get damp on the bottom but not the top?**

Condensation on the cold floor slab. The floor stays close to ground temperature, so moisture in the air condenses there first. Raising the box four to six inches solves it, provided the room's humidity is also being controlled.

**Is it better to ventilate a basement or dehumidify it?**

Dehumidify in summer and ventilate in cool, dry weather. Ventilating on a warm humid day adds moisture. The two are not alternatives; a basement with no air sealing and no dehumidifier cannot be fixed by opening windows.

**How do I stop mould on things already in storage?**

First remove the source — reduce humidity to under 55 percent before cleaning anything. Then treat the items: wash hard surfaces, discard porous items with visible growth, and dry everything thoroughly before it goes back. Mould returns within weeks if the humidity is unchanged.

**Do silica gel packs actually work in storage bins?**

Yes, in a bin that is genuinely closed. They absorb a limited amount, so replace or dry them annually, and never rely on them instead of controlling the room's humidity.

## Bottom Line

Basement storage is a height problem before it is a container problem. Keep everything four to six inches off the floor and two inches off the walls, put the things that matter between 18 inches and five feet, seal paper and textiles in dry bins with desiccant, and hold the room at 50 to 55 percent humidity with a dehumidifier on a continuous drain. Then label the aisle-facing end of every bin and walk the room twice a year. None of that stops a flood, but it keeps the slow damage — the kind that has already been happening for three years before anyone opens the box — from starting in the first place.
