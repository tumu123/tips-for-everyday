Leather is often treated as a durable version of fabric, which leads to two opposite mistakes: scrubbing it like upholstery, and assuming it needs no care at all. Both damage it, and the damage is usually irreversible.

The reason is that leather is **skin** — a protein matrix that has been tanned, and in most furniture, finished with a surface coating. It has a narrow band of acceptable moisture. Too little and it dries, stiffens, and cracks along the flex lines. Too much, or the wrong chemistry, and it strips the oils that keep it supple.

**The single most important step is identifying what kind of leather you have**, because the correct method differs completely.

---

## 1. Identify Your Leather Type First

Test this before applying anything. The wrong product on the wrong leather is how people ruin furniture.

**Protected (pigmented / finished) leather** — the most common on affordable-to-mid sofas.

- Surface has a visible coating, often with a slight sheen
- Water droplets bead on the surface rather than soaking in
- Usually a uniform colour, sometimes with a printed grain
- **A drop of water placed on an inconspicuous area will sit on top for a minute or more**
- Care: forgiving. Damp cloth, mild soap, and a conditioner.

**Aniline / semi-aniline (unprotected) leather** — high-end, soft, natural-looking.

- No surface coating; you are touching the actual hide
- Water darkens it immediately and soaks in
- Colour varies naturally across the piece; visible pores and healed scars
- **A water drop darkens the spot within seconds**
- Care: **water is a risk, not a tool.** Use purpose-made aniline cleaner, blot rather than wipe, and never scrub.

**Suede and nubuck** — brushed surface, velvety nap.

- Different category entirely, closer to fabric care
- Water leaves marks; brushing restores the nap
- Care: suede brush and eraser, dedicated products, no conditioner

**Bonded leather / "leather match"** — reconstituted leather fibres bonded to a backing, often with a polyurethane top coat.

- Peels and flakes rather than cracking
- Cannot be conditioned in any meaningful way
- Care: gentle cleaning only; the failure mode is delamination, and nothing reverses it

**Split leather** — the lower layer of the hide, often finished with a heavy coating. Behaves like protected leather but is less durable.

If you are unsure, assume the least forgiving option and test on the underside of a cushion or the back of the frame. This matters most if you are buying second-hand, where you cannot know what has already been applied — see [buying used furniture safely](/save-money/shopping/buy-used-furniture-safely/) for what to check before you commit.

## 2. Dust Before You Wipe

Dust is abrasive, and wiping it across leather drags fine grit over the finish.

- **Vacuum** with a soft brush attachment, or use a dry microfibre cloth.
- **Pay attention to the seams and the creases** at armrests and seat cushions, where crumbs and grit collect and then grind.
- **Lift the cushions** and vacuum underneath, including the frame edge where dust accumulates.

Do this weekly. It is the cheapest thing you can do for leather, because most wear comes from abrasion rather than from dirt.

---

## 3. Clean With the Mildest Thing That Works

**For protected leather:**

1. Mix a few drops of **mild, pH-neutral soap** in warm water. Dish soap is acceptable if it is a gentle one and heavily diluted; saddle soap is traditional and works well.
2. Dampen a microfibre cloth and **wring it out thoroughly**. The cloth should feel barely damp.
3. Wipe in **small circles with light pressure**, working one section at a time.
4. **Dry immediately** with a second cloth. Do not leave the surface wet.
5. Rinse the cloth often; a cloth loaded with soap and soil just redistributes both.

**For aniline leather:** use a cleaner made for aniline leather. Blot rather than wipe. Do not use soap and water, which will leave a tide mark.

**Do not use:**

- **Household spray cleaners or all-purpose cleaners** — surfactants and solvents strip the finish
- **Alcohol, acetone, or nail polish remover** — dissolve the surface coating
- **Baby wipes** — most contain alcohol, fragrance, or both
- **Vinegar** — acidic, and it dulls the finish
- **Bleach, ammonia, or "magic eraser" sponges** — remove the top layer
- **Hand cream, olive oil, or coconut oil as conditioner** — these are food oils, and they go rancid, attract dirt, and darken the leather unevenly

## 4. Handle Water Marks Properly

Water marks on leather are usually **tide lines**: water has dissolved something in the leather or the finish and deposited it at the edge of the wet area as it dried. This is why the mark is a ring rather than a patch.

**The counterintuitive fix:** do not treat the mark locally. Local treatment creates a new tide line at the boundary of your new wet area.

**For protected leather:**

1. Dampen a cloth evenly and wipe the **whole panel** — the entire seat cushion, or the full arm — so the moisture is uniform.
2. Dry it evenly, away from direct heat.
3. Let it dry completely before judging the result; leather darkens while damp and lightens as it dries.

**For aniline leather**, water marks are much harder. The correct approach is a dedicated aniline cleaner applied evenly across the panel, or professional help. Do not experiment on a visible surface.

**Do not use a hairdryer or place the piece in direct sun.** Rapid drying is what causes the leather to shrink unevenly and crack, and it can set the tide line permanently.

## 5. Deal With Body Oil and Dark Patches

The dark, slightly shiny areas on armrests, headrests, and seat fronts are **accumulated body oil**, not dirt. They appear where skin and hair contact the leather repeatedly.

This is worth understanding because the fix is different from general cleaning:

- Body oil is what actually degrades leather over time. It oxidises, becomes tacky, attracts dust, and gradually softens and weakens the finish.
- **Warm water and a mild soap** remove it better than cold, because warmth helps the oil emulsify.
- Work in small sections and **rinse the cloth frequently** — you are removing oil, and a loaded cloth puts it back.
- For heavy buildup, a **dedicated leather degreaser** used sparingly is more effective than repeated soap passes.

**The balancing act:** you want to remove the oil without stripping the leather's own conditioning. That is why you clean and then condition, rather than cleaning aggressively and stopping there.

---

## 6. Condition — Less Often Than You Think

Conditioning replaces oils lost to cleaning and to time. It is the step that prevents cracking, and it is also the step most often overdone.

**How often:** every six to twelve months for most furniture, and more often in very dry climates or with heating that runs constantly. If the leather feels supple and looks even, it does not need more.

**How to apply:**

1. Clean first. Conditioning over dirt seals the dirt in.
2. Apply a **small amount** of leather conditioner to a soft cloth.
3. Work it in with light circular motions, section by section.
4. **Buff off the excess** after ten to fifteen minutes with a dry cloth. Leather can only absorb so much; the rest sits on the surface and attracts dust.
5. Test on a hidden area first if the product is new to you.

**Signs of over-conditioning:** a tacky feel, a dull or uneven sheen, and dust that sticks. If that happens, stop conditioning and wipe with a barely damp cloth.

**Do not** use products containing petroleum distillates, silicone, or wax on aniline leather. They can darken it permanently and clog the pores.

## 7. Prevent the Damage You Cannot Undo

Cracking along the flex lines is the failure mode that ends a leather sofa's life, and it is mostly preventable.

- **Keep it out of direct sun.** UV dries and fades leather. Move the piece, or use a blind during peak hours.
- **Keep it away from heat sources** — radiators, fireplaces, and heating vents blowing directly on it.
- **Maintain indoor humidity** in winter. Dry heated air is the main cause of cracking in cold climates; a humidifier does more for leather than any conditioner.
- **Rotate cushions** if they are reversible, so wear is distributed.
- **Address scratches early.** Most surface scratches on protected leather can be blended with a fingertip warmed by body heat, or touched up with a leather pen matched to the colour.
- **Do not sit on the arms.** The arm is usually a single layer over a frame edge, and it is where cracking starts.

## Why This Works

Leather behaves the way it does because it is a collagen matrix with a finish on top, and every cleaning decision either preserves or compromises that structure.

The narrow moisture window is the key constraint. Leather contains oils that keep the collagen fibres able to slide past one another, which is what makes it supple. Water alone does not remove those oils, but soap does, and repeated soap cleaning gradually strips them. That is why cleaning must be followed by conditioning — the two steps are a pair, and doing only the first is how leather becomes stiff and eventually cracks.

The tide-line behaviour follows from how water moves through leather. Water dissolves soluble material in the finish and the upper layers, then migrates outward as it evaporates, carrying that material to the drying edge. The result is a ring with a concentrated border. Wetting the whole panel prevents a border from forming, because there is no dry edge for the material to accumulate against.

The body-oil point explains a pattern that looks like dirt but is not. Human sebum is chemically similar to the fats used in leather conditioning, so it penetrates rather than sitting on the surface. It also oxidises, which is why old contact areas go tacky and dark rather than simply grubby. Warm water and a surfactant remove it; cold water alone does not, because the oil is semi-solid at room temperature.

The over-conditioning caution is about saturation. Leather absorbs a finite amount, and product that cannot be absorbed remains on the surface as a film that traps dust and holds it against the finish. Buffing the excess is not cosmetic — it is what keeps the surface from becoming a dust magnet.

Finally, humidity explains why the same sofa survives in one home and cracks in another. Cracking is a moisture-loss failure, and central heating produces very dry indoor air. A humidifier addresses the cause; conditioner addresses the symptom. Neither is sufficient alone in a dry climate.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Using all-purpose spray cleaner | Surfactants and solvents strip the finish. Use a pH-neutral leather or mild soap solution. |
| Wiping with alcohol, acetone, or baby wipes | Dissolve the surface coating. Most baby wipes contain alcohol or fragrance. |
| Treating a water mark locally | Creates a new tide line at the boundary. Wet the whole panel evenly instead. |
| Drying with a hairdryer or in direct sun | Causes uneven shrinkage and cracking, and can set the mark permanently. |
| Scrubbing with a melamine sponge | Removes the top layer of finish. The damage is permanent. |
| Using olive oil, coconut oil, or hand cream as conditioner | Food oils go rancid, attract dirt, and darken unevenly. Use a leather conditioner. |
| Conditioning over dirt | Seals the dirt into the surface. Always clean first. |
| Applying conditioner heavily and leaving it | Leather absorbs only so much. Excess stays on the surface and attracts dust. Buff it off. |
| Using water on aniline leather | Unprotected leather absorbs it and marks. Use an aniline-specific cleaner and blot. |
| Assuming all leather is the same | Protected, aniline, suede and bonded leather need completely different methods. |
| Ignoring dry indoor air in winter | Low humidity is the main cause of cracking. A humidifier does more than any product. |

## FAQ

**How do I know what kind of leather I have?**
Put a drop of water on an inconspicuous spot. If it beads and sits on top, the leather is protected. If it darkens and soaks in within seconds, it is aniline or another unprotected type and needs different care.

**Can I clean leather with soap and water?**
On protected leather, yes — heavily diluted mild soap on a barely damp cloth, then dry immediately. On aniline or suede, no.

**How do I remove water stains from a leather sofa?**
For protected leather, dampen a cloth and wipe the entire panel evenly so there is no dry edge, then dry away from heat. Local treatment makes it worse.

**How often should leather be conditioned?**
Every six to twelve months for most furniture. More often in dry climates or with constant heating. If it feels supple and looks even, it does not need more.

**Why do the armrests look darker and shinier?**
Accumulated body oil, not dirt. Warm water with a mild soap removes it; a dedicated leather degreaser helps with heavy buildup. Then condition.

**Is vinegar safe on leather?**
Not recommended. It is acidic and dulls the finish. Use a pH-neutral cleaner.

**Can I use baby wipes on a leather couch?**
No. Most contain alcohol or fragrance, both of which degrade the finish with repeated use.

**How do I stop leather from cracking?**
Keep it out of direct sun and away from heat sources, maintain indoor humidity in winter, rotate cushions, and do not sit on the arms. Cracking is mostly a moisture-loss failure.

**What is bonded leather and can it be repaired?**
Reconstituted leather fibre with a coating. It peels rather than cracks, and delamination cannot be reversed. Gentle cleaning is the only care available.

**Can scratches be fixed?**
Surface scratches on protected leather often blend with warmth from a fingertip. Deeper ones can be touched up with a colour-matched leather pen. Tears and punctures need professional repair.
