They are about a millimeter long, pale grey or white, and they appear in a cluster — a hundred of them on the bathroom floor, or a seething patch on the soil of a houseplant, or a scatter across the bottom of a kitchen sink. Touch one and it vanishes upward, having flicked itself with a tail-like appendage folded under its body. That jump is the entire basis of the name.

Springtails are the pest that most reliably gets misidentified, and the misidentification leads to the wrong treatment. They are not fleas, they do not bite, they do not infest food, they do not damage fabric or wood, and they do not survive in dry air. Their presence in a house is a moisture reading for one specific location, and the correct response is to find that location rather than to spray.

This is the clearest case in the whole house of a pest that treatment cannot fix. The general principle is in [the DIY pest control method](/save-money/bills/save-money-on-pest-control-diy/); springtails are the extreme version of it, because a springtail population is not an infestation at all — it is an indicator.

## First, Confirm What You Are Looking At

Three small jumping or swarming things get confused. The differences are easy to see with a phone camera in macro mode.

| | Springtails | Fleas | Termite swarmers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size | 1–3 mm, uniform | 2–3 mm, flattened side to side | 8–10 mm with wings |
| Body shape | Elongated, soft, no wings | Laterally flattened, hard shell | Straight-sided, four equal wings |
| Movement | Flicks upward, lands randomly, jumps repeatedly | Jumps onto a host, then runs | Flies clumsily, sheds wings |
| Where | Wet surfaces, soil, drains | Carpet, bedding, near a host | Near a window or light, after rain |
| Bites | Never | Yes, ankles and lower legs | Never |

Springtails are also not insects in the strict sense: they belong to the class Collembola, a group of six-legged arthropods that are now classified outside Insecta. That taxonomic detail has a practical consequence — they breathe through their cuticle and have no waxy layer to retain water. They dehydrate in hours if the air around them drops much below saturation.

For the flea comparison in full, including the bite pattern and the paper-towel test for flea dirt, see [fleas in a house with no pets](/pest-control/insects/fleas-without-pets/). If there are bites, it is not springtails.

## The Single Variable: Moisture

Springtails need relative humidity near saturation — in practice, above 90 percent in the microclimate right where they are sitting. This is not a preference. It is a hard requirement of their body plan, and it explains every fact about where you find them.

- On the floor around a shower or tub, they are living in the damp film along the caulk line.
- In a kitchen sink or a bathroom basin, they are feeding on the biofilm in the overflow and the trap.
- On houseplant soil, they are feeding on algae and fungus growing because the potting mix is kept wet.
- Along a basement wall, they are in a persistently damp band where condensation or seepage keeps the concrete wet.
- On a newly poured slab or new drywall, they are feeding on the mold that grows in the construction moisture, and they disappear once the building dries.

Their food is fungus, algae, mold spores, and decaying organic matter. That is worth pausing on: a springtail population is usually sitting on a patch of microbial growth you have not noticed. If you find springtails, look closely at the surface underneath them with a flashlight at a low angle. There is very often a dark or greenish film there.

## Step 1: Map Where They Appear and When

Before touching anything, spend three days writing it down. The pattern narrows the cause faster than any inspection.

- **Location.** One spot, or scattered? A single spot points to a local water source. Scattered across a whole basement points to ambient humidity.
- **Time of day.** Springtails are active at night and retreat when the air dries. If they appear only in the morning, the surface is drying during the day and rewetting at night — usually condensation or a slow leak.
- **Weather.** If they appear after rain, the source is exterior water entering. If they appear in humid summer weather only, the source is condensation on cold surfaces.
- **Recent changes.** New flooring, a new bathroom, a new houseplant, a new roof, a season change, or a change in how often a bathroom is used. A springtail bloom often follows a specific event.

A single location with a clear weather correlation is a five-minute diagnosis. A scattered population with no pattern means the whole space is too humid, and the fix is a dehumidifier.

## Step 2: Find the Water

At each marked location, work through the four sources in order. Each has a different fix.

**Condensation.** Cold surfaces in humid air collect water. A toilet tank, a cold-water pipe, a basement wall in summer, the underside of a metal sink. Test by taping a square of plastic film to the surface for 24 hours: moisture on the outside-facing side of the film means condensation; moisture trapped under the film means the material itself is wet and the water is coming through. The fix for condensation is reducing indoor humidity and insulating the cold surface, not sealing the wall.

**Plumbing leaks.** A trap that weeps at the slip joint, a supply line with a slow drip, a shower valve that seeps behind the wall, a toilet that seeps at the base. Look for a tide mark or a mineral stain on the cabinet floor or the wall behind the fixture. Springtails in a bathroom that appears dry are frequently a leak behind the tub or under the floor.

**Exterior water.** A downspout discharging against the foundation, soil sloping toward the house, a gutter overflowing at a corner, a basement window well that fills. Any of these produces a permanently wet band of masonry on the inside face. The fix is drainage, and the diagnostic sequence for finding it is in [how to find moisture problems early](/fix-it/maintenance/find-moisture-problems-early/).

**Organic growth that holds water.** A layer of biofilm in a drain, a mat of algae on a shower floor, a wet bath mat that never dries, a pile of damp cardboard in a basement. These are both the water source and the food source. Removing them removes both.

## Step 3: Dry the Space

Once you know where the water is, drying is mechanical, not chemical.

- **A dehumidifier set to 45–50 percent**, plumbed to a drain so it does not stop when the tank fills. Run it continuously for at least two weeks, then as needed. In a basement, this is often the whole solution.
- **Ventilation.** Run the bathroom exhaust fan during and for twenty minutes after every shower. Confirm the fan moves air — a tissue held to the grille should stay. A fan that hums without moving air is a placebo.
- **Wipe down wet surfaces** after use for two weeks. Bathroom floors, the shower surround, the kitchen sink deck. This is tedious and it shortens the timeline considerably.
- **Remove the wet substrate.** A bath mat that stays damp, a cardboard box on a basement floor, a stack of newspapers under a sink. These are the actual habitat.
- **Open the airflow.** Move furniture off the damp wall, leave the bathroom door open after use, and keep storage a few inches off a basement floor.

A springtail population in a room that stays below 50 percent relative humidity will be gone in days, without any pesticide. This is not an exaggeration of the mechanism — they simply cannot hold water.

## Step 4: Deal With the Growth They Are Eating

Springtails are eating something. Killing the springtails without removing the film leaves the food and the moisture, and they return.

Clean the surface properly rather than wiping it:

- **Bathroom caulk and grout.** Remove failed caulk rather than cleaning over it, treat the joint with a mildew cleaner, let it dry completely, and recaulk. The sequence, including why caulk fails, is in [how to remove mold from bathroom caulk](/home-hacks/stains/remove-mold-bathroom-caulk/).
- **Drain biofilm.** Brush the drain body and the overflow, not just the visible opening. A bottle brush and hot water do more than any drain chemical.
- **Slab and basement walls.** A dilute bleach or quaternary solution removes the film, but only after the water source is fixed. Otherwise the film returns within weeks.
- **Under appliances.** Pull the washer, the dryer, and the refrigerator out and clean and dry the floor beneath.

## Step 5: The Houseplant Case

Indoor plants are the most common single source of springtails in a dry, well-kept home, and the fix is counterintuitive.

Springtails in potting soil are harmless to the plant — they feed on fungus and algae, not roots. They are there because the mix is staying wet, which is often already a problem for the plant itself. Overwatering, a pot without drainage, a saucer that holds water, or a dense mix that stays soggy are all causes.

- **Let the top inch of soil dry between waterings.** This alone usually ends it.
- **Empty the saucer** an hour after watering. A saucer full of standing water is a permanent reservoir.
- **Top-dress the soil with a half-inch of coarse sand or gravel.** It dries fast and removes the damp surface the springtails need.
- **Repot plants in a mix with more perlite** if the soil stays wet for days after watering.
- **Check that every pot has a drainage hole.** A pot without one is a swamp.

You do not need to replace the soil. Drying the surface is sufficient, and the springtails in the lower profile will die as the moisture front recedes.

## Step 6: New Construction and New Flooring

If the house is new, or a bathroom or basement was recently tiled or replastered, a springtail bloom is expected and temporary.

Concrete cures for months and releases water vapor the whole time. Fresh thixotropic tile adhesive, fresh grout, and fresh drywall compound all hold water. The result is a period of elevated humidity in the assembly, and springtails find it. They will disappear on their own once the building dries, typically within a few weeks to a few months depending on the season and the ventilation.

What helps in the interim is the same as everywhere else: ventilation, a dehumidifier, and patience. What does not help is pesticide treatment, which neither dries the slab nor removes the mold the springtails are feeding on.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not treat for fleas.** Springtails do not bite, and a flea product applied to a floor where there are no fleas is unnecessary exposure. Confirm with the table above before assuming you have a biting pest.

**Do not use a fogger.** It does not reach the moisture the springtails need, and the population returns as soon as the mist settles.

**Do not spray the visible cluster.** Killing the animals on the floor leaves the wet patch, the biofilm, and the source. The cluster is a symptom.

**Do not seal a basement wall to stop springtails** before finding out whether the water is coming through the wall. Sealing the inside face of a wall that is wet from outside traps the water in the masonry and produces spalling and rot. Fix the drainage first.

**Do not ignore a large population in a wall or floor cavity.** A bloom that is inside the building envelope, not on a surface, usually means water is entering somewhere you cannot see. That is worth investigating rather than cleaning up.

## When to Call Someone

Springtails are almost never a reason to call an exterminator, because no pesticide addresses the cause. Call a different trade instead:

- **A moisture or waterproofing contractor** if the wet band is a basement wall and the cause is exterior drainage or a failed footing drain.
- **A plumber** if the pattern suggests a leak behind a wall or under a slab and you have ruled out the visible fixtures.
- **A roofer or gutter contractor** if the wet spot tracks to an overflowing gutter or a roof leak.
- **A mold assessor** if the growth under the springtails covers more than about ten square feet, or if anyone in the house has asthma or a compromised immune system.

The one situation where a pest professional is useful is when you cannot find the water and need help narrowing it, but the work they will recommend is still moisture control.

## FAQ

**Are springtails harmful?**

No. They do not bite, sting, transmit disease, damage plants, or eat stored food, fabric, or wood. They are harmless to people and pets. The concern they represent is the moisture condition that supports them, not the animal itself.

**How do I tell springtails from fleas?**

Springtails are smaller, softer-bodied, and pale; fleas are flattened, hard-shelled, and dark. Springtails jump repeatedly and land randomly, while fleas jump onto a host and then run. Fleas bite, usually on the ankles and lower legs; springtails never bite. If there are bites, it is not springtails.

**Why are there hundreds of them all at once?**

Springtails reproduce fast in a wet, food-rich microclimate and die fast when it dries. A population builds to visible numbers in a small area, then collapses. Seeing hundreds at once is normal for a localized wet spot and does not indicate a building-wide infestation.

**Do springtails come up through drains?**

They can appear in sinks and floor drains, because the trap and the overflow stay wet and grow biofilm. They are feeding on the film rather than emerging from the sewer. Cleaning the drain body and the overflow ends it.

**Will bug spray get rid of springtails?**

Temporarily, and only the ones present. Spraying does not change the humidity, remove the biofilm, or fix the leak, so the population returns within days to weeks. Drying the space is the only durable fix, and it works without any product.

**Are springtails a sign of mold?**

Often, yes. They feed on mold, algae, and fungal growth, so a population is usually sitting on microbial growth that is too faint to notice at a glance. Look at the surface under them with a low-angle flashlight. Whether that growth is a health concern depends on its extent, which is worth assessing.

**Do springtails mean my house has a structural problem?**

Not necessarily. Condensation on a cold pipe, an overwatered houseplant, or a wet mat produces them with nothing wrong with the building. But a population that persists after drying, or one inside a wall cavity, is worth investigating as a water intrusion issue.

## Bottom Line

Springtails are a moisture gauge with legs. They cannot survive dry air, they eat the microbial film that grows where water sits, and they bite nothing. Finding them means a specific spot is staying wet — a shower caulk line, a sink overflow, an overwatered plant, a basement wall wicking from a bad downspout, or a new slab still curing. Identify the location, fix the water, dry the space to below 50 percent, and clean the film they were feeding on. No pesticide is involved, and none would work.
