The rule that governs all of it: **store things clean, dry, and with air able to move.** Most summer gear that fails in storage was stored dirty, stored damp, or stored sealed in plastic. Dirt holds moisture against surfaces, moisture drives rust and mold, and sealed plastic traps it.

The second rule is specific to fuel and batteries: **engines and batteries don't like sitting.** Fuel degrades and gums up carburetors; batteries self-discharge and sulfate. Neither survives a winter untouched.

---

## Step 1: Clean everything before storage — this is non-negotiable

Dirt is not inert. Organic matter holds moisture and feeds mold; food residue attracts rodents; road salt and fertilizer residue are actively corrosive.

1. **Wash, then dry completely.** Two full dry days in sun and air beats a towel.
2. **Don't store anything with visible mold or mildew** — it will spread in a closed space. Treat it first.
3. **Clean the storage space too.** A dusty, damp garage contaminates what you put in it.
4. **Never store in plastic bags or sealed containers.** Condensation forms inside them regardless of how dry the item was going in.

## Step 2: Store the grill properly

The grill is the item most often ruined over winter, usually because of leftover food residue and grease.

**Gas grills**

1. **Burn off residue** with a high heat cycle, then brush the grates while hot.
2. **Scrub the grates and the interior** with a degreaser. Grease left on the interior turns rancid and attracts pests.
3. **Empty and clean the grease tray.** This is the step most people skip and it's the worst one to skip — old grease is a major attractant and a fire risk on the first use next year.
4. **Remove and clean the drip pan and the burner tubes**, checking the burner ports for blockages.
5. **Disconnect the propane tank and store it upright, outdoors**, never in a garage or basement. Propane is heavier than air and pools at floor level.
6. **Cover the grill with a breathable cover**, and place a small container of moisture absorber inside if it will be stored in a damp space.
7. **Store indoors if possible**, or in a covered, ventilated spot.

**Charcoal grills**

1. **Empty and clean the ash pan completely.** Ash absorbs moisture and holds it against the metal, causing rust from inside out.
2. **Store charcoal in a sealed container in a dry place** — damp charcoal won't light.
3. **Scrub and oil the grates** lightly, and store them in a dry location.
4. **Cover with a breathable cover.**

## Step 3: Wash and store cushions, textiles, and soft goods

Anything with fabric or foam is where mold establishes first.

1. **Cushions:** wash removable covers per the tag, then **dry the foam completely** — this can take several days. Never store a foam core that is anything but fully dry.
2. **Store cushions indoors**, in a dry, ventilated space. This is the single most important storage decision for patio furniture.
3. **Awnings and umbrellas:** clean, dry fully, then store retracted indoors if possible. Leaving an awning rolled up and damp outside over winter is how the fabric rots.
4. **Hammocks and nets:** wash, dry, store indoors away from rodents.
5. **Beach and pool towels:** wash and store with other linens. Don't leave them in a damp pool bag.

## Step 4: Service the lawn equipment

Small engines fail in spring because of what happened in fall. Do the work now.

1. **Run the mower until the fuel runs out** — or add fuel stabilizer to a full tank. Both work; the empty-tank method avoids stale fuel entirely, while the full-tank method avoids condensation inside the tank. Don't do neither.
2. **Change the oil** if it's due, or at minimum check the level.
3. **Service the air filter and spark plug.** Spark plugs are cheap; replace rather than clean.
4. **Sharpen or replace the mower blade.** A dull blade tears grass rather than cutting it, which leaves the lawn brown-tipped.
5. **Scrape the underside of the deck clean.** Caked grass holds moisture against the metal all winter and rusts through.
6. **Store the mower in a dry, covered location.**
7. **Drain the fuel from other small engines** — string trimmers, edgers, leaf blowers.
8. **For a snow blower you're bringing online, do the same in reverse:** fresh fuel, check the auger and belts, test before the first snow.

## Step 5: Store bikes, sports gear, and pool equipment

**Bikes**

1. **Clean the frame and drivetrain** — road grime and grit wear the chain.
2. **Degrease, then lubricate the chain** properly. A dry chain rusts; an over-oiled chain attracts grit.
3. **Check tire pressure** and inflate. Tires sitting flat all winter develop flat spots and can crack at the sidewall fold.
4. **Store hanging off the ground if possible**, by the frame not the wheels.
5. **For long storage, reduce tire pressure slightly** on tubeless tires, and store in a dry space.
6. **Lithium batteries:** don't store fully charged or fully depleted. Store at roughly 50 percent and recharge every few months.

**Pool equipment**

1. **Winterize the pool properly** — this is a process in itself, and freezing lines are expensive.
2. **Clean and dry the cover** before storing, and store it folded, not crumpled.
3. **Drain, clean, and store** pool toys, floats, and inflatables indoors. Vinyl cracks in cold.
4. **Store pool chemicals** in a dry, ventilated location, sealed, and away from anything metal — chlorine fumes corrode metal across a garage.

**Sports and camping gear**

1. **Clean and dry tents completely** before storing, and store loosely, not compressed.
2. **Wash and dry sleeping bags** per their care instructions, and store uncompressed in a large sack — a compressed bag destroys loft over months.
3. **Clean and oil tools** like clippers and pruners, and check for rust.
4. **Check bikes and gear for damage** now, so you can repair or replace in the off-season rather than at the start of spring.

## Step 6: Set up the storage space itself

Where you put things matters as much as how.

1. **Store off the floor.** Concrete wicks moisture year-round; place items on pallets, shelving, or blocks.
2. **Leave space between items** for air circulation. Packing things tight against each other holds moisture.
3. **Don't store against exterior walls.** They're the coldest and dampest surfaces in a garage or basement.
4. **Use breathable covers, never plastic sheeting.**
5. **Add moisture absorbers** (silica gel, calcium chloride) in enclosed storage areas and replace them periodically.
6. **Control the temperature** if you can; a stable, cool, dry space is far better than one that swings above and below freezing.
7. **Check for rodent access.** Mice nest in stored gear — cushions, engine air boxes, sleeping bags. Seal openings and use traps, not poison, in a storage space.

## Step 7: Make next spring easy

1. **Label and group by season** so you're not digging through everything in April.
2. **Photograph the layout** if the storage is tight.
3. **Note what needs replacing** in spring and buy it in the off-season, when prices on summer goods are lowest.
4. **Write down what you ran out of** this season so you don't repurchase the wrong things.
5. **Keep a "to-do in spring" list** with the storage items — items like "mower blade sharpened? check air filter."

## Why This Works

Storage damage has three causes and each has a specific countermeasure.

Corrosion requires water and oxygen at a metal surface. Dirt holds water against that surface, so cleaning removes the water reservoir. Air circulation lets moisture evaporate rather than condense. Plastic wrap defeats the second mechanism by sealing moisture in, which is why it makes things worse despite feeling protective. Breathable covers keep rain and dust off while letting vapor escape.

Mold and mildew need moisture, organic nutrients, and still air. Residue on a grill or lawn mower deck supplies the nutrients; a sealed container supplies the still air and the moisture. Storing clean, dry, and ventilated removes all three conditions, which is why thorough drying matters more than any product.

Small engine failure in spring is almost always a fuel problem. Gasoline begins oxidizing within weeks, and the volatile fractions that make it ignitable evaporate. What's left forms varnish and gum deposits in the carburetor jets, which are tiny — a few thousandths of an inch. Either method of fuel management (run dry or stabilize) avoids it; leaving untreated fuel to sit does not.

Battery failure follows charge state. Lead-acid batteries self-discharge and, when left discharged, sulfate — a permanent chemical change that reduces capacity. Lithium batteries degrade with both full and empty storage states and with temperature. Storing at partial charge in a cool place is what both chemistries tolerate best.

The reason sleeping bags must be stored uncompressed is that loft is a physical structure. The synthetic or down filaments that trap air recover from compression over hours to days, but months of compression causes permanent loft loss — the insulation stops working. The same principle applies to foam cushions stored crushed under other items.

Freeze-thaw cycling drives concrete and pool plumbing damage. Water expands about 9 percent on freezing, and in a confined pipe or crack that expansion generates enormous force. Pool lines left full of water crack; driveways with unfilled cracks break apart. Both are prevented by removing the water before the freeze.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Storing gear while still dirty | Dirt holds moisture and feeds mold | Wash and dry fully first |
| Wrapping items in plastic sheeting | Traps condensation against the surface | Breathable covers only |
| Storing a grill with grease in the tray | Rancid grease attracts pests and is a fire risk | Clean the tray completely |
| Storing propane indoors | Propane is heavier than air and pools at floor level | Store upright, outdoors, ventilated |
| Storing cushions damp | Foam core mildews within days | Dry the foam fully, store indoors |
| Leaving fuel in small engines untreated | Gums up the carburetor jets over winter | Run dry or add stabilizer |
| Leaving ash in a charcoal grill | Absorbs moisture, rusts from inside | Empty and clean the ash pan |
| Storing items on concrete floors | Concrete wicks moisture year-round | Elevate on pallets or shelving |
| Storing bikes with flat tires | Flat spots and sidewall cracking | Inflate fully, store off the ground |
| Storing sleeping bags compressed | Permanent loft loss destroys insulation | Store loose in a large sack |
| Packing storage tight with no air gaps | Traps moisture between items | Leave space for circulation |
| Storing things against exterior walls | Coldest, dampest surface in a garage | Leave a gap, use interior shelving |
| Storing pool chemicals near metal | Chlorine fumes corrode metal across the space | Separate, sealed, ventilated storage |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How should I store my grill over winter?**
Burn off residue, scrub the grates and interior with degreaser, empty and clean the grease tray, disconnect the propane tank and store it upright outdoors, then cover with a breathable cover. Store indoors if you have space.

**Should I drain the gas from my mower for winter?**
Either drain it completely by running the engine dry, or fill the tank and add fuel stabilizer. Both work. Leaving untreated fuel in the tank is what causes carburetor problems in spring.

**Can I leave patio cushions outside over winter?**
No, not if you want them next year. The foam core absorbs moisture through seams and mildews, and it won't dry in winter conditions. Wash, dry fully, and store indoors in a ventilated space.

**How do I store bikes for winter?**
Clean the frame and drivetrain, lubricate the chain, inflate the tires fully, and store off the ground by the frame. For e-bike batteries, store at about 50 percent charge and top up every few months.

**Is it bad to store things in plastic bags?**
Yes, for most items. Plastic traps condensation against the surface regardless of how dry the item was when stored. Use breathable covers or open storage with air circulation.

**Should I store things on the garage floor?**
No. Concrete wicks moisture year-round and is the dampest surface in most garages. Elevate items on pallets, blocks, or shelving.

**Do I need to wash patio furniture before storing it?**
Yes. Dirt, pollen, and organic residue hold moisture against the finish for months. Clean, dry fully, treat wood with oil or metal with wax, then store.

**How do I keep mice out of stored gear?**
Seal openings to the storage space, store items elevated and with air gaps, avoid storing food-adjacent items (grills, cushions) with residue on them, and use traps rather than poison in storage areas.
