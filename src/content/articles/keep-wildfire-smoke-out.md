Wildfire smoke does not behave like the smoke from a burnt pan. It is not a plume that rises and leaves. It is a mass of fine particles that can sit over a region for weeks, and the particles are small enough — most of them between 0.1 and 1 micron — to pass through the gap under a door, the trickle vents in a window frame, and any filter that is not designed to catch them.

That size is the whole problem. A house is not a sealed box; it is a collection of intentional and unintentional openings, and a smoke event turns each of them into a slow leak. The goal is not to make the house airtight. It is to reduce infiltration, stop pulling air in on purpose, and clean the air in the one or two rooms where people actually spend the night.

## What You Are Actually Trying to Remove

Smoke is a mixture, and the parts matter differently.

| Component | Size | What it does | What removes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| PM10 and larger ash | Above 10 microns | Visible, irritates eyes and throat, settles | Closed windows, wiping surfaces |
| PM2.5 | Under 2.5 microns | Reaches deep into the lungs, drives the health risk, causes the haze | HEPA filtration, good seals, MERV 13 |
| Ultrafine particles | Under 0.1 microns | Highest surface area, worst inflammatory profile | HEPA captures most, but sealing matters more |
| Volatile organic compounds | Molecular | The smell, some irritation | Activated carbon, ventilation when air clears |
| Ozone | Molecular | Formed downwind, irritates airways | Nothing in the house; comes from outside air |

The practical consequence: **the number that matters is PM2.5**, the filter that matters is HEPA, and the action that matters most is reducing how much outside air gets in. A house with a good purifier and a door left open is doing very little.

## Step 0: Get a number, not a smell test

Smoke that you can smell is not the same as smoke that is dangerous, and the reverse is also true: PM2.5 is invisible at concentrations that matter.

Get the AQI for your location from a government source or a monitor with a PM2.5 sensor, and read the PM2.5 band specifically rather than the overall index.

| AQI | Category | What it means indoors |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 50 | Good | Normal operation |
| 51 to 100 | Moderate | Sensitive people may notice symptoms |
| 101 to 150 | Unhealthy for sensitive groups | Close windows, run filtration |
| 151 to 200 | Unhealthy | Everyone reduces outdoor activity, clean room for sleeping |
| 201 to 300 | Very unhealthy | Stay in, seal the obvious gaps, no outdoor exercise |
| Above 300 | Hazardous | Consider leaving the area if you can |

An indoor monitor is worth having if you live in a smoke-prone region, because the interesting number is the **difference** between indoor and outdoor PM2.5. If outdoor air is 180 and indoor is 25, the house is doing its job. If indoor is tracking outdoor within an hour, the house is leaking and the purifier is fighting the whole outdoors.

## Step 1: Close the house properly, starting with the openings that matter

Shut the windows. Then deal with the four openings that are usually still open in a "closed" house.

**The gap under exterior doors.** A door with a half-inch gap under it is a hole with a doormat in front of it. A door sweep or a rolled towel along the base closes it immediately.

**Window frames and trickle vents.** Older windows have vents built in to prevent condensation. Close them. Check the seals at the meeting rail of a sash window — that is where most of the leak is, not at the glass.

**The chimney damper.** An open fireplace damper is a direct duct to the outdoors, and smoke will come down it. Close it, and if the fireplace is not in use, seal the throat with a temporary cover.

**Dryer and bath vents with backdraft flaps.** Check that the flaps actually close. A stuck-open flap lets outside air in continuously.

For gaps that cannot be fixed in an afternoon, painter's tape around a window frame is a legitimate temporary measure for the duration of a smoke event, particularly in the room where people sleep. It leaves residue on painted surfaces if left for weeks, so use it for the worst days rather than the whole season.

If the smoke event coincides with a storm, the same sealing work serves both purposes — see [preparing a house for hurricane season](/seasonal/seasons/prepare-home-for-hurricane-season/) for the order in which openings should be handled.

## Step 2: Put the HVAC on recirculate, and understand what that means

A central system set to "on" with the fan running and the outdoor-air intake closed is a whole-house filter. Set to "auto", it only runs when heating or cooling calls for it, which in mild smoke-season weather can mean it barely runs at all.

- **Set the fan to "on"** during a smoke event, so air keeps circulating through the filter.
- **Close the fresh-air intake** if the system has one, and close the economiser if it is a commercial-style unit.
- **Use a MERV 13 filter if the system can take it.** MERV 13 catches the fine fraction; MERV 8 does not. Check the blower's rated maximum first — a filter that is too restrictive drops airflow, and a system with restricted airflow can ice the coil or overheat the heat exchanger.
- **Change the filter when the event ends, not before.** It will be loaded.

Two system types are a problem and should be treated differently. **Evaporative coolers** (swamp coolers) work by pulling outside air through wet pads — they must be off during a smoke event, without exception. **Single-hose portable air conditioners** pull room air, exhaust it outside, and therefore pull outside air in through every gap to replace it; a dual-hose unit or a window unit on recirculate is the right choice.

## Step 3: Build one clean room, not a clean house

Filtering an entire house to a low PM2.5 level during a severe event is expensive and rarely succeeds. Filtering one room to a very low level is cheap and works.

Choose the room where the household sleeps, because that is where eight hours are spent, and because sleep is when the exposure is longest and least interruptible.

- **Smallest practical room**, with the door that closes best.
- **Fewest windows**, and seal the gaps on those windows.
- **Room for everyone to sleep**, even if that means a mattress on the floor for a few nights.
- **A purifier sized for that room**, running continuously at a speed that is tolerable at night.

Then use it as intended: door closed, purifier on, everyone in it for the worst hours. A clean room at PM2.5 of 10 with the rest of the house at 60 is a better outcome than every room at 45.

## Step 4: Size the purifier by CADR, not by the box

The only number that describes what a purifier can do is **CADR** — clean air delivery rate, in cubic feet per minute, usually quoted separately for smoke, dust, and pollen. Use the smoke number.

The working rule for a room with an eight-foot ceiling is that **CADR should be at least two-thirds of the room's floor area in square feet.** A 150 square foot bedroom needs a CADR of about 100 for smoke. A 300 square foot living room needs about 200.

| Room size | Minimum CADR (smoke) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 100 sq ft | 70 | Small bedroom, one unit |
| 150 sq ft | 100 | Typical bedroom |
| 250 sq ft | 170 | Large bedroom or small living room |
| 400 sq ft | 270 | Open plan; two units usually beat one big one |

Three things the marketing does not tell you. **CADR is measured at maximum fan speed**, which is always the loudest setting; a unit run at half speed delivers roughly half the number, so buy for the speed you will actually use. **Activated carbon is what removes the smell**, and most units carry very little of it; if odour is the main complaint, a unit with a substantial carbon stage matters more than a slightly higher CADR. And **ozone generators are not air purifiers** — they are an irritant sold as a solution, and a HEPA unit that produces ozone is worse than no unit at all.

A do-it-yourself box fan taped to a 20-inch furnace filter works better than its appearance suggests, and is a reasonable emergency measure. It is also a fire risk if left unattended or run for days, and it does not have the airflow pattern of a designed unit. Use it for a day, not a season.

## Step 5: Stop pulling air in on purpose

This is the step people miss, and it can undo everything else.

Every exhaust fan in a house removes air, and the replacement air has to come from somewhere. During a smoke event, that somewhere is outside.

- **Bathroom exhaust fans: off.**
- **Range hood: use only if you must, and briefly.**
- **Clothes dryer: run it as little as possible**, and never with a window open nearby.
- **A fireplace or wood stove: do not use it.** It will draw smoke into the room, and it is also a fire risk during a regional emergency when water pressure may be reduced.

The general principle is that a house under negative pressure finds its own openings, and they are never the ones you sealed. Keep the pressure balanced: no big exhaust running, no window cracked "for air".

## Step 6: Seal the gaps you can actually reach

Beyond doors and windows, a few locations account for a surprising share of infiltration, and all of them are cheap to fix permanently.

- **Electrical outlets and switches on exterior walls** — foam gaskets behind the cover plates.
- **The gap where pipes and cables enter** — caulk or expanding foam.
- **The rim joist area in a basement**, which is usually the leakiest part of the envelope.
- **The attic hatch**, which is often just a board with no seal.

None of these are smoke-specific. They are the same fixes that make a house cheaper to heat, and they pay twice. If you want the wider version of this work, [improving indoor air without buying a gadget](/home-hacks/cleaning/improve-indoor-air-without-gadgets/) covers the source-control and ventilation side that no filter can replace.

## Step 7: Know when to leave

Filtration has a limit, and there is a point where the right answer is to leave the area rather than to run another appliance.

Consider leaving when: the AQI is above 300 for more than a day or two and you have no clean room; anyone in the household has asthma, COPD, heart disease, is pregnant, or is an infant; the indoor PM2.5 cannot be held under about 35 despite sealing and filtration; or the power goes out, which takes the purifier and the HVAC with it.

If you leave, and the outdoor air is hot, the combination of heat and smoke is substantially more dangerous than either alone. [Keeping a house safe in extreme heat without AC](/seasonal/seasons/extreme-heat-without-ac/) covers the decision when the two overlap, which in a smoke event with a heat wave they often do.

## Step 8: Clean up after, in this order

Smoke leaves residue, and the residue keeps re-entering the air until it is removed.

1. **Replace the HVAC filter and clean the purifier's pre-filter.** Both are loaded.
2. **Wet-wipe hard surfaces** rather than dry-dusting, which lifts settled particles back into the air.
3. **Vacuum with a HEPA machine**, slowly, including soft furnishings.
4. **Wash curtains, bedding, and anything porous** that was near an open window.
5. **Run the purifiers for a day after the outdoor air clears**, then switch them off and store them where they can be found next time.

What not to do: do not run a standard vacuum without HEPA filtration, which is a particle distribution device; do not dry-sweep; do not assume the smell going away means the particles are gone.

## FAQ

**Do air purifiers actually help with wildfire smoke?**

Yes, if they are HEPA units sized correctly for the room, and if the house is not simultaneously pulling outside air in. A correctly sized unit in a closed room will reduce PM2.5 substantially within an hour. The main reason purifiers disappoint is undersizing, not the technology.

**What MERV rating do I need for smoke?**

MERV 13 is the usual target for a residential HVAC system, because it captures a meaningful share of the fine fraction. Higher ratings capture more but restrict airflow, and a system not designed for them will lose performance. MERV 8, common as a default, does very little for smoke.

**Should I run my bathroom fan to clear smoke smell?**

No. Any exhaust fan pulls outside air into the house to replace what it removes, and during a smoke event that outside air is the problem. The same applies to the range hood and the dryer.

**Is a box fan with a furnace filter enough?**

It is a real improvement and it is much better than nothing, especially in a single room. It is not equivalent to a HEPA unit, it is a fire risk if left unattended, and it should be a stopgap rather than a permanent setup.

**Can I just seal the house completely?**

You can reduce infiltration a great deal, but a house that is truly sealed has its own problems, including indoor pollutant buildup and, if combustion appliances are running, carbon monoxide risk. Seal the obvious openings, keep the exhaust fans off, and filter the air rather than trying to make the envelope airtight.

**Do N95 masks help indoors?**

They help when you have to be outdoors or in a smoky space, and they must be fitted to seal. Indoors, in a filtered room, they are unnecessary and uncomfortable. Surgical and cloth masks do very little against PM2.5.

**How long does the smoke last indoors after the outside air clears?**

Settled particles keep re-entering the air until they are physically removed, so a house can stay elevated for a day or more after outdoor air improves. Change the filter, wet-wipe, and run the purifier for a day rather than expecting it to clear on its own.

## Bottom Line

Smoke control is three actions in a fixed order: stop new air coming in, stop pulling air in on purpose, then filter the air where people sleep. Closing the openings that actually leak, setting the HVAC fan to run continuously with a MERV 13 filter, switching every exhaust fan off, and putting a correctly sized HEPA unit in one closed bedroom will hold indoor PM2.5 far below the outdoor number without any heroics. Check the number rather than the smell, and treat a power outage or a household member at high risk as the signal to reconsider the whole plan.
