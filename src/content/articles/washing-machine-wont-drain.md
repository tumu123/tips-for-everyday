**A washer that won't drain is a plumbing problem before it is an appliance problem.** The machine is doing its job — it filled, it agitated, it rinsed. What failed is the second half of the cycle: getting the water out. And that half is a short, physical chain with only a handful of failure points.

The reason this matters is cost. A drain pump replacement is a $60–$150 part plus labour. A clogged filter is free. And the two look identical from the outside: standing water, a machine that hums when it should be pumping, or a machine that does nothing at all and then stops with an error code.

The single most common mistake is assuming the pump has failed before checking the filter. In most households with a front-loader, the pump is fine and the filter is full of hair, coins, and a sock.

---

## Step 1: Read What the Machine Is Doing

| What you observe | What it means | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| **Water standing, drum won't spin, no sound** | Pump not running — no signal or failed pump | Lid switch, control, pump |
| **Water standing, pump hums but nothing moves** | Pump running, blocked or jammed | Filter, hose, impeller |
| **Water drains very slowly but does drain** | Partial blockage | Filter, drain hose, standpipe |
| **Error code on the display** | The machine has already diagnosed it | Owner's manual code table |
| **Water drains, then fills back up** | Drain hose too low or connected to a blocked standpipe | Hose height, drain line |
| **Water on the floor during drain** | Loose hose clamp or split hose | Hose connections |

**"Hums but doesn't move" is the most useful signal on this list.** A humming pump has power and a spinning motor with a mechanical block — almost always debris in the impeller or a clogged filter upstream of it. That is a free fix.

**Silence is different.** If there is no sound at all when the cycle reaches drain, the pump is not being told to run. On a top-loader, the lid switch is the first suspect — many machines will not drain or spin with the lid open, and a failed switch reads as "open" permanently.

## Step 2: Before You Open Anything — Contain the Water

This step takes two minutes and prevents an hour of mopping.

1. **Unplug the machine.** Not just off — unplugged. There is water and electricity in the same box.
2. **Shut off the water supply valves** behind the machine, particularly if the water level is above the door.
3. **Put a shallow tray or baking sheet** under the filter door, with towels around it.
4. **Have a bucket and a second towel ready.** Expect more water than the tray holds.

On a front-loader, the filter housing holds a litre or two even after the drum has drained. On a top-loader there is no filter door — the drain path is the pump itself, accessed from the front panel or by tipping the machine.

**Do not tip a front-loader onto its side with water inside.** The water goes into places it isn't designed to go, and on some models it reaches the motor or the control board.

---

## Step 3: The Filter — The Answer Most of the Time

Front-loaders have a drain pump filter behind a small door at the bottom front, usually on the right. It exists specifically to catch what shouldn't reach the pump.

1. **Open the access door.** Some have a small coin-slot latch, some a push-in panel.
2. **Pull out the emergency drain tube** if there is one, remove its cap, and let it drain into the tray. This drains the drum without opening the filter housing.
3. **Unscrew the filter** — turn counter-clockwise. It will be tight and it will release more water. Go slowly.
4. **Clean the filter** under running water with a bottle brush. Pull out hair, lint, coins, bra wires, hairpins, and the occasional sock.
5. **Look inside the housing** with a flashlight. Reach in and check the impeller — the small plastic vanes should turn freely. Hair wrapped around the impeller shaft is the classic cause of "hums but won't turn."
6. **Reassemble and test** with a short rinse-and-spin cycle.

**How often this needs doing:** monthly in households with pets or long hair, every three months otherwise. It is the single highest-value maintenance task on a washer, and almost nobody does it until something breaks.

| What you find in the filter | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Hair wrapped around the impeller | The pump is fine; clean and reassemble |
| Coins, bra wires, hairpins | Normal debris; check pockets going forward |
| A sock or small garment | It made it past the drum — retrieve it fully |
| Sludge with a sour smell | Biofilm; clean the drum too, not just the filter |
| Nothing at all | The blockage is downstream — go to Step 4 |

## Step 4: The Drain Hose — Kinks, Height, and the Standpipe

If the filter is clean and the pump runs, the blockage is in the hose or the house drain.

**Check for kinks first.** The drain hose runs from the pump, up the back of the machine, and into a standpipe or a sink. Machines get pushed back against the wall; the hose folds. A kink that closes the hose completely produces standing water with a perfectly healthy pump.

**Check the hose height.** A washer drain needs the hose to rise to a minimum height — typically 30–36 inches above the floor — before it turns down into the standpipe. This height is not a suggestion: it is what prevents the machine from siphoning water out during the wash cycle, and what lets the pump work against a predictable head. If the hose was recently re-hung lower, water can drain continuously during fill — or fail to drain at all if the standpipe is also partially blocked.

**Check the standpipe and trap.** Remove the hose from the standpipe and look inside with a flashlight. Lint mats the inside of standpipes over years. A standpipe that drains slowly backs water up into the machine, and the machine reads that as "won't drain."

**Check the hose itself** for a lint plug. Disconnect both ends over a bucket and run water through it from a tap. If flow is restricted, replace the hose — they are inexpensive and cleaning a corrugated hose never works completely.

## Step 5: The Pump — Testing and Replacing

If the filter is clean, the hose is clear, and the pump hums but doesn't turn, the pump impeller is jammed or the pump has failed.

**On a top-loader:** remove the cabinet's front or back panel, find the pump (usually a small unit attached to the bottom of the tub), and check the impeller by hand with power off. Debris in the impeller is common — a small object that got past the tub.

**On a front-loader:** the pump sits behind the filter housing. With the filter removed, the impeller is visible. Turn it by hand; it should move with slight resistance and no grinding.

**Signs the pump genuinely needs replacing:**

- The impeller turns freely but the machine still won't drain (pump motor failed)
- The pump runs and drains, but leaks from the housing
- Grinding or screeching from the pump area
- The pump runs constantly, even between cycles

Pump replacement is a moderate DIY job — usually two hose clamps and a wiring connector, access depending on the model. Photograph the hose routing before disconnecting anything, because getting the inlet and outlet swapped is an easy mistake and produces a machine that pumps water *into* the drum.

## Step 6: The Lid Switch and the Control

On a top-loader, the lid switch does more than prevent the drum from spinning with the lid open. On many models it also gates the drain and spin portion of the cycle. A failed switch — often a small plastic actuator that snaps off — leaves the machine stuck at the end of the wash, full of water, doing nothing.

**How to test it:** most lid switches are accessible under the top panel at the back. With power off, check continuity across the switch with the lid closed. No continuity means a failed switch.

This is a cheap fix and worth checking before condemning a pump. It is also the reason a machine that "won't drain" suddenly works when you hold the lid down — that's diagnostic information, not a workaround.

**Control board failure** is the last suspect and the most expensive. Symptoms: the machine advances through the cycle but never energises the pump, or behaves erratically across multiple functions. On a machine over ten years old, a control board usually means replacement rather than repair.

---

## Step 7: Special Case — Water Drains, Then Comes Back

If the drum empties and then refills, you do not have a drain problem. You have a siphon.

The drain hose is too low, or it is pushed too far into a standpipe that is itself partially blocked, and the machine is siphoning water in from the standpipe. Fix the hose height first — 30–36 inches of rise before it goes down — then clear the standpipe.

The tell: the water that comes back is cold and clean, not sudsy. If it is sudsy, you have a different problem — too much detergent producing a foam lock that the pump cannot move.

---

## Step 8: Suds Lock — The Failure That Isn't a Failure

A machine that stops mid-cycle, full of water, with the drum full of foam is not blocked. It has a suds lock: too much detergent or the wrong detergent for the machine has produced so much foam that the pump is moving air and foam rather than water, and the pressure switch never reads "empty."

The fix is patience: let it sit 30–60 minutes for the foam to collapse, then run a drain-and-spin cycle. The prevention is less detergent — high-efficiency machines need far less than the bottle suggests, and the "fill to the line" habit from top-loaders causes this constantly.

This is worth knowing because it is often misdiagnosed as a pump failure and results in an unnecessary service call. [Cutting laundry costs](/save-money/bills/save-money-on-laundry/) covers dosing correctly, which also happens to be the cheapest fix for the problem.

## Step 9: After It Drains — Deal With What the Water Left

Standing water in a washer for more than a day produces a sour, mildewy smell that lingers even after the machine is fixed. The drum, the gasket, and the filter housing all need attention once drainage is restored.

Run a hot cycle with a drum cleaner or two cups of white vinegar, wipe the door gasket folds, and clean the detergent drawer. [Removing mildew smell from laundry](/home-hacks/laundry/remove-mildew-smell-laundry/) deals with the fabric side; the machine side is covered in [cleaning a washing machine](/home-hacks/appliances/how-to-clean-washing-machine/), which is the maintenance task that prevents both the smell and, indirectly, the clog.

## Why This Works

**Drain is a pressure problem, not a flow problem.** The machine doesn't measure how fast water leaves — it measures pressure in the drum via a pressure switch or a float. The cycle advances when the switch reads "empty." Anything that keeps pressure high — a blocked filter, a kinked hose, a foam layer — stalls the cycle at drain, regardless of whether the pump is working.

**The filter exists because the pump is the most vulnerable part of the water path.** It has the tightest clearances and the smallest passages. Debris that would pass through a drain trap will destroy an impeller. Catching it upstream is cheaper than replacing the pump, which is exactly why front-loaders put the filter where you can reach it without tools.

**Hose height is calibrated to the pump's head pressure.** A washer pump is a low-head, high-volume device. It can lift water 30–36 inches and no more. Set the hose lower and the machine siphons; set it higher and the pump cannot overcome the head, so water stays in the drum.

**The lid switch is a safety interlock that also happens to gate drain.** Because drain and spin occur together in the cycle logic, and spin requires a closed lid, manufacturers wire the switch to both. A failed switch therefore takes out draining as a side effect — which is why the symptom looks like a pump failure.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Opening the filter door without a tray | Several litres on the floor | Tray, towels, bucket, ready |
| Working on the pump with the machine plugged in | Shock risk with water present | Unplug first, every time |
| Tipping a front-loader on its side with water inside | Water reaches motor and control board | Drain via the emergency tube first |
| Pouring drain cleaner into the washer | Damages seals, pump, and hoses | Mechanical clearing only |
| Assuming the pump failed without checking the filter | Paying for a repair you didn't need | Filter first, always |
| Running the machine repeatedly to "clear it" | Floods the floor; can burn out the pump | Stop and diagnose |
| Replacing the pump when the hose is kinked | Same symptom after the new pump | Check the hose before ordering parts |
| Pushing the machine flush against the wall | Kinks the drain hose | Leave 4 inches of clearance |
| Ignoring the filter for years | Pump failure, sour smell, poor drainage | Clean monthly with pets in the house |
| Using non-HE detergent in an HE machine | Suds lock, repeated drain stalls | HE detergent, less of it |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Where is the drain filter on my washer?**
On a front-loader, behind a small door at the bottom front, usually on the right side. Top-loaders generally don't have one — the pump is behind the cabinet panel.

**Can I just bail the water out and keep using it?**
The machine will stop again at the same point in the next cycle. Bailing is fine as a stopgap to get your clothes out, but it doesn't fix anything.

**How much water will come out when I open the filter?**
Usually one to three litres, plus whatever is in the drum. Use the emergency drain tube first if your machine has one — it lets the drum empty slowly without opening the housing.

**The filter was clean and the pump runs. Now what?**
Check the drain hose for kinks and lint plugs, then the standpipe. A blocked standpipe backs water up and looks exactly like a machine that won't drain.

**Why does my washer drain and then refill?**
Siphoning. The drain hose is too low or pushed too far into the standpipe. It needs to rise 30–36 inches before turning down.

**Is replacing a drain pump a DIY job?**
Yes on most machines — it's two hose clamps and a plug. Photograph the hose routing first. On some models the pump is buried behind the tub and the job is not worth doing yourself.

**Why does the machine stop with foam everywhere?**
Suds lock from too much or wrong detergent. Let it sit, then run drain-and-spin. Use less HE detergent.

**Is it worth fixing a 12-year-old washer?**
Usually not for a pump or control board. Most washers last 10–13 years, and a $300 repair on a machine that age is money spent on borrowed time.

## Bottom Line

Standing water in the drum is a chain problem, and the chain is short: filter, hose, standpipe, pump, control. Work it in that order and most cases resolve at the first link for free.

The two things that mislead people are silence and foam. Silence usually means the pump was never told to run — check the lid switch. Foam means the pump is running fine and has nothing to pump — let it collapse. Neither one is a broken pump, and both are commonly paid for as if they were.

If you've cleared the filter, confirmed the hose and standpipe, and the pump turns freely but the machine still won't drain, the pump motor has failed. That is the point where a replacement part makes sense — or where [the repair-or-replace calculation](/save-money/shopping/repair-or-replace-appliance/) tells you the machine is done.
