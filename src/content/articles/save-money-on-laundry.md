Laundry is one of the more expensive routine chores, and most of that cost is invisible. **Heating the water** and **running the dryer** dominate the energy use, while **over-dosing detergent** and **fabric softener** quietly degrade clothes so they wear out faster.

**The changes with essentially no perceptible downside, ranked by savings:**

1. **Wash in cold water** (saves 75–90% of washing energy)
2. **Air-dry instead of using the dryer** (typically the single largest line item)
3. **Use less detergent** (saves product cost and prevents machine odor)
4. **Drop fabric softener** (saves money and extends fabric life)
5. **Run full loads** (same cost per cycle regardless of load size)

The rest of this guide explains each, with the numbers behind the claims.

**Caveat:** Savings figures are typical U.S. household ranges. Actual amounts vary by energy rates, water rates, machine age, and family size.

## 1. Wash in Cold Water (The Biggest Energy Lever)

**Roughly 75–90% of the energy a washing machine uses goes to heating water**, not to running the motor. This is the single most impactful change available.

**Why cold water works now:**

Modern detergents are formulated with enzymes designed to activate at lower temperatures. The older advice that hot water cleans better is largely obsolete for everyday laundry.

**How to make the switch:**

1. **Set cold as the default** in your machine's settings.
2. **Reserve warm or hot water for specific cases:**
   - Heavily soiled work clothes
   - Bedding and towels during illness
   - Cloth diapers (for sanitizing)
   - Grease-stained items
3. **Use a detergent labeled for cold water** — most modern formulas are.
4. **Do not overfill the machine.** A crowded drum prevents proper agitation in any water temperature.

**What stays the same:** Cleaning results for normal soil levels. Independent testing consistently finds cold-water washing comparable to warm for everyday laundry when a modern detergent is used.

## 2. Air-Dry Instead of Using the Dryer

The dryer is among the most energy-intensive appliances in a home. Air-drying even part of your laundry produces meaningful savings.

**Typical dryer cost:** roughly $0.30–0.60 per load for an electric dryer, which works out to $60–140 per year for an average household. Gas dryers cost less per load but still consume energy.

**Practical air-drying:**

1. **Use a folding drying rack** for shirts, dresses, delicates, and anything that benefits from not shrinking.
2. **Use a retractable line outdoors** when weather permits.
3. **Hang items on hangers from a shower rod** — a zero-cost option that works for most shirts.
4. **Keep using the dryer for towels and bedding** if you prefer, but shorten the cycle by using the moisture sensor setting rather than a fixed timer.
5. **Add wool dryer balls** to the loads you do run. They separate fabrics, which improves airflow and reduces drying time. They also replace dryer sheets entirely.

**The bonus:** Air-drying extends fabric life. The lint in your trap is largely fabric fibers worn off in the dryer. Every load you air-dry is fabric you keep.

## 3. Use Less Detergent (Yes, Really)

Over-dosing detergent is nearly universal, and it causes problems that cost money.

**The hidden costs of too much detergent:**

- **Residue buildup in the machine** — the leading cause of musty odor and a common reason machines need early repair
- **Residue in fabric** — makes towels stiff and scratchy, prompting people to use more softener
- **Poorer cleaning** — excess suds cushion fabrics and prevent agitation from working
- **Higher product cost** — you buy more, more often

**How much to actually use:**

| Load type | Recommended amount |
|---|---|
| Normal load, moderately soiled | **Half** the manufacturer's recommended amount |
| Lightly soiled (worn once) | 1/3 the recommended amount |
| Heavily soiled | Full recommended amount |
| High-efficiency (HE) machine | Follow HE quantities and use only HE detergent |

**Why half?** Manufacturer recommendations are designed for the worst-case load and the hardest water. Most households have neither. Starting at half and adjusting upward is the reliable way to find your actual requirement.

**Note on HE machines:** Only use detergent labeled HE. Regular detergent produces excessive suds that can damage the machine and leave residue.

## 4. Drop Fabric Softener

Liquid fabric softener is one of the more expensive laundry products per load, and it causes measurable harm.

**What fabric softener actually does:**

- Coats fibers with a waxy, electrically charged film
- That film makes fabric feel soft, but also:
  - **Traps lint and detergent residue**
  - **Feeds biofilm** that causes musty machine odor
  - **Reduces towel absorbency** over time
  - **Degrades elastic and flame-resistant treatments** in some fabrics

**Alternatives:**

| Option | Cost | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Wool dryer balls (3–6) | One-time $10–20, lasts years | Separates fabrics, reduces drying time, softens by agitation |
| White vinegar in the rinse cycle (1/2 cup) | Very low | Softens naturally, removes detergent residue — **do not combine with bleach** |
| Nothing | Zero | Works fine for most households |

**Important:** Vinegar in the rinse cycle and chlorine bleach in the wash cycle produce dangerous gas if they meet. Many machines draw from the same dispenser compartment. Use one or the other, not both, in the same load.

**On dryer sheets:** They work similarly to liquid softener — coating fibers. They also leave residue in the dryer and reduce towel absorbency. Wool dryer balls are the more economical long-term choice.

## 5. Run Full Loads

A washing machine uses roughly the same amount of water and energy per cycle regardless of how full it is. Two half-loads cost nearly twice as much as one full load.

**Practical approach:**

1. **Match the load size setting to the actual load.** Many machines default to the largest setting, wasting water on small loads.
2. **Combine loads where sensible** — but do **not** overload. A crammed drum prevents agitation and results in clothes that are not actually clean, requiring a re-wash.
3. **Wash towels together** and **bedding together** to build full loads.
4. **Reuse towels.** Many households wash towels after a single use, which is unnecessary for most people and adds several loads weekly.

**A note on sorting:** You do not need to sort as aggressively as older advice suggested, but do separate items that shed lint (towels, flannel) from items that attract it (dark knits), and wash new deeply colored items separately for the first few washes.

## 6. Maintain the Machine

A well-maintained machine uses less energy and lasts longer.

1. **Clean the lint trap before every dryer load.** A clogged trap significantly increases drying time and is a fire risk.
2. **Clean the dryer vent line annually.** Restricted airflow is the most common cause of slow drying.
3. **Deep clean the washing machine monthly** with a hot vinegar cycle. See our dedicated guide.
4. **Clean the washing machine drain filter quarterly** (front-loaders).
5. **Check hoses annually** for cracking or bulging.
6. **Level the machine.** An unbalanced machine vibrates, which wears bearings and can require expensive repair.

## 7. Choose the Right Cycle

Settings matter, and the differences are meaningful over a year.

| Setting | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Eco / Cold** | Least water and energy; appropriate for most loads |
| **Normal** | Middle ground |
| **Heavy Duty** | Uses more water, longer agitation |
| **Sanitize** | Heats water significantly; use sparingly |
| **Extra Rinse** | Adds a full rinse cycle; only needed for detergent sensitivity |
| **Moisture sensor (dryer)** | Stops when clothes are dry rather than running a fixed time |
| **Timed dry** | Runs for the full duration regardless — wasteful |

**The dryer setting matters as much as the wash setting.** Switching from timed dry to the moisture sensor setting can reduce drying energy substantially, because timed dry always runs the full duration even when clothes are already dry.

---

## 8. Buy Detergent Strategically

1. **Buy concentrated or ultra-concentrated formulas.** You pay for water in standard formulas.
2. **Compare unit price per load, not per bottle.** This is the only meaningful comparison — bottle sizes vary and concentrations differ.
3. **Buy in bulk only if you will use it.** Detergent has a long shelf life, so bulk is usually fine.
4. **Skip the "with added softener" and "with scent booster" variants.** They cost more and add the residue problems described above.
5. **Try store-brand detergent.** Laundry detergent is a category where private label performs comparably at lower cost.

## Why This Works

| Change | Cost impact | Fabric impact | Noticeable downside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold water washing | Large (75–90% of wash energy) | Gentler on fabrics | None for normal laundry |
| Air-drying | Large ($60–140/year) | Extends fabric life | Takes planning |
| Less detergent | Moderate | Better rinse results | None |
| No fabric softener | Moderate | Extends absorbency and elasticity | Slightly less "cushioned" feel |
| Full loads | Moderate | None | Requires accumulation |
| Moisture sensor drying | Moderate | Less heat exposure | None |
| Machine maintenance | Prevents repair costs | None | Requires time |

The logic behind the ranking: **energy is the largest cost in laundry, so the changes that reduce energy (cold water, air-drying) save the most.** Product changes (less detergent, no softener) save a smaller absolute amount but also extend fabric life, which compounds the benefit.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Using hot water for everything | 75–90% of wash energy is water heating; this is pure waste for normal loads |
| Using regular detergent in an HE machine | Excessive suds damage the machine and leave residue |
| Mixing vinegar and bleach | Produces toxic gas. Use one or the other, never both. |
| Over-dosing detergent "to get clothes cleaner" | Results in worse cleaning, buildup, and earlier machine failure |
| Using fabric softener on towels | Destroys absorbency. Use wool dryer balls instead. |
| Running timed dry instead of moisture sensor | Wastes energy on every load |
| Overloading the machine | Prevents agitation; clothes come out not clean, requiring a re-wash |
| Ignoring the lint trap | Increases drying time and creates a fire risk |
| Putting items in the dryer that are still soaked | Extends drying time significantly; a spin cycle costs far less than dryer time |

## FAQ

**Does cold water actually clean clothes as well as warm?**
For normal soil levels, yes, when using a modern enzyme-based detergent. Independent testing consistently finds cold-water results comparable to warm for everyday laundry. Use warm or hot for heavily soiled work clothes, items soiled with bodily fluids, and cloth diapers.

**How much can I save by air-drying laundry?**
Roughly $60–140 per year for an average household using an electric dryer, depending on energy rates and load volume. You do not have to air-dry everything to capture much of the saving — air-drying half your loads captures a large portion.

**Is fabric softener bad for my washing machine?**
It contributes to biofilm that causes musty odor, and it leaves a waxy film inside the machine. It is one of the leading reasons front-load washers develop persistent odor problems. Skipping it reduces both machine maintenance and product costs.

**Do wool dryer balls actually work?**
Yes, for reducing drying time and separating fabrics. They do not add scent and do not soften the way chemical softener does, but over time they soften fabrics through mechanical agitation. They last for years and replace both dryer sheets and liquid softener.

**How much detergent should I really use?**
Start with half the manufacturer's recommended amount and adjust upward only if clothes are not coming out clean. Recommendations are designed for worst-case loads and hard water. Most households can use considerably less than the label suggests.

**Is it worth buying a high-efficiency washing machine?**
Front-loaders and HE top-loaders use roughly 40–50% less water and significantly less energy than older top-loaders. If your machine is due for replacement, the savings over its lifetime are substantial. But maintaining your existing machine properly and switching to cold water captures much of the benefit at no cost.

**Does washing towels after every use make sense?**
For most people, no. Towels stay fresh for several uses if hung to dry between uses rather than left in a damp pile. Washing towels weekly rather than after each use eliminates several loads per month.

**Should I use cold water for bedding and towels?**
Cold water is fine for routine bedding and towel washing. Use hot water when someone has been ill, when washing cloth diapers, or when bedding has been contaminated with bodily fluids. Hot water also helps with dust mite allergen, though drying heat accomplishes much of the same.
