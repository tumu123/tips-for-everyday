If your pipes bang when you close a tap, you are hearing a pressure wave — and the fix depends on which of three things is causing it.

**What is actually happening:** water is moving through the pipe with momentum. When a valve closes quickly, that moving column of water has nowhere to go, so it slams into the closed valve and the pressure spike travels back through the pipe. That spike is what rattles the pipe against the framing. It is the same principle as a car hitting a wall, not the same as a blockage.

**Three things make it happen, and they are different jobs:**

| Cause | How you can tell | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| High house pressure | Banging happens at several fixtures; pressure at an outdoor tap is high | Pressure-reducing valve |
| Loose pipework | Bang is a rattle more than a thud; pipes accessible in a basement or crawlspace are loose | Secure and insulate the pipes |
| Fast-closing valves, no arrestor | Bang is specific to a washing machine, dishwasher, or a single tap | Fit an arrestor, or service the valve |

Most houses have some combination. Start with the cheapest test.

## Step 0: Identify Which Fixture Causes It

Run each fixture one at a time and close it quickly. Note which ones produce the bang.

**A single fixture** → local problem. Usually a fast-closing valve with no arrestor. This is the common case for washing machines and dishwashers, which close their solenoid valves almost instantaneously.

**Several fixtures, especially the ones nearest the main shut-off** → high house pressure.

**Any fixture, with a rattling quality** → loose pipework amplifying a normal pressure wave.

Also note whether it happens on closing only, or on opening too. Closing is the classic water hammer. Banging on opening often points to loose pipes or a worn washer.

## Step 1: Check the House Pressure

This is a five-minute test that determines whether the whole problem is upstream.

Screw a **water pressure gauge** onto an outdoor tap — usually a hose-thread gauge from a hardware store — and read it.

**Typical residential pressure is around 40 to 60 psi.** Above about 80 psi is high, and many plumbing components are rated for a maximum around 80.

If your pressure is high, that is the root cause. A **pressure-reducing valve** on the main supply fixes it and also extends the life of every appliance, valve, and flexible hose in the house.

**If you have high pressure, fit a PRV before doing anything else.** Arresting the symptom while leaving the pressure high means you will keep damaging things.

If your pressure is normal, continue.

## Step 2: Secure Loose Pipework

If you have access to the pipe run — a basement, crawlspace, or an open ceiling — look at how the pipe is supported.

**Pipes should be clipped or hung at regular intervals.** A long unsupported run will move when a pressure wave passes through it, and the movement is what you hear. The wave may be entirely normal in magnitude; the noise is the pipe hitting wood.

Add pipe hangers or clips at intervals, and check that existing ones are actually tight.

Insulating the pipe run with foam sleeve insulation also reduces noise transmission, though it does not stop the movement.

**This step alone fixes a surprising number of "water hammer" complaints** where the real issue was never pressure at all.

## Step 3: Fit an Arrestor at the Offending Fixture

For a washing machine, dishwasher, or a single fast-closing tap, a **water hammer arrestor** is the correct fix.

An arrestor contains a cushion of air or a spring-loaded piston that absorbs the pressure spike instead of letting it travel back through the pipe.

**Piston-type arrestors are the modern standard** and do not lose their charge the way old air chambers do. Older homes sometimes have vertical capped pipe stubs that were intended as air chambers; those fill with water over time and stop working.

For a washing machine, arrestors are commonly fitted as a pair at the hot and cold supply, either screwing directly onto the tap's outlets or teeing into the supply.

Turn off the supply, fit the arrestor using PTFE tape on the threads, restore the water, and test.

## Step 4: Service a Fast-Closing Valve

If the banging is at a hand-operated tap and there is no solenoid involved, the valve itself may be closing too abruptly because a worn washer or [cartridge](/fix-it/plumbing/fix-dripping-shower-valve/) lets it snap shut.

A lever tap with a failing ceramic disc can snap closed at the end of its travel. Servicing or replacing the cartridge usually resolves it.

**This is worth checking before fitting an arrestor** if the offending fixture is a normal tap rather than an appliance.

## Step 5: Consider a Whole-House Arrestor

If banging occurs at many fixtures and the pressure is already normal, a single arrestor near the main supply or at the water heater can cover the house.

This is less targeted than fitting them at fixtures, and it is generally the second choice after a PRV if pressure turns out to be the issue.

If pressure is high, do the PRV first and re-test — many houses need nothing further once pressure is corrected.

## Step 6: Re-Test

Close each offending fixture quickly and listen.

**Also check for new leaks** at anything you disturbed, and confirm flexible supply lines to the washing machine are in good condition — high pressure and water hammer both shorten their life, and a burst washing machine hose is one of the most damaging household failures there is.

## Why This Works

The bang is energy that has to go somewhere. A moving column of water carries momentum; when it stops suddenly, that momentum converts into a pressure spike. If the spike is large, or if the pipe is free to move, you hear it.

Each fix addresses a different part of that chain.

A pressure-reducing valve lowers the energy in the system. Less pressure means less momentum in the moving water, so a smaller spike when it stops. This is the only fix that addresses the cause rather than the symptom, which is why it comes first when pressure is high.

Securing the pipework does not change the spike at all — it changes whether the spike produces noise. A pipe held firmly against its framing cannot rattle. This works because in many houses the wave is not abnormal; the mounting is simply inadequate.

An arrestor absorbs the spike locally. It gives the moving water somewhere to go, converting the shock into compression of air or a spring. It is the right answer for appliances because their solenoid valves close far faster than any human can, so no amount of careful behaviour on your part will prevent it.

The diagnostic order follows from cause-and-effect: correct the system-wide cause first, then the mechanical mounting, then the local absorption. Doing it in the reverse order means fitting arrestors to a house that is still over-pressured.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not fit arrestors before checking the pressure.** If pressure is high, that is the cause, and arrestors only mask it.

**Do not ignore high pressure.** It damages appliances, valves, and flexible hoses throughout the house.

**Do not assume it is a clog.** Water hammer is a pressure phenomenon and has nothing to do with drainage.

**Do not rely on old air chambers that have gone silent.** They have likely filled with water. Modern piston arrestors hold their function.

**Do not overtighten pipe clips** so that they deform the pipe or prevent normal thermal movement. Snug, not crushed.

**Do not forget the washing machine hoses.** If banging has been going on, inspect them; they are the most likely thing to fail next.

**Do not fit a PRV without checking whether one already exists.** Many houses have one, and it may simply be failing or set wrong.

## FAQ

**Is water hammer dangerous?**
The noise is not, but repeated pressure spikes stress joints, valves, and flexible supply lines. Over time it can contribute to failures — and a failed washing machine hose is genuinely destructive.

**Why does it only happen with the washing machine?**
Appliance solenoid valves close in a fraction of a second, far faster than a tap you turn by hand. That abrupt stop creates the sharpest spike.

**What pressure is too high?**
Above roughly 80 psi is high for residential plumbing, and many components are rated near that. Around 40 to 60 psi is a typical target.

**Do I need a plumber to fit a PRV?**
It involves the main supply and usually some pipework, so many people have it done. The diagnosis — measuring pressure — you can do yourself in minutes.

**Will an arrestor fix banging at every fixture?**
It fixes it at the fixture it serves. If the cause is house pressure, a PRV is the answer instead.

**My house has old capped pipe stubs that were supposed to prevent this. Why is it banging?**
Those are air chambers, and they fill with water over the years and stop working. Draining them restores function temporarily; piston arrestors are the durable fix.

**Can I just close taps more slowly?**
For hand-operated taps, yes — that genuinely reduces it. It does nothing for appliances with solenoid valves.

**Why does the bang happen in the wall rather than at the tap?**
The spike travels back along the pipe, and the loose section that rattles is wherever the pipe is unsupported — often well away from the fixture you just closed.
