Rust stains in a sink or tub are almost always **metal transfer marks**, not corrosion of the fixture itself. The porcelain or acrylic is not rusting — a metal object left on it transferred rust. Typical sources:

- A shaving cream can, razor, or metal soap dish left sitting on the surface
- A can or bottle with a rusted bottom left on the sink
- Metal plumbing parts, screws, or drain components rusting
- Iron content in the water supply itself (well water in particular)

**The method that works:** an acid to dissolve the iron oxide, plus a mild abrasive for stubborn transfer marks. Fresh stains lift with lemon juice and salt. Set stains need acetic acid (vinegar), oxalic acid (cream of tartar or a commercial rust remover), or a pumice stick.

**The critical constraint:** how aggressive you can be depends entirely on the surface. Porcelain enamel tolerates acids and mild abrasives. Acrylic and fiberglass do not tolerate abrasives at all. Determine the material first.

---

## Step 0: Identify the Surface Material

| Material | Appearance | Acid safe? | Abrasive safe? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Porcelain enamel** (most tubs, sinks) | Hard, glossy, cool to the touch, glass-like surface over metal | Yes | Yes, mild only (pumice stick, baking soda) |
| **Cast iron with enamel** | Very heavy, very hard surface | Yes | Yes, mild only |
| **Acrylic** | Plastic feel, warmer to the touch, slightly flexible | Yes, mild acid | **No** — scratches permanently |
| **Fiberglass** | Plastic feel, may flex under pressure, gel coat finish | Yes, mild acid | **No** — scratches through the gel coat |
| **Cultured marble / solid surface** | Uniform, matte, stone-like | **No on cultured marble** (acid dulls it) | Light polishing only |
| **Stainless steel sink** | Metal, brushed | Mild acid only | **No** — scratches show |
| **Natural stone sink or tub surround** | Visible veining | **No** — acid etches stone | No |

**The test:** Tap the surface. Porcelain sounds hard and glassy. Acrylic and fiberglass sound dull and feel slightly warmer. If the surface flexes slightly when pressed, it is acrylic or fiberglass — and abrasives are off the table.

**If your fixture is acrylic or fiberglass**, skip every abrasive method in this guide and stick to acids only (vinegar, lemon, or oxalic acid), applied with a soft cloth.

---

## Method 1: Lemon Juice and Salt (Fresh Stains)

Best for light, recent rust marks and small transfer spots.

1. Sprinkle **table salt** generously over the stain. The salt acts as a mild abrasive and helps the acid cling.
2. Squeeze **fresh lemon juice** over the salt to make a paste.
3. **Let it sit for 10–15 minutes.** Do not let it dry out completely.
4. Rub gently with a non-scratch sponge or your fingers.
5. Rinse thoroughly.

This method works because citric acid in lemon juice reacts with iron oxide to form soluble iron citrate, which rinses away. The salt provides light mechanical action to lift the loosened stain.

**Note:** This is the gentlest method and is safe on acrylic and fiberglass. If it does not work within two attempts, move to the vinegar method.

---

## Method 2: Vinegar and Baking Soda (Moderate Stains)

1. **Pour undiluted white vinegar** over the stain, or soak a paper towel in vinegar and press it over the stain.
2. **Let it sit for 15–30 minutes.** The paper towel keeps the acid in contact instead of running off.
3. For thicker stains, add **baking soda** to create a paste on top of the vinegar. It will fizz.
4. Scrub gently with a non-scratch sponge.
5. **Rinse thoroughly** — residual acid on porcelain is harmless, but on metal fixtures it promotes further corrosion.

**Do not use this on cultured marble, natural stone, or unsealed surfaces.** Acid dulls these materials permanently.

---

## Method 3: Cream of Tartar and Hydrogen Peroxide (Set Stains)

Cream of tartar is potassium bitartrate, which forms tartaric acid when mixed with liquid. Combined with hydrogen peroxide, it handles stubborn set stains.

1. Mix **cream of tartar with 3% hydrogen peroxide** to form a thick paste. Use roughly 2 parts cream of tartar to 1 part peroxide.
2. Apply the paste to the stain.
3. **Let it sit for 15–30 minutes.**
4. Scrub gently with a non-scratch sponge.
5. Rinse thoroughly.

**Test on an inconspicuous area first** — hydrogen peroxide can bleach some colored surfaces.

---

## Method 4: Commercial Rust Remover (For Severe Stains)

For heavy, long-standing rust stains, a commercial product containing **oxalic acid** is the most effective option.

1. **Choose an oxalic-acid-based cleaner** labeled for porcelain, sinks, or rust removal. Common brands include products marketed as "rust stain remover" for bathroom use.
2. **Test on an inconspicuous spot first**, wait 10 minutes, and check for damage.
3. **Wear rubber gloves and ventilate the room.**
4. Apply according to the label — typically a gel or paste left for 5–15 minutes.
5. Scrub gently and rinse **thoroughly**.
6. **Do not use oxalic acid on natural stone** — it etches stone.

**On availability:** Oxalic acid is the active ingredient in many commercial rust removers and is also sold as a powder (sometimes as "wood bleach"). It is effective and relatively safe when used as directed, but it is toxic if ingested — store it away from children and pets.

---

## Method 5: Pumice Stick (For Porcelain Only)

A pumice stick or pumice stone is a natural abrasive used specifically for porcelain fixtures. It works on stubborn stains that acids cannot lift.

1. **Confirm the surface is porcelain enamel or enameled cast iron.** Never use pumice on acrylic, fiberglass, stainless steel, or solid surface.
2. **Wet the pumice stick and the surface.** Dry pumice scratches.
3. **Rub the stick lightly over the stain** using gentle pressure and a small circular motion.
4. Keep both the stick and surface wet throughout.
5. Rinse and check progress. Repeat lightly as needed.

**Technique matters more than pressure.** Light pressure with many passes removes the stain without dulling the surface. Heavy pressure leaves visible scratches.

**Important:** Only use a pumice stick sold for plumbing and bathroom use. Pumice stones for foot care are softer and less effective, and generic pumice may be too abrasive.

---

## Step: Pre-treat the Source of the Rust

Cleaning the stain without fixing the source guarantees it returns.

1. **Look for the metal object that caused it.** A rusted can, a metal soap dish, a razor left on the surface, a rusted drain stopper, or metal screws.
2. **Remove and replace rusted metal components.** Drain stoppers, overflow plates, and faucet parts are common culprits.
3. **If the rust is coming from the water supply** (well water with high iron), the stain will keep returning. A whole-house iron filter is the long-term fix. For new stains appearing continuously near the faucet, test the water.
4. **Do not leave wet metal objects on the surface.** A coaster basin or a non-metal soap dish eliminates most recurrence.

## Why This Works

| Component | Function | Why it is necessary |
|---|---|---|
| Citric acid (lemon) or acetic acid (vinegar) | Converts insoluble iron oxide to soluble iron salts | Iron oxide does not dissolve in water; acid is what removes it |
| Salt / baking soda / pumice | Mild abrasion to lift loosened transfer marks | Acid dissolves the rust but the mark remains embedded in surface texture |
| Oxalic acid | Stronger chelating agent for iron | Handles old, set stains that weaker acids cannot |
| Contact time | Allows acid to react through the full stain layer | A quick wipe only affects the surface |

The chemistry is a chelation and conversion reaction: the acid binds the iron ions and converts them to a compound that water can carry away. This is why the process is chemically different from cleaning grease or organic stains — it is dissolving a mineral deposit, not emulsifying a fat.

---

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Using a scouring pad or steel wool on any fixture | Permanently scratches porcelain, acrylic, fiberglass, and stainless steel |
| Using pumice on acrylic or fiberglass | Scratches through the gel coat permanently |
| Using acid on cultured marble or natural stone | Dulls or etches the surface permanently |
| Using chlorine bleach on rust stains | Bleach does not dissolve iron oxide; it can set the stain and, on some metals, make corrosion worse |
| Leaving acid on metal fixtures | Promotes corrosion of chrome, brass, and stainless |
| Not removing the rusting object | The stain returns within weeks |
| Mixing acid cleaners with bleach | Produces toxic gas. Never combine. |
| Letting the acid dry on the surface | Concentrates the acid and can etch or dull the finish |

## FAQ

**What removes rust stains from a bathtub?**
For light stains, lemon juice and salt. For moderate stains, undiluted white vinegar with a paper towel pressed over the stain for 15–30 minutes. For set stains, a paste of cream of tartar and hydrogen peroxide, or a commercial oxalic-acid-based rust remover. For porcelain, a pumice stick handles what acids cannot.

**Will vinegar damage my bathtub?**
On porcelain enamel, acrylic, and fiberglass, vinegar is safe in normal use — it dissolves the rust without attacking the surface. Do not use it on cultured marble or natural stone, where acid causes permanent dulling. On metal fixtures, rinse promptly to prevent corrosion.

**Can I use bleach to remove rust stains?**
No. Chlorine bleach does not dissolve iron oxide and can actually set the stain or worsen corrosion on metal. Use an acid instead (vinegar, lemon, or oxalic acid).

**How do I get rust off a stainless steel sink?**
Use a mild acid — vinegar or lemon juice — applied for 10–15 minutes and rinsed thoroughly. Do **not** use abrasives: stainless steel scratches visibly and the scratches cannot be removed. For stubborn marks, use a commercial rust remover labeled safe for stainless steel, and always scrub with the grain.

**Is a pumice stone safe on all sinks?**
No. Pumice sticks are safe on porcelain enamel and enameled cast iron only. They scratch acrylic, fiberglass, stainless steel, and solid surface materials permanently. Confirm the material before using one.

**Why does the rust stain keep coming back?**
Because the metal source is still present. Look for a rusted can, a metal soap dish, a razor, rusted drain components, or high iron in the water supply. Cleaning without removing the source means the stain returns within weeks.

**Does WD-40 remove rust stains?**
It can help with light surface rust on metal, but on porcelain and acrylic surfaces it is not effective and leaves an oily residue that attracts dirt. Use acid-based methods instead.

**Can I use a Mr. Clean Magic Eraser on rust stains?**
Melamine sponges are mild micro-abrasives. They help with light rust transfer marks on porcelain but are not effective on set stains, and they dull the finish on acrylic, fiberglass, and high-gloss surfaces. Use them as a supplement, not as the primary method.
