Here is the number that reframes this entire problem: a school-age child brings home somewhere between **500 and 1,000 pieces of paper a year**. Worksheets, notices, tests, birthday invitations, permission slips, and drawings. By the end of elementary school, that is a stack roughly four feet tall per child.

No storage system survives that volume. Not a file box, not a portfolio, not a labeled binder. Which means this is not an organizing problem at all — it is a **selection problem**, and the sooner you treat it that way, the sooner the pile stops growing.

The good news is that children's paper output is extremely low-value on average and extremely high-value at the tail. Roughly 5% of what comes home is worth keeping for twenty years. The entire job is separating that 5% from the other 95% quickly, while the paper is still in your hand.

## Step 1: Install a landing zone before you sort anything

The reason paper becomes a pile is that it has nowhere to go when it arrives. It gets put on the kitchen counter, and the counter is where decisions go to die.

Set up a three-slot landing zone at the door or in the kitchen — a shallow tray or three upright file slots, about 12 inches wide:

1. **Action** — permission slips, forms to sign, payment envelopes, invitations that need an RSVP.
2. **Keep this week** — the drawing they want on the fridge, the test they are proud of, anything with a date attached.
3. **Everything else** — worksheets, completed practice pages, flyers, fundraising catalogs.

The slots work because the decision happens in three seconds at the moment of arrival, not in a two-hour session in June. The "everything else" slot gets emptied into recycling weekly, and that alone removes 80% of the volume before it ever becomes a storage question.

## Step 2: Apply the one-year test, not the "is it good" test

The instinct when sorting artwork is aesthetic: *is this a good drawing?* That question is unanswerable in the moment and produces a keep pile of everything, because every drawing is evidence of a stage.

Use a different question: **would I recognize this in a year?** Not "is it nice" — recognizable. A page of scribbles that records a specific afternoon with a specific person passes. A page of scribbles that is one of forty identical pages does not.

Three more tests that catch what the first one misses:

- **Does it mark a change?** First recognizable person, first letters, first attempt at perspective, a drawing of a lost pet. Development markers are the ones parents actually want at 40.
- **Is there a story attached?** If you can say one sentence about when or why it was made, it passes. If you cannot, it is one of the forty.
- **Does the child ask about it?** Anything a child pulls out of the pile and shows someone is doing work for you. Keep those without debate.

Apply the same test to schoolwork, with one addition: **keep the work that shows thinking, not the work that shows completion.** A math page with a wrong answer corrected in a child's own handwriting is more interesting in ten years than a perfect worksheet.

The one-year test sorts the artwork. For the paperwork you are obliged to keep, and for how long, see [which home records to keep](/organize/closet/home-records-retention/).

## Step 3: Run a two-stage keep system

Anything that passes the test goes into one of two places, and the distinction matters more than the container.

**The current-year folder.** One folder per child per school year, holding no more than 25 to 30 pieces. This is the "maybe" tier — things you liked but are not yet sure about. It lives in a closet and is reviewed once, at the end of the year.

**The permanent archive.** One archival box per child, holding the selected work that survived the year-end review. This is the tier that follows them into adulthood.

At the end of each school year, do the review: open the current-year folder, apply the one-year test again with actual distance, and move three to five pieces into the archive box. The rest goes. The second pass is easier than the first because a year of distance makes the ordinary pages obvious.

The archive box has a hard capacity. A standard 14 by 18-inch archival box holds roughly 80 to 100 flat pieces, which at four to six pieces a year is more than a childhood's worth. When the box is full, you choose what leaves. It will not fill up if the yearly review is actually done.

## Step 4: Decide what digitizing is for

Scanning is often presented as the solution to the volume problem. It is not, and the reason is worth understanding before you spend a weekend photographing 800 drawings.

A photograph of a drawing captures the image but loses what makes it an object: the texture of the paper, the dent from pressing hard with a crayon, the size, the way it smells, the tape on the back. A digital file is a **backup and a sharing tool**, not a replacement for a child's actual work. Treat it that way and it becomes genuinely useful; treat it as a substitute and you will throw away things you regret.

What digitizing is good for:

- **Sharing with relatives** who live far away.
- **Oversized work** — the mural on a roll of butcher paper that cannot be stored flat.
- **Three-dimensional projects** — the clay figure, the cardboard castle, the diorama. Photograph from two angles, then keep the photo and release the object after a few weeks.
- **Insurance against loss** — a fire or flood takes the originals, not the cloud copy.

What to do when you do scan: photograph flat, in daylight, with the paper square to the frame and no shadow across it. Name the files `YYYY-MM-child-description` so they sort chronologically. Upload to a folder that gets backed up. Do not spend money on a scanner for this — a phone camera in indirect daylight is indistinguishable at normal viewing size.

## Step 5: Display a rotating few, not the whole archive

The single most effective way to reduce the pressure to keep everything is to **show** the good ones. Work that is on display is work that the family is enjoying; work in a box is work nobody sees, which makes it feel like it must be kept for later.

A wall line solves this in an hour: two screw eyes and a length of picture wire or cord, with small clips. Hang eight to ten pieces, and change them at the start of each month. The pieces that come off go straight into the current-year folder or the recycling, with the child involved in the choice.

Three rules that keep the display from becoming another pile:

- **The line holds a fixed number.** Eight clips means eight pieces. New arrivals wait for a space.
- **The child chooses.** Being allowed to pick what goes up is what teaches the selection skill, and it makes discarding the rest far less contested.
- **Nothing goes on the fridge door as well.** One display location, or the kitchen becomes a second archive with worse conditions.

## Step 6: Deal with the school-paper categories separately

Artwork is the emotional half. The rest of the paper is administrative, and it needs different handling:

- **Notices and forms.** Handled in the action slot the day they arrive. Anything requiring a signature goes back into the school bag immediately, not onto the counter.
- **Tests and graded work.** Keep only the ones that show a pattern worth remembering — a breakthrough, a struggle that got resolved. Most graded work has no archival value and the grade is already in the school's system.
- **Certificates and awards.** Flat, in the archive box, one per achievement. Do not laminate; lamination is irreversible and traps moisture if the paper is not perfectly dry.
- **Class photos and yearbooks.** These belong in the archive box, not the artwork folder, because they are dates rather than creations.
- **Homework you supervised.** Goes. There is no version of the future in which you want the third-grade multiplication worksheet.

## Step 7: Set a size and volume limit on three-dimensional work

Clay figures, cardboard structures, and dioramas are the hardest category because they cannot be flattened. They also rarely survive well — cardboard warps, clay chips, glue fails.

The workable rule is time-limited display plus a photo: **each 3D piece gets two weeks on a shelf, then it is photographed and released.** The child keeps the photo in the archive folder. If a piece is genuinely special, it gets one permanent spot on a shelf, and the spot holds one object at a time.

Without a limit, 3D work accumulates in every corner of the house because nobody wants to be the parent who threw away the castle. The photo resolves the conflict: the work is honored, and the object is not stored forever.

## Step 8: Store flat, cool, and dry

The storage conditions matter more than the container brand, though both matter:

- **Flat, never rolled.** Rolling cracks paint and crayon and permanently deforms paper. If you must store oversized work, use a flat portfolio, not a tube.
- **Acid-free or archival boxes** for the permanent archive. Ordinary cardboard is acidic and yellows paper over a decade, which is exactly the timescale you are storing for.
- **Cool and dry, inside the house.** Not the attic, not the garage, not the basement. Those three places have the largest temperature and humidity swings, and paper fails there first.
- **Interleaved with acid-free tissue** if pieces are different sizes, so tape and stickers do not press into the piece below.
- **No plastic bins for long-term storage.** Plastic traps moisture and off-gasses. A lidded archival box breathes.

## Step 9: Involve the child, and make the exit ceremonial

Children resist discarding their own work for a reason that is easy to miss: the paper is proof they made something, and throwing it out feels like saying the making did not matter.

Two practices defuse this completely:

- **Sort together, and let them keep more than you would.** The goal of the first pass is not a perfect archive, it is a child who can eventually do this alone. A child who keeps 40 pieces in year one and 12 in year three has learned the skill.
- **Give the exit a ritual.** Photograph the pile before it goes, or have them pick one piece for the wall line first. The object being released is much easier to accept when the memory has been captured.

By the time they are teenagers, the archive box should be theirs to review. Handing it over at 18 — complete, organized, and theirs — is the payoff for all of this.

## Why This Works

The system treats paper as a flow rather than a stock. Decisions happen at three points: arrival (three-second sort), year end (one-year test), and archive capacity (box full means choose). Each point removes volume, so nothing accumulates long enough to become a project.

The capacity limit is doing quiet work throughout. A fixed box size means the archive cannot grow without a decision, and a fixed number of clips means the display cannot grow either. Both limits convert "keep everything" from a default into a choice someone has to make deliberately.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Sorting everything in June | 800 sheets at once guarantees a keep pile of 800 | Sort at arrival, review yearly |
| Judging by "is it good" | Every drawing is evidence of a stage, so everything passes | Ask "would I recognize this in a year" |
| Scanning instead of selecting | Digital copies do not reduce the physical pile and lose the object | Select first, scan only oversize and 3D |
| Rolling large artwork | Cracks paint and permanently deforms paper | Flat portfolio |
| Storing in the attic or garage | Heat and humidity swings destroy paper first | Interior closet, cool and dry |
| Laminating certificates | Irreversible, and traps moisture | Archival box, flat |
| Keeping every 3D project | Accumulates in every corner; nobody wants to throw out the castle | Two weeks on display, photo, release |
| Displaying everything on the fridge | Becomes a second archive with worse conditions | One wall line, eight clips, monthly rotation |
| Throwing out work without the child | Reads as "your making did not matter" | Sort together, photograph, pick one for the wall |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long should you keep kids' school papers?**

Keep the selected few permanently — four to six pieces per year in an archival box. Administrative paper like notices and graded worksheets can go within the week; the grade already exists in the school's records. Tests worth keeping are the ones that show a change, not the ones that show a score.

**Should you throw away your child's artwork?**

Most of it, yes — but never without a selection process. Roughly 5% of what comes home is worth twenty years of storage. Apply the one-year test, keep what marks a developmental change or carries a specific memory, photograph the rest if you need to let go gently, and recycle the paper.

**How do you store large artwork and posters?**

Flat, in a portfolio or an archival box sized for the piece, never rolled. If the work is larger than any flat storage you have, photograph it at high resolution, display it for a set period, and then release it. A rolled drawing will never lie flat again.

**Is it worth digitizing kids' artwork?**

Yes for sharing, backing up, oversized work, and 3D projects. No as a replacement for the originals. A photo cannot reproduce paper texture, crayon pressure, or size, which is most of what makes the object meaningful. Digitize as insurance, not as a substitute.

**How do you organize papers during the school year?**

Three slots at the door: action, keep this week, everything else. The action slot empties daily, the keep slot rotates monthly, and the third slot goes to recycling weekly. One folder per child per year holds the 25 to 30 pieces still under consideration.

**What do you do with 3D art projects?**

Display for two weeks, photograph from two angles, then release the object. Keep one permanent spot per child for the single piece that matters most, and hold it to one object at a time. Cardboard and clay rarely survive long-term storage anyway.

**How do you get a child to let go of their art?**

Sort together and let them keep more than you would, at least for the first year. Photograph the pile before it goes out. Let them choose what goes on the display line. The skill is learned gradually, and a child who keeps 40 pieces at age six and 12 at age nine has learned it.
