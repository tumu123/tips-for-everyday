Time-of-use pricing is sold as a way to save money by using electricity at the right time. It is more accurately described as a bet: you are wagering that you can move enough of your usage out of the expensive hours to more than pay for the hours you cannot move. For some households that bet is nearly free money. For others it raises the bill by 20% and the household never quite works out why.

Which one you are is not a matter of attitude. It is a number, and you can look it up.

## What the Plan Actually Changes

Under a flat rate, every kilowatt-hour costs the same whatever time it is used. Under time-of-use, the day is divided into windows:

**Peak** — typically late afternoon to evening, most commonly 4 pm to 9 pm on weekdays, when the grid is most strained. This is the expensive window, often two to three times the off-peak rate.

**Off-peak** — the rest of the day, at roughly the flat rate or a little below.

**Super off-peak** — often overnight, roughly 9 pm or 11 pm to 6 am or 8 am, at the cheapest rate on the schedule. This is the window that makes EVs cheap to run.

Three variations that catch people out:

- **Seasonal windows.** Many utilities change both the hours and the prices between summer and winter, and the winter peak is often the expensive one for a house with electric heating.
- **Weekends and holidays.** Some plans have no peak on weekends; some do.
- **Tier structure disappearing.** Many TOU plans drop the baseline/tier system, so the very low usage that used to be cheap in tier one is now priced at the TOU rate. High-usage households often gain, low-usage households sometimes lose.

## The Break-Even, in One Line

The question is what share of your kilowatt-hours lands in the peak window. Here is the arithmetic with a realistic set of prices: flat rate $0.16/kWh, peak $0.32, off-peak $0.09 (a 3.5:1 ratio, which is typical).

| Share of usage on peak | TOU cost per kWh | Flat rate | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | $0.113 | $0.16 | Save 29% |
| 20% | $0.136 | $0.16 | Save 15% |
| 30% | $0.159 | $0.16 | Break even |
| 40% | $0.182 | $0.16 | Lose 14% |
| 50% | $0.205 | $0.16 | Lose 28% |

The threshold sits near **30%** for this ratio, and it moves with the ratio: at a 2:1 ratio the break-even is closer to 40% on peak; at 4:1 it falls to about 25%. That single number — the share of your kWh inside the peak window — is the whole decision. Everything else is detail.

For a typical household, the 4 pm to 9 pm window is 21% of the day's hours, and it contains more than 21% of usage, because that is when people are home, cooking, and running the air conditioner. Somewhere between 25% and 40% is where most families land, which is exactly why the answer varies and why the utility's marketing ("save up to 30%!") is both true and irrelevant.

## Getting Your Own Number

Do not estimate this from memory. Three ways to get the real figure, in order of effort — and if the bill itself moved unexpectedly, [separate rate from usage](/save-money/bills/why-electric-bill-jumped/) before you blame the plan:

**1. The utility's hourly data.** If you have a smart meter — and most US households now do — the utility portal usually shows 15-minute or hourly usage, often with a download option (look for "Green Button" or "Download my data"). Take twelve months of hourly data, sum the kWh inside the peak window, divide by total kWh. That is your number, by season, and it will be different in July and January.

**2. The utility's own comparison tool.** Many utilities offer a "compare plans" tool that runs your historical usage against each available rate and shows the annual cost. It is worth using, and worth checking that it uses at least a year of data — a tool that uses the last 30 days will mislead you.

**3. A two-week experiment.** If no data is available, read the meter at the window boundaries for a couple of weeks. Crude, but enough to tell whether you are at 20% or 45%.

While you are in the data, look at the shape, not just the totals:

- **The evening band.** Is there a sharp block of consumption from 4 pm to 9 pm? That is cooking, air conditioning, laundry, and screens.
- **The overnight floor.** A constant 0.3 to 0.8 kW overnight is the refrigerator, standby loads, and possibly a water heater recovering. It is nearly free under TOU.
- **The evening air-conditioning tail.** In summer, AC often runs past 9 pm because the house is still hot. Under TOU that tail is expensive; pre-cooling the house before 4 pm moves it.
- **Winter morning peak.** If your utility has a morning peak in winter and you heat with a heat pump, that window is where the money goes.

## Who Usually Wins

**EV owners who charge overnight.** This is the strongest case by a wide margin. A car using 350 kWh a month, moved entirely into super off-peak, can cut its running cost by 60% or more. It also usually justifies the 240V circuit and a timer.

**Households with solar and a battery.** Solar produces in the afternoon, and a battery shifts that production into the peak window, which is where the value is highest. Without a battery, solar under TOU is worth less than under flat net metering, because most production is exported at times when the grid does not need it.

**People who are home during the day.** Retired households, remote workers, and shift workers can run laundry, dishwashing, and cooling in the cheap hours without much effort.

**Low-usage households in a mild climate.** If the bill is mostly a refrigerator and lights, the peak share is small and there is little to lose.

## Who Usually Loses

**Families with everyone home from 5 pm to 9 pm.** This is the archetypal losing profile: cooking, laundry, homework, showers, and the AC running during exactly the window that costs triple.

**Electric resistance heating in winter.** If your heat is a baseboard heater or a heat pump with aux strips, the coldest hours are the peak hours. A single 1,500-watt heater running through a five-hour peak is 7.5 kWh at peak prices.

**Electric water heating without a timer.** A tank reheats whenever it cools, including at 7 pm when three people are showering. Without a timer or a heat pump water heater's scheduling, the water heater does what it wants.

**Households that will not change any habits.** TOU rewards shifting. If nobody is willing to delay the dishwasher, the plan is just a higher rate with extra steps.

## What Can Actually Be Shifted

Realistic shifts, with a rough sense of what they save at the example prices (each kWh moved from peak to off-peak saves about $0.23):

| Load | Shift method | Monthly kWh movable |
|---|---|---|
| EV charging | Timer, charge after 9 pm | 250 to 400 |
| Clothes dryer | Delay start to after 9 pm or before 4 pm ([line drying](/save-money/bills/air-dry-vs-dryer-cost/) is cheaper still) | 50 to 90 |
| Dishwasher | Delay start | 20 to 35 |
| Water heater | Timer on the tank, or schedule on a heat pump water heater | 100 to 250 |
| Pool pump | Run overnight | 150 to 250 |
| Dehumidifier | Run midday instead of evening | 60 to 120 |
| Air conditioning | Pre-cool before 4 pm, raise the setpoint during peak | 30 to 100 |
| Baking and oven cooking | Batch-cook midday on weekends | 20 to 60 |

The two biggest levers are almost always the EV and the water heater. Everything else is a few dollars a month. This is why a household without an EV often finds TOU roughly a wash — the plan works, but the savings after behaviour change are $10 to $20 a month, which is real but not transformational.

## The Traps

**Peak price applies to aux heat.** If your heat pump calls for backup strips during a cold snap at 6 pm, those kilowatt-hours are priced at the peak rate and generated at a COP of 1. This is the single most expensive thing that can happen on a TOU plan, and it can wipe out a summer's savings in a week. If you have a heat pump, ask the utility whether there is a separate winter rate, and consider locking out aux heat above a certain outdoor temperature.

**Base charges and minimum bills.** Some TOU plans carry a higher fixed monthly charge, or a minimum bill that applies regardless of usage. A plan that saves 20% on the energy line but adds $8 a month in fixed charges is not a 20% saving.

**Export compensation changes with the plan.** If you have solar, the plan you choose may change what your exports are worth, not just what you pay. Check both sides.

**Billing surprises in the first month.** Utilities usually allow one switch back per year, and some offer a bill-protection guarantee for the first year. Ask. If the option does not exist, switch in a shoulder month (April or October) rather than at the start of summer, so a bad result does not run through the expensive season.

**"Peak" is not the same as "expensive" in every plan.** Some plans have three tiers plus a critical peak pricing event that is called a handful of times a year at 5 to 10 times the normal rate. Those events are where a careless household loses the year's savings.

## How to Decide

1. **Get twelve months of hourly data.** Not one month, not an estimate.
2. **Compute peak share for July and for January separately.** The plan has to work in both.
3. **If peak share is under 25%:** switch, then keep an eye on it.
4. **Between 25% and 35%:** it depends on whether you will actually shift the water heater and the dryer. Decide that before switching, not after.
5. **Above 35%:** do not switch unless you have an EV or a battery that changes the shape.
6. **If you have electric resistance heat or a heat pump with strips:** check the winter rate structure before you sign anything.

## What NOT to Do

- **Do not switch on the strength of the marketing comparison**, which typically assumes you shift usage you have no plan to shift.
- **Do not run the dryer, the oven, and the dishwasher at 7 pm and expect the plan to work.**
- **Do not charge an EV during the peak window.** It is the most expensive mistake available on this tariff.
- **Do not ignore the seasonal window changes.** A household that learns the summer window and keeps it in winter gets the worst of both.
- **Do not forget the fixed charges.** They are often the reason a "cheaper" plan is not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is time-of-use always cheaper if I shift usage?**
No. It is cheaper if your peak share is low enough or your shifting is large enough. The plan itself does nothing; the shifting does the work.

**What percentage of usage is typically on peak?**
For a family that is home in the evening, 30% to 40%. For a household with an EV charged overnight and no evening AC, 15% to 25%. That difference is the whole decision.

**Do I need a smart meter for a TOU plan?**
Yes, and most utilities will install one free if you do not have one. Without hourly metering, the utility cannot bill the windows.

**Can I switch back?**
Usually once per year, sometimes with a fee, and sometimes only after a minimum period. Ask before switching, in writing if possible.

**Does TOU help if I have solar panels?**
It changes the value of solar. Without storage, most production is exported in the afternoon and credited at whatever the export rate is, which may be low. With a battery, you can shift production into the peak window and capture the higher price. The economics of the panels themselves are in [solar payback](/save-money/big-decisions/solar-panel-payback/).

**Is a heat pump a problem on TOU?**
It can be. The heat pump itself is efficient, but aux heat during a winter peak is expensive. Set the aux lockout conservatively and, if available, choose a plan with a lower winter peak.

**What about a "free nights" plan?**
Those plans typically recover the cost in a much higher daytime rate and a higher base charge. Run the same hourly arithmetic against them — the free window is a marketing structure, not a discount.

---

## Bottom Line

One number decides this: the share of your kilowatt-hours inside the peak window. Under about 25%, switch. Above about 35%, do not, unless an EV or a battery changes the shape. In between, it depends entirely on whether you will actually move the water heater, the dryer, and the AC — and on whether the winter rate structure punishes the way you heat.
