Replacing a shower head is a short job with two ways to get it wrong, and both cause the same symptom: a drip at the joint.

**The two joints are different, and they seal differently:**

1. **Shower arm to shower head** — a tapered pipe thread (NPT). It seals because the threads wedge together, so it **needs thread tape**.
2. **Shower head to hose** (on a handheld unit) — a straight thread with a **rubber washer**. It seals on the washer, and it must **not** get thread tape.

Putting tape on the washer joint is the most common reason a brand-new shower head leaks. The tape prevents the washer from seating.

**The other common failure is removal:** the old head has been on for years, mineral deposits have bonded the threads, and turning it puts torque into the pipe inside the wall. That is the part that turns a ten-minute job into a repair.

---

## Step 0: Identify the Joint and Check the Size

1. **Confirm the thread.** Most shower arms use 1/2-inch NPT, which is the standard size. Handheld hoses use a straight thread with a washer.
2. **Look at the old fitting** and note whether it has a rubber washer inside. If it does, that joint seals on the washer.
3. **Check the shower arm's condition** — look for corrosion, cracks, or a loose fit where it enters the wall.
4. **Decide whether you need a new arm.** If the arm is corroded or the ball joint is seized, replace the arm too; it is the same job plus one more step.
5. **Have the new head's instructions to hand**, since some use a washer and some do not.

---

## Step 1: Protect the Finish and Get Grip

1. **Wrap the fitting with a cloth** before using any wrench. Wrench jaws will mark chrome and brushed finishes permanently.
2. **Try to undo it by hand first.** Many heads are only hand-tight.
3. **If it will not move, apply penetrating oil** to the threads where the head meets the arm, and give it time to work — several minutes at minimum.
4. **Do not use the shower arm as a lever.** Gripping the arm and twisting puts torque directly on the fitting inside the wall, which is where elbows crack.

---

## Step 2: Back Up the Shower Arm While Loosing the Head

This is the step that prevents expensive damage.

1. **Hold the shower arm steady with a second wrench** while turning the head with the first.
2. **If the arm is short or smooth**, use locking pliers with a cloth pad, gripping as close to the wall as possible.
3. **Turn the head counterclockwise** to loosen.
4. **If it still will not move,** reapply penetrating oil and wait longer rather than increasing force. Force is what breaks the fitting behind the wall.
5. **If the arm itself starts to turn, stop.** That means the joint behind the wall is taking the load — the arm needs to come out and be replaced properly.

---

## Step 3: Clean the Old Threads Completely

Old tape left on the threads is the number one cause of a leak after replacement.

1. **Peel off all of the old PTFE tape.** Use a toothbrush or a cloth; do not leave fragments.
2. **Scrub off mineral deposits** with an old toothbrush. White vinegar on a cloth helps dissolve scale.
3. **Inspect the thread** for damage. If it is corroded or the first threads are deformed, the arm should be replaced.
4. **Wipe the threads dry.** Tape adheres poorly to a wet or greasy surface.
5. **Check that the arm is still tight in the wall** before continuing.

---

## Step 4: Wrap Tape Correctly — Direction Matters

Tape applied in the wrong direction unwinds as you screw the head on.

1. **Hold the arm so you can see which way the head will turn.** The head tightens clockwise when viewed from below, facing the arm.
2. **Wrap the tape clockwise around the arm** when viewed from the end — that is, in the same direction the fitting will turn when tightening. Wrapping this way means tightening pulls the tape tighter instead of bunching it.
3. **Wrap 3–5 turns**, overlapping by about half the tape width.
4. **Leave the first thread or two bare** at the end, so tape does not get pushed into the waterway.
5. **Press the tape into the threads** as you go so it follows the thread profile rather than bridging over it.
6. **Do not wrap tape on any joint that uses a rubber washer.**

**If you are unsure of direction:** tighten the head by hand first without tape and note which way it turns, then remove it and wrap accordingly.

---

## Step 5: Fit the New Head

1. **Screw the head on by hand first**, turning clockwise. It should thread on easily for several turns — if it binds immediately, the threads are cross-threaded; back off and restart.
2. **Tighten by hand until snug.**
3. **Then use a wrench for about a quarter to half a turn more,** with the cloth protecting the finish. Tapered threads seal by wedging, so some tightening is needed — but overtightening can crack the fitting.
4. **For a handheld unit, make sure the rubber washer is seated** in the hose connection, and tighten that joint by hand only. Do not use tape there.
5. **Position the head** so it points where you want, unless it has a ball joint that allows adjustment afterward.

---

## Step 6: Test Gradually and Check for Leaks

1. **Turn the water on partway first**, not full. A leak at low pressure is easy to see and easy to fix before the ceiling below is involved.
2. **Look at both joints** — the arm-to-head joint and, if present, the head-to-hose joint.
3. **If it drips at the arm joint,** tighten slightly more. If it still drips, undo it, re-clean, and re-tape.
4. **If it drips at the hose joint,** check that the washer is present and seated; tape is not the fix there.
5. **Run it at full pressure** and check again once it has been running for a minute.

---

## Step 7: Deal With Low Flow or Uneven Spray

If the new head sprays poorly, it is usually debris or mineral scale, not the head.

1. **Remove the head and check the inlet screen** — many heads have a small mesh filter that catches debris from the pipes.
2. **Rinse the screen** and refit.
3. **If the spray pattern is uneven,** soak the face in white vinegar to dissolve scale, then scrub the nozzles gently with a toothbrush.
4. **Flex the rubber nozzles** on heads that have them, which breaks up deposits.
5. **Do not poke metal nozzles with a pin**, which enlarges the opening and permanently changes the spray.

---

## Step 8: Check the Flow Restrictor Before You Blame the Head

New heads are fitted with a flow restrictor to meet efficiency standards.

1. **Understand what it does.** The restrictor limits flow rate, which lowers hot water use. Removing it increases water and energy consumption.
2. **Check local rules.** In some areas removing or modifying a restrictor is not permitted, and it may affect a product's certification.
3. **If flow is genuinely too low**, the cause is more often supply pressure or scale in the pipes than the restrictor.
4. **Clean the restrictor rather than removing it** — it can trap debris, and cleaning restores flow without changing the rating.

## Why This Works

| Design choice | Problem solved | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Tape on tapered threads only | Leaks at the arm joint | Tapered threads seal by wedging; tape fills the voids |
| No tape on washer joints | Leaks at the hose joint | The washer does the sealing; tape stops it seating |
| Clockwise wrap in the tightening direction | Tape bunching and unwinding | Tightening pulls the tape in rather than pushing it out |
| Old tape fully removed | Leaks after a correct installation | Fragments prevent the threads from mating |
| Back-up wrench on the arm | Cracked fitting inside the wall | Torque must not reach the in-wall joint |
| Penetrating oil and patience | Seized fittings | Force breaks pipe, time does not |
| Cloth under the wrench jaws | Permanent finish damage | Chrome and brushed finishes mark instantly |
| Gradual pressure test | Hidden leaks found late | A drip is visible and fixable at low pressure |
| Cleaning rather than removing the restrictor | Reduced flow | Debris is the usual cause, and cleaning keeps the rating |

The mechanism is that **the two joints seal on different principles**. Once you know which joint uses a wedge and which uses a washer, the rest of the job is preparation: clean threads, correct tape direction, and no torque into the wall.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Putting tape on the hose-to-head joint | Prevents the rubber washer from seating; the joint will leak |
| Wrapping tape counterclockwise to the tightening direction | The tape unwinds as you screw the fitting on |
| Leaving old tape on the threads | The new tape cannot seat; the joint leaks |
| Using the shower arm as a lever | Cracks the elbow inside the wall |
| Increasing force on a seized fitting | Same problem — use penetrating oil and time instead |
| Overtightening with a wrench | Can crack the fitting or the head |
| Turning the arm itself | The in-wall joint is taking the load; stop and reassess |
| Wrench directly on a finished surface | Permanent jaw marks on chrome or brushed nickel |
| Testing only at full pressure | Leaks are harder to see and more damaging |
| Poking metal nozzles with a pin | Enlarges the openings and ruins the spray pattern |
| Removing the flow restrictor without checking local rules | May not be permitted and changes the product rating |

## FAQ

**Do I need plumber's tape on a shower head?**
On the threaded connection to the shower arm, yes — that is a tapered pipe thread and needs PTFE tape. On the hose connection with a rubber washer, no — that joint seals on the washer, and tape will make it leak.

**Which direction should I wrap the tape?**
Wrap clockwise around the arm when viewed from the end, so that tightening the head pulls the tape in the same direction it was wound. Wrapping the other way causes the tape to bunch and unwind as you tighten.

**My shower head is stuck. How do I remove it?**
Apply penetrating oil to the threads and let it sit. Use a cloth-padded wrench on the head and a second wrench to hold the arm steady. Never use the arm as a lever — that puts torque into the fitting inside the wall, which is where elbows crack.

**Why does my new shower head leak at the joint?**
Usually old tape left on the threads, tape wrapped in the wrong direction, or too few turns. Undo it, clean the threads completely, re-wrap with 3–5 turns in the correct direction, and retighten about a quarter to half a turn past hand-tight.

**How tight should a shower head be?**
Hand-tight plus roughly a quarter to a half turn with a wrench. Tapered threads need enough tightening to wedge and seal, but overtightening risks cracking the fitting or the head.

**Do I need to turn off the water to replace a shower head?**
Usually not the main supply — closing the shower valve is enough for the head itself, since it is downstream of the valve. Confirm by opening the valve briefly after removal and checking that nothing flows before you proceed.

**Why is my new shower head spraying unevenly?**
Debris in the inlet screen or mineral scale on the nozzles. Remove and rinse the screen, then soak the face in white vinegar and flex rubber nozzles gently. Do not poke metal nozzles with a pin.

**Can I remove the flow restrictor for more pressure?**
Check local rules first — in some areas it is not permitted, and it changes the product's certified flow rate. Low flow is more often caused by supply pressure or scale in the pipes. Clean the restrictor before considering anything else.

**Should I replace the shower arm too?**
If it is corroded, cracked, or the ball joint is seized, yes. It is the same job plus one more step, and a new head on a failing arm will not seal properly.
