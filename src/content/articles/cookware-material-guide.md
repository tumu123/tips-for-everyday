Pans are sold on brand and coating, but what actually determines how a pan behaves is one physical property: how fast it moves heat. Copper moves heat at roughly 400 W/m·K. Aluminium at 237. Cast iron at 52. Carbon steel at 43. Stainless steel at 15.

That range — a factor of twenty-six — is why a copper saucepan responds to a turned knob in seconds and a stainless one takes a minute, why cast iron holds a sear through a cold steak, and why a thin aluminium pan develops a hot ring in the middle and a scorched spot on the base.

## The Materials, Ranked by What They Do

| Material | Conductivity | Heat response | Reactivity | Main use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copper | ~400 | Fastest | High (needs lining) | Sauces, sugar work, precise control |
| Aluminium | ~237 | Fast | High (pits with acid) | Cheap pans, baking sheets, anodised cookware |
| Cast iron | ~52 | Slow, holds heat | Low once seasoned | Searing, oven-to-table, cornbread |
| Carbon steel | ~43 | Medium, holds heat | Low once seasoned | Woks, searing, the restaurant skillet |
| Stainless steel | ~15 | Slow, hot spots | None | Clad pans, pots, anything acidic |
| Enamel over steel or iron | Varies with core | Slow | None | Dutch ovens, braises, tomato sauces |
| Nonstick (PTFE) | Depends on substrate | Medium | None | Eggs, fish, delicate items |

Two of these need explanation, because the ranking is misleading on its own.

**Stainless steel by itself is a poor conductor.** A single-ply stainless skillet produces a hot ring and a pale edge. This is why good stainless pans are clad — three to five layers with an aluminium or copper core bonded between stainless skins. The stainless provides the non-reactive surface; the core does the heat work. A "tri-ply" pan behaves like aluminium with a stainless face, which is why it is responsive enough for a pan sauce.

**Cast iron's low conductivity is the point.** It heats unevenly and slowly, but once hot it holds temperature. Drop a cold steak into a thin aluminium pan and the surface temperature collapses; in cast iron it barely moves. Heat retention, not heat speed, is what produces a crust - which is also why the pan matters less than [the internal temperature](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/safe-cooking-temperatures/) when the question is whether the food is done.

## What Cladding Actually Means

"Tri-ply" and "five-ply" are not quality grades; they describe how many metal layers are bonded together and where.

| Construction | What it is | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Single-ply stainless | One layer of steel | Hot ring in the middle, pale edges, warps |
| Disc base | Thin steel body with an aluminium or copper disc pressed into the bottom | Even on the base only; sides scorch; disc can separate |
| 3-ply clad | Stainless / aluminium / stainless, full height | Even across the base and up the sides — the practical standard |
| 5-ply clad | Stainless / aluminium / copper / aluminium / stainless | Slightly more even, heavier, more expensive |
| Copper core | Stainless with a copper inner layer | Fastest response, highest price |

The word that matters is **clad**, meaning the layers run the full height of the pan, not just the base. A disc-base pan looks similar in the shop and behaves completely differently: the walls are single-ply steel, so the sides scorch while the base is even, and the disc seam is where warping starts after a few years of high heat.

Three-ply is the sensible default. Five-ply adds a little evenness and a lot of weight, and most home cooks cannot tell the difference in a sauce. Copper core is a genuine upgrade for sugar work and delicate sauces, and it costs like one.

One more thing the label will not tell you: **thickness matters more than ply count**. A 2.5 mm tri-ply pan outperforms a 2.0 mm five-ply pan on evenness and heat retention. If the specification is available, look at the millimetres.

## Pan Shapes, and What They Change

Two pans with the same diameter and different walls cook differently, and the difference is evaporation.

| Shape | Walls | Best for | What it changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skillet / frying pan | Sloped, low | Searing, sautéing, eggs | Fast evaporation, easy tossing |
| Sauté pan | Straight, tall | Braises, large batches, one-pan meals | Less evaporation, more capacity |
| Saucier | Rounded, sloped | Sauces, risotto, reduction | Whisk access, no corners to catch |
| Saucepan | Straight, tall | Grains, reheating, small liquid volumes | Slow evaporation |
| Wok | High, curved | Stir fry, tossing, deep frying | High heat at the base, cool walls |
| Grill pan | Ridged | Marks, rendering fat | Less contact area, slower cooking |

The sauté pan versus skillet distinction is the one people miss. A straight-sided 3-inch sauté pan holds roughly twice the volume of a skillet of the same diameter and loses far less liquid, which is exactly what a one-pan chicken-and-rice dish needs. A skillet with the same recipe will boil dry.

## The Job-to-Pan Map

This is the table worth printing.

| Job | Best pan | Why | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Searing steak, chops | Cast iron or carbon steel | Heat retention through a cold load | Thin nonstick, single-ply stainless |
| Pan sauce, deglazing | Clad stainless | Fond forms and releases; wine and lemon are fine | Nonstick (no fond, coating damage) |
| Eggs, fish fillets | Nonstick or seasoned carbon steel | Release without tearing | Bare stainless (unless the pan is hot and fat is sufficient) |
| Tomato sauce, wine braise | Enameled cast iron, clad stainless | Acid attacks bare iron and aluminium | Unseasoned cast iron, uncoated aluminium |
| Stir fry | Carbon steel wok | Takes high heat, light enough to toss | Nonstick wok (coating fails at wok temperatures) |
| Braise, stew | Enameled Dutch oven | Retains heat, non-reactive, oven-safe | Thin stockpots |
| Stock, pasta | Tall stainless pot | Volume, no reactivity | Aluminium (clouds with acid) |
| Baking, roasting | Aluminium sheet pan | Even heat, cheap, no warping when heavy-gauge | Dark nonstick sheets (overbrown bases) |
| Sugar work, custard | Copper or heavy clad | Instant response to control temperature | Thin pans with hot spots |

## What About Nonstick

Nonstick is a coating, not a material, and it is the one category where the useful life is short and known.

PTFE is stable to about 500°F, but the coating begins to degrade at temperatures a pan can reach in two minutes on a high burner with nothing in it. The practical rules that come from that:

- **Never preheat empty above medium.** This is the single behaviour that destroys nonstick fastest.
- **No metal utensils**, and no stacking without a protector. A scratched pan releases more coating and sticks anyway.
- **Replace at the first visible scratch pattern**, not when food starts sticking. Typical life is one to three years with heavy use, longer if the pan is only used for eggs.
- **Ceramic-coated pans are not more durable than PTFE.** They are marketed as such, but the coating degrades faster in most testing, and the same high-heat rule applies.

The trade-off is worth naming: a nonstick pan does one thing better than anything else — eggs and delicate fish — and does everything else worse. Treat it as a specialist tool rather than the default pan.

## Seasoning, in One Paragraph

Cast iron and carbon steel are protected by polymerised oil, not by a factory coating. Oil heated past its smoke point bonds to the metal and forms a slick, hard layer. Every time you sear, you add to it. To maintain it: cook with fat, wash with hot water and a stiff brush (a little soap is fine — the lye concern is from an era of harsher soap), dry it on the stove, and wipe a thin film of oil while it is warm. If the seasoning is stripped, reseason by baking thin oil layers at 450°F for an hour each - [the cast iron cleaning guide](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/clean-cast-iron-skillet/) covers the everyday version of the same routine.

Enameled cast iron and clad stainless need no seasoning and cannot be seasoned — a dark patina on stainless is polymerised oil and can be removed with a cleaner, but it does no harm.

## Warping, Cracking, and Thermal Shock

Pan failures are mostly thermal, not mechanical.

**Warping** happens when a thin pan is heated hard on a small burner or cooled suddenly. Disc-bottom pans — aluminium or copper discs pressed onto a stainless body — warp at the disc seam and then rock on the burner forever. Heavy-gauge and fully clad pans resist it.

**Thermal shock** is the dangerous one. Pouring cold water into a hot enameled pot can craze or chip the enamel; the same into a hot glass baking dish can shatter it. Let both cool on the stove before washing.

**Induction** requires a magnetic base. Cast iron and carbon steel always work. Stainless works if it is magnetic — test with a fridge magnet. Aluminium and copper do not, unless they have a steel plate bonded into the base, which most clad cookware does.

## The Four Pans That Cover Almost Everything

Most kitchens have too many pans and not the right ones. Four do 95 percent of home cooking:

1. **A 10- to 12-inch skillet** — clad stainless if you want one pan that handles sauces and searing; cast iron or carbon steel if you want the best sear and do not mind the weight.
2. **A 2- to 3-quart saucepan** — clad stainless. Rice, grains, reheating, small batches of sauce.
3. **A 6- to 8-quart pot** — stainless or enameled. Pasta, stock, big batches.
4. **An 8- to 10-inch nonstick skillet** — eggs, fish, anything delicate.

Add a heavy aluminium sheet pan and a Dutch oven if you braise, and the kitchen is complete. Everything else is a preference, and [the gadget audit](/kitchen-tips/equipment/kitchen-gadgets-worth-the-space/) applies to pans as much as to anything else.

## FAQ

**Is stainless steel or cast iron better for steak?**

Cast iron, for the crust — it holds heat when the meat goes in. Clad stainless works if it is heavy and fully preheated, and it lets you deglaze in the same pan.

**Can I use a nonstick pan for high-heat searing?**

No. Empty or near-empty nonstick above medium heat degrades the coating, and the pan will not produce a crust anyway because the surface cannot exceed the coating's limit.

**Why does my stainless pan stick?**

It was not hot enough before the food went in. Heat the empty pan until a drop of water beads and skates, add oil, then add the food and do not move it until it releases on its own.

**Do I need to worry about aluminium and Alzheimer's?**

No. The link has been investigated repeatedly and not supported; the practical concern with bare aluminium is acidic food pitting the surface and tasting metallic, which is a quality issue.

**What pans work on induction?**

Anything magnetic: cast iron, carbon steel, magnetic stainless, and clad pans with a steel base. Test with a magnet.

**Can I put a cast iron skillet in the dishwasher?**

You can, and it will strip the seasoning and possibly rust. Wash by hand, dry on the stove, oil lightly.

**How long does a nonstick pan last?**

One to three years with regular use. Replace it when the coating is visibly scratched rather than waiting for food to stick.

## Bottom Line

Match the pan to the heat behaviour you need. Cast iron and carbon steel for searing, because they hold heat. Clad stainless for pan sauces and anything acidic, because it is non-reactive and responsive. Enamel for braises. Nonstick for eggs and fish, treated as a consumable with a two- to three-year life. Buy four good pans instead of twelve mediocre ones, and check any pan against a magnet before you buy it for an induction hob.
