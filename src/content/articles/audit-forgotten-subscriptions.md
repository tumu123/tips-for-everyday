Nobody sets out to pay $47 a month for a streaming service they stopped watching in March. It happens one small decision at a time, each one reasonable, and the total is invisible because it is spread across four payment methods and never appears as a single line anywhere.

This is why a subscription audit works better as a bank-statement exercise than a memory exercise — the same principle behind [tracking household spending](/save-money/shopping/track-household-spending/), applied only to the charges that recur. You will not remember all of them. The statements will tell you, if you read them in the right order and know where to look.

## Why This Is Worth an Hour

The average US household now spends somewhere between $60 and $120 a month on subscriptions of all kinds — streaming, software, storage, boxes, memberships, apps — and consumer research consistently finds that people underestimate their own total by a third or more. The gap between what people think they spend and what they actually spend is the recoverable money.

Two things make this different from ordinary budgeting. First, subscriptions are **recurring and small**, which is exactly the profile that escapes attention; a $6.99 charge is invisible in a way that a $300 purchase is not. Second, subscriptions are **designed not to be cancelled** — the whole commercial model depends on inertia. That is not a conspiracy, it is just the incentive structure, and the audit is the counter-move.

## Step 1: Build the Charge List From Statements, Not Memory

Take **twelve months** of statements, not one. Annual renewals are the ones that hurt most and they only appear once — a $119 annual plan that renews in September is invisible in an October statement review.

Sources to pull:

- **Primary checking account** — twelve months, transactions downloaded as a file if your bank offers it (most do; look for CSV, QFX, or Excel export).
- **Credit cards** — every card that is used for anything, including a store card that exists only for a discount.
- **PayPal, Apple Pay, Google Pay, Shop Pay** — these are where small app subscriptions hide, because the charge shows the processor rather than the merchant.
- **App stores.** On iPhone: Settings → your name → Subscriptions. On Android: Play Store → Payments & subscriptions. These lists are authoritative for anything billed through the store, and they are the only place some charges are visible.
- **Amazon** — "Subscribe & Save" and digital subscriptions are managed separately from regular orders.
- **Email search.** Search your inbox for "your subscription", "renewal", "auto-renew", "payment receipt", "we've updated your payment method". This finds the ones billed to a card you no longer check.

Now build one table. Columns: merchant, amount, frequency, payment method, what it is for, last time you used it, annual cost. The annual cost column is the point — a $9.99 monthly charge becomes $120 a year, and that reframing changes decisions more than any amount of willpower.

## Step 2: Find the Ones That Do Not Look Like Subscriptions

The entries that survive a casual review are the ones that do not announce themselves as subscriptions.

**Free trials that converted.** Every free trial is a subscription with a delay fuse. Search statements for the first charge date and check whether the service was ever actually used after the trial ended.

**Price increases you never approved.** A service that went from $9.99 to $12.99 sends an email most people never read. Compare the amount this year against the same merchant last year — the audit table makes this visible.

**Bundled subscriptions inside other bills.** Phone plans with a streaming service included, Amazon Prime with a music tier added, a credit card with a $95 annual fee that made sense for the first year's bonus and has not since. The mobile bundle is usually the largest of these, and [the cell phone bill breakdown](/save-money/bills/lower-cell-phone-bill/) shows how to price it. These are not separate charges; they are higher amounts on a charge you already recognize.

**Annual memberships.** Warehouse clubs, roadside assistance, a museum, a professional association, a domain name you registered once, hosting for a site you abandoned. All annual, all easy to miss.

**Dormant app subscriptions.** A meditation app, a language app, a photo editor, a fitness app, a kids' learning app. Small, monthly, and stopped being used months ago. These are usually the largest category by count.

**Duplicate services.** Two cloud storage plans, two music services, three streaming services with overlapping catalogues. Duplication is common in households where each person subscribed separately.

**Charges from a former address or a former partner.** A gym membership, a storage unit, a security monitoring plan for a house you sold. Worth checking specifically after a move.

## Step 3: Score Each One With Three Questions

For each row, answer:

1. **Did I use it in the last 30 days?**
2. **Would I subscribe again today, at this price, knowing what I know?**
3. **Is there a cheaper way to get the same thing** — an annual plan, a family plan, a bundle, the ad-supported tier, or the free tier?

Then sort into four piles:

**Keep.** Used recently, worth the price. Note whether the annual plan would save money — many services discount 15% to 20% for annual billing. Do the arithmetic both ways: an annual plan only saves money if you would have kept it for the whole year, which for uncertain services is not a given.

**Downgrade.** Used, but the premium tier is not earning its price. Ad-supported tiers, a lower storage tier, or dropping extra seats frequently halves the cost with no real loss. This is the pile people skip, and it is often where the money is, because the service itself is worth keeping.

**Pause.** Some services allow a one-to-three-month pause rather than cancellation. Useful for a service you use seasonally — a sports package, a gardening app, a language course you do in bursts.

**Cancel.** Everything else. If you hesitated on question 2, the answer is cancel. The cost of re-subscribing later is a few clicks; there is no scarcity.

## Step 4: Cancel in the Right Order

Cancellation is where good intentions fail, because the friction is deliberate. Work in this order, easiest first, so you get momentum:

**App store subscriptions first.** These cancel in two taps and stop immediately, with no retention conversation. If a service offers both direct and in-app billing, cancelling the store subscription is faster, but check which one you are actually billed through — cancelling the wrong one does nothing.

**PayPal and payment-processor subscriptions.** These have their own subscription management screens, separate from the merchant. Cancel the agreement there as well as with the merchant, or you can end up with a "cancelled" account that keeps billing.

**Merchant websites next.** Expect a retention flow: a discount offer, a pause offer, a "are you sure" screen, sometimes a chat requirement. Take the discount only if you were going to keep it anyway — a 50% discount on something you do not use is still a waste, just a smaller one. For the accounts you keep, the same leverage applies to the bigger bills: [which bills you can actually negotiate](/save-money/bills/negotiate-household-bills/) is a shorter list than most people assume.

**Phone, cable, and anything with a contract last.** These have early termination fees and require a call. Handle them when you are not rushed, and get the confirmation number.

**Then verify.** Screenshot the cancellation confirmation and save it. Check next month's statement for the charge. If it appears, you have written proof and a chargeback is straightforward.

Two practical notes. If a cancellation requires a phone call and you cannot face it, a virtual card number that you cancel is a legitimate fallback — the payment fails and the service lapses, though you should still cancel the account to avoid collections on an annual contract. And if a service will not let you cancel online in a state that requires it (California and several others have auto-renewal statutes), quoting the statute usually resolves it in one message.

## Step 5: Prevent the Next Round

An audit is a snapshot; the problem recurs. Three mechanisms that actually hold:

**One payment method for all subscriptions.** A single card or virtual number used only for subscriptions. The statement for that card is a complete subscription list, and the audit takes five minutes instead of an hour. This alone is the highest-value change in the whole process.

**A calendar entry 3 days before each renewal.** Not the renewal date — three days before, so you have time to decide. For annual services, this is the difference between paying $119 for another year and choosing not to.

**A rule about free trials.** A trial that does not get a calendar entry at sign-up is a subscription. Put the cancellation date in the calendar before you enter the card number, and use a virtual card with a low limit for trials where possible.

**The 30-day rule for new subscriptions.** Any new recurring charge is a decision worth one month of reflection. Most are not worth it; the ones that are, are obvious after a month.

## A Realistic Outcome

A household that goes through this exercise typically finds $25 to $70 a month in charges it does not want, plus another $10 to $30 in downgrades it does want to keep. That is $400 to $1,200 a year — more than most people save by changing energy suppliers, and it takes one afternoon every six months.

The reason it feels smaller than it is: subscriptions are priced below the level where people notice, and paid in twelve instalments. Converting to annual cost is the only honest way to look at them.

## What NOT to Do

- **Do not audit from memory.** You will miss a third of them, and the missed ones are the ones you forgot, which are exactly the ones to cancel.
- **Do not review one month of statements.** Annual renewals hide in the other eleven.
- **Do not accept a retention discount on something you were going to cancel.** It converts a clean saving into a smaller ongoing cost.
- **Do not cancel a service whose annual plan has already renewed** and assume a refund. Most do not pro-rate; some do within 14 to 30 days. Check the terms before you cancel.
- **Do not forget the bundled ones** inside phone, internet, and card annual fees.
- **Do not replace subscriptions with more subscriptions.** The audit is not a shopping opportunity.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often should I do this?**
Twice a year, with the calendar reminders running continuously in between. A full audit annually plus a five-minute monthly scan of the single subscription card covers it.

**Where do most forgotten subscriptions hide?**
App stores, PayPal, and annual renewals. App store subscriptions are the most numerous; annual renewals are the most expensive per instance.

**Can I get a refund for a subscription I forgot to cancel?**
Sometimes. Many services refund within 14 to 30 days of a renewal if the account has not been used. It costs one message to ask, and the answer is often yes for a first request.

**What if the merchant will not cancel it?**
Cancel the payment method as well, in writing, and keep the record. For a card charge you did not authorize after cancellation, a chargeback with the cancellation confirmation attached is usually successful.

**Is it worth cancelling a $3 app?**
Individually, marginally. In aggregate, four $3 apps are $144 a year. Cancel them as a group rather than judging them one at a time.

**Should I switch to annual plans to save money?**
Only for services you are confident you will use for the full year. The annual discount of 15% to 20% is real, but a service cancelled after four months on an annual plan costs more, not less.

**Do subscription audits affect my credit?**
Cancelling does not. Failing to pay a contracted annual commitment can, if it is sent to collections. Cancel properly rather than letting a payment method fail on a contract.

## Bottom Line

Pull twelve months of statements from every payment method, add the app store subscription lists, and put everything in one table with an annual cost column. Score each row on use and intent, then cancel in order of easiest first: app store, processor, merchant, contract. Then move every subscription to a single card and put a reminder three days before each renewal. The audit saves money once; the single-card habit is what keeps it saved.
