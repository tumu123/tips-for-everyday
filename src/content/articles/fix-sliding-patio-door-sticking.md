A sliding door that has become hard to move is rarely a broken door. In the large majority of cases it is dirt in the track, or rollers that have worn or dropped out of adjustment.

**The diagnostic order matters, because the cheap fix is also the most common one.** Cleaning the track costs nothing and takes fifteen minutes. Replacing rollers means taking the door out, which is heavy, awkward work. Check the cheap cause first.

**Why the door gets worse gradually:** the rollers at the bottom of the door carry the whole weight, and they run in a track that sits at floor level where dust, sand, pet hair, and grit collect. That mixture packs into the track and into the roller itself. The door does not suddenly break — it gets progressively harder as the resistance builds.

## Step 0: Find Out Where the Resistance Is

Slide the door slowly and pay attention to **where** it resists.

**Resistance the whole way** → track is dirty, or rollers are worn throughout.

**Resistance at one specific spot** → there is a dent, a raised section, or debris in the track at that point. Look closely there.

**The door drags on one side only** → the door has dropped on that side; check the roller adjustment on that corner.

**The door scrapes at the top** → the rollers have dropped too far, or the header track is damaged.

**The door will not move at all and feels stuck** → check whether it is locked, then check the track for an obstruction, and check whether a roller has jumped out of the track.

## Step 1: Clean the Track Thoroughly

Open the door fully and vacuum the entire exposed track with a crevice tool.

**Get into the corners and the ends.** Debris accumulates where the door does not travel, and it gets pushed back into the travel path the next time the door moves.

Scrub the track with a stiff brush and mild detergent. Use an old toothbrush for the corners and the rail profile.

Pay particular attention to the **raised rail** the roller runs on — that is the surface that matters. It should be smooth and continuous. Run your finger along it.

Wipe dry and vacuum again.

## Step 2: Inspect the Rail for Damage

With the track clean, look at the rail closely in good light.

**Look for flat spots, dents, and raised burrs.** A damaged steel rail will wear out new rollers quickly, so this matters before you spend money on rollers.

A small burr can be dressed carefully with a file. Significant damage to the rail is a bigger job — sometimes a new track can be fitted over the old one, sometimes the door frame is involved.

If the rail is sound, continue.

## Step 3: Try Lubrication — But Use the Right Kind

**Use a dry silicone spray or a wax-based lubricant. Do not use oil or grease.**

This is the same principle as drawer slides: the track is open to dust, and a wet lubricant turns tracked-in grit into a grinding paste that accelerates wear. Oil makes a sliding door better for a week and worse for a year.

Spray the rail and the rollers lightly, then work the door back and forth to distribute it.

If the door now moves freely, you are done. In many cases this plus cleaning is the whole repair.

## Step 4: Adjust the Roller Height

If cleaning and lubrication did not fix it, the rollers probably need adjusting.

Look at the **bottom edge of the door**, near each lower corner. There is usually a small hole or slot giving access to an adjustment screw.

Turning that screw raises or lowers that corner of the door relative to its roller.

**Adjust in small increments — a quarter turn at a time — and test the door after each.** It is easy to overshoot, and a door raised too far will scrape the top track.

Use a level to check the door is square in the opening, and aim for an even gap at the top and along the latch side.

If adjustment does not change anything, the roller is likely seized or worn.

## Step 5: Inspect the Rollers

With the track clean, watch the rollers as someone slides the door.

**A healthy roller turns.** If a roller skids rather than rolls, it is seized or has developed a flat spot.

You can often see the roller by looking into the track from the end, or by lifting the door slightly at that corner.

If the roller is visibly worn, cracked, or has a flat side, it needs replacing.

## Step 6: Remove the Door (With a Helper)

Sliding door panels are heavy — often 25 to 45 kg — and awkward. Get a second person.

Adjust the rollers to their lowest setting first if your door allows it. That gives clearance to lift the panel out.

**Remove the door from the top first.** With the door partly open, lift it up into the header track, then swing the bottom out toward you and lower it down.

Lay the panel flat on a protected surface — a blanket or cardboard — with the roller edge accessible.

Do not lay it on the handle side, and do not lean it against a wall where it can fall.

## Step 7: Replace the Roller

The roller assembly is usually held in the bottom rail by one or two screws, sometimes accessible from the side of the door through a slot.

Remove the screws and slide or pull the old roller out.

**Take the old roller with you when buying a replacement.** Sliding door hardware is not standardised; the same-looking roller comes in different widths, wheel diameters, and mountings. Match the old part physically rather than by description.

Fit the new roller, reinstall the door by reversing the removal — bottom in first, then lift into the header — and re-adjust the height.

## Step 8: Check the Locking Side and Weatherstripping

Once the door slides properly, check that it still closes and latches.

If the door slides well but now does not seal, the strike alignment may have moved with the roller adjustment. Adjust the strike plate as needed.

**Also check the [weatherstripping](/fix-it/maintenance/seal-gaps-doors-windows/).** A door that was hard to slide for a long time has often had its weatherstripping worn through by dragging. If you feel a draft now that the door moves, that is the next thing to address.

## Why This Works

The order of operations is the whole method: cheapest and most probable first, invasive and expensive last.

Dirt is the most common cause because the track is a horizontal channel at floor level with no cover — it is designed to collect whatever the floor has. The roller is a small wheel carrying a heavy door, so even a modest increase in resistance is very noticeable at the handle. That is why a cleaning that looks trivial can transform how a door feels.

Lubricant choice follows from the same environment. The track will keep collecting grit, so the goal is a surface that sheds it, not one that holds it. Dry lubes do that; oil does the opposite.

Roller adjustment works because the door hangs on the rollers, and the door's height in the frame is set by them. Over years the adjustment creeps and the door settles, which changes the clearances at the top and bottom. Adjustment restores the designed geometry without replacing anything.

And replacement is last because it is the only step that requires taking a heavy panel out — which is worth avoiding if the actual problem was a dirty track.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not use oil or grease on the track.** It collects grit and accelerates wear. Use dry silicone or wax.

**Do not replace rollers before cleaning the track.** In many cases cleaning alone fixes it.

**Do not force a stuck door.** If it does not move, find out why. Forcing can derail a roller or bend the frame.

**Do not try to remove the panel alone.** These doors are heavy and awkward, and a dropped panel is dangerous and expensive.

**Do not adjust rollers more than a quarter turn at a time.** Overshooting causes top-track scraping, which looks like a new problem.

**Do not buy rollers by description.** Take the old one and match it physically.

**Do not ignore a damaged rail.** New rollers on a damaged rail wear out fast.

**Do not forget to check the lock after adjusting.** Changing door height changes latch alignment.

## FAQ

**Why is my sliding door suddenly hard to open when it was fine last week?**
Usually a specific obstruction — a stone, a toy, or debris pushed into the travel path — or a roller that has jumped its rail. Check the track at the point where it resists.

**Can I adjust rollers without removing the door?**
Yes, on most doors. The adjustment screw is accessed through a small hole in the bottom edge. Removal is only needed to replace a roller.

**How do I know when rollers need replacing?**
If the roller skids instead of rolling, has a visible flat spot, is cracked, or the adjustment screw no longer changes the door height.

**How long do sliding door rollers last?**
Commonly ten to twenty years depending on use, door weight, and how clean the track has been kept. A dirty track shortens that considerably.

**Should I lubricate the track regularly?**
Lightly, once or twice a year, with a dry lubricant. Combined with cleaning, that is the best thing you can do for roller life.

**Why does the door rattle in the wind now?**
Often because the door is sitting higher than before after adjustment, or the weatherstripping has worn. Check both.

**Is a sticking sliding door a security problem?**
Indirectly, yes. A door that does not close and latch reliably is a door that may not actually be locked. Fix it rather than living with it.

**Can I clean the track without moving the door?**
You can clean the exposed portion, but you will miss the ends where debris accumulates most. Open the door fully to reach them.
