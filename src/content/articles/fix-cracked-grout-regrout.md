[Grout](/home-hacks/cleaning/clean-grout-keep-white/) cracks for a reason. If you do not identify that reason first, new grout will crack in the same place — usually within months.

**The most important distinction is where the crack is:**

| Location | What it actually means | Correct fix |
|---|---|---|
| A corner where two planes meet (wall to wall, wall to floor, wall to tub) | Movement. Grout is rigid and cannot accommodate it | Caulk, not grout |
| A flat field of tile, a single crack | Possible substrate movement or deflection | Investigate before regrouting |
| A flat field, widespread crumbling | Old grout, wrong mix, or long-term water exposure | Regrout |

**The corner case is the one people get wrong most often.** Tile corners always move slightly as the house settles and as materials expand and contract. Grout is cement-based and rigid, so it cracks there by design — no grout survives that location. Every change-of-plane joint should be caulked, and if yours was grouted, that is the root cause.

## Step 0: Diagnose the Crack

Take a photo and note the pattern.

**Cracks at every change of plane** → normal movement. Remove grout and caulk those joints. No amount of new grout fixes this.

**A single crack running across a flat wall** → check whether it follows a straight line across several tiles. If it does, it may indicate movement in the substrate — a board joint, or deflection in the floor below.

**Crumbling that you can scrape out with a fingernail** → the grout has degraded. This is a straightforward regrout.

**Cracks that reappear after you have already regrouted once** → almost certainly substrate movement, and regrouting again will fail again.

## Step 1: Check the Substrate Before You Regrout

This is the step that prevents doing the job twice.

**For floor tile**, check for deflection. Tile is brittle and the substrate beneath it flexes; if the floor moves underfoot, grout and tile will keep cracking. A squeak or a soft spot is a warning sign.

**For wall tile**, press on the tiles near the crack. If any tile moves or sounds hollow, the bond has failed and that tile needs resetting before the grout goes in.

**Check for water damage.** In showers, persistent cracking along the lower walls or the floor often means water has been getting through. Soft or crumbly backing board needs addressing before any cosmetic work.

If you find substrate movement, fixing that is the real job, and regrouting is only the finish.

## Step 2: Remove the Old Grout

Remove grout to a depth of at least half the joint depth, ideally two-thirds.

**A manual grout saw** works fine for small areas and is more controllable. **An oscillating multi-tool with a grout blade** is much faster for a whole shower, but it is aggressive and can chip tile edges if you are careless — go slowly at first.

Hold the tool in the joint, not against the tile face. Wear eye protection; grout chips fly.

Vacuum out the joints thoroughly when you are done. Loose dust left in the joint prevents the new grout from bonding.

## Step 3: Choose the Right Grout

Two decisions matter.

**Sanded or unsanded:** sanded grout for joints wider than about 3 mm; unsanded for narrower joints and for polished or soft stone that sanded grout would scratch.

**Cementitious or epoxy:** standard cement grout is easier to work and fine for most situations. Epoxy grout resists staining and water far better, costs more, and is much less forgiving to apply. For a shower floor or a backsplash behind a sink, epoxy is worth considering.

**Get the colour right before you start.** Grout dries lighter than it looks wet, and "matching" an existing colour after years of ageing is unreliable. Test a small inconspicuous area if colour matters.

## Step 4: Decide Grout or Caulk, Joint by Joint

Walk every joint and sort them.

**Change-of-plane joints get 100% silicone caulk.** Wall-to-wall corners, wall-to-floor, wall-to-tub, wall-to-shower-base. All of them.

**Field joints get grout.**

This is not a shortcut. Silicone stays flexible and accommodates the movement that cracks rigid grout. It is the correct material for that location and it is what professionals use there.

Do the caulked joints after the grout has cured.

## Step 5: Mix and Apply

Mix the grout to the consistency the package specifies — usually like peanut butter, not soupy. Too wet weakens the grout and causes shrinkage cracking.

**Let it slake** — most cementitious grouts need to sit for several minutes after mixing, then be remixed before use. Skipping this changes the working properties.

Using a grout float held at an angle, press grout firmly into the joints. Work diagonally across the joints rather than parallel to them, so you are pushing grout into the joint rather than dragging it out.

Work in sections of about a square metre. Overfill — you will remove the excess.

## Step 6: Clean Off at the Right Moment

This is where most DIY regrouting looks amateur.

Wait until the grout film hazes over on the tile surface — typically ten to twenty minutes, depending on temperature and humidity.

**Then wipe with a damp grout sponge, using light pressure and clean water.** Rinse the sponge constantly and change the water often.

**Too early and you pull grout out of the joints. Too late and the haze sets hard and is very difficult to remove.** If you haze too soon, wait — do not scrub.

Wipe each section once and move on. Repeated wiping of the same area pulls grout out.

After it fully cures, buff off any remaining haze with a dry cloth.

## Step 7: Let It Cure, Then Seal

Follow the cure time on the package before exposing it to water. Rushing this is a common cause of a job that looks fine and then fails.

**Apply grout sealer once cured.** Cementitious grout is porous and will absorb water and stains without it. Sealing is not optional in a wet area.

Note that sealer is a maintenance item, not a permanent treatment — it needs reapplying, typically annually in a shower.

## Step 8: Caulk the Movement Joints

Using 100% silicone, caulk every change-of-plane joint.

Remove any grout in those joints first. Clean and dry the joint thoroughly — silicone does not bond to dust or to wet surfaces.

Apply a consistent bead, tool it smooth, and remove painter's tape immediately after tooling rather than after the caulk skins.

**Use 100% silicone, not acrylic latex or "painter's caulk."** In a wet area, anything less will fail.

## Why This Works

Cracked grout is a symptom, and the treatment depends entirely on which of two mechanisms produced it.

The first mechanism is movement, and movement is unavoidable at changes of plane. Two rigid surfaces meeting at an angle will shift relative to each other as the structure settles and as materials respond to temperature and humidity. Grout cannot stretch, so it fails there every time. Caulk can stretch, which is why it is the correct material in that location — this is not a compromise, it is the specification.

The second mechanism is degradation. Cementitious grout is porous, and in a shower it is wet repeatedly. Over years, water and cleaning products break down the binder, and the grout becomes sandy and crumbles. That is a material-at-end-of-life problem, and new grout genuinely fixes it.

The reason to diagnose first is that these two mechanisms look identical from the outside but have opposite treatments. Caulking a degraded field joint leaves crumbling grout in place. Regrouting a movement joint guarantees you will do it again.

Removing grout to proper depth matters because new grout needs depth to bond mechanically. A thin skim of new grout over old has almost nothing to grip and flakes out — which is exactly the failure people see when they "refresh" grout without removing it properly.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not grout a change-of-plane corner.** It will crack. Use silicone.

**Do not regrout without checking the substrate.** Loose tiles, deflection, or water damage will crack new grout.

**Do not use acrylic caulk in a shower.** Use 100% silicone.

**Do not wipe the haze too early.** You will pull grout out of the joints. Wait for the haze.

**Do not mix grout too wet.** It weakens and shrinks.

**Do not use sanded grout on polished stone or narrow joints.** It scratches and does not pack properly.

**Do not skip the sealer.** Unsealed cementitious grout stains and absorbs water.

**Do not expose new grout to water before it cures.** Follow the stated cure time.

**Do not use a metal tool that can chip tile edges** without care near glazed edges.

## FAQ

**Why does grout crack in corners but not on flat walls?**
Corners are where two planes meet and move relative to each other. Grout is rigid and cannot flex, so it fails there. Caulk is the correct material for those joints.

**Should I caulk or grout where the tub meets the tile?**
Caulk. Always. That joint moves more than almost any other in a bathroom.

**How deep should I remove old grout?**
At least half the joint depth, ideally two-thirds. A thin skim over old grout will not bond.

**Can I regrout over the top of existing grout?**
No. It will not bond properly and will flake out.

**How long does new grout last?**
Cementitious grout, sealed and maintained, commonly ten years or more. Epoxy considerably longer. Unsealed grout in a shower degrades much faster.

**Do I need to seal epoxy grout?**
No. Epoxy is non-porous and does not require sealing, which is part of why it costs more.

**Why did my grout crack again after I regrouted?**
Almost certainly substrate movement that was not addressed, or a change-of-plane joint that should have been caulked.

**Can I regrout just the cracked area?**
Yes for isolated damage, but colour-matching aged grout is difficult. Regrouting a whole continuous area often looks better than patching.

**Is cracked grout in a shower urgent?**
If it is letting water reach the substrate, yes — that becomes a much larger repair. If it is surface crumbling in a dry area, it is cosmetic and can wait.
