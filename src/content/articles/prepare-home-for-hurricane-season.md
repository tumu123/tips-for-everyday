Hurricanes are described as wind events and remembered as water events. The gap between those two facts is where most of the damage happens.

Wind opens a house. Once an opening fails  a window, a garage door, a sliding door  the wind pressurizes the interior, and the pressure lifts the roof from underneath. A house that loses its roof is usually a house that lost a window first. Water, meanwhile, arrives three ways: rain driven through failed openings, storm surge pushing in from the coast, and freshwater flooding from rainfall hundreds of miles inland. Surge and flooding cause the majority of deaths, and none of the wind fixes address them.

So the prep divides cleanly: **seal the envelope for wind, understand your zone for water, and decide evacuation on elevation rather than on how you feel about the forecast.**

## Step 1: Find out your flood zone and your evacuation zone

Two different maps, two different answers, and both matter more than any hardware purchase.

- **FEMA flood zone** tells you the risk of flooding from rainfall and surge, and it determines your insurance requirement. Zones AE and VE are high risk; X is moderate to low. Look it up by address, not by neighborhood reputation.
- **Your state's evacuation zone** is usually lettered or colored, and it determines when you are told to leave. Know your zone letter and the route. Most coastal states publish both in one place.

Write both on a card and put it in the document folder. In an approaching storm, the local emergency management feed will reference zones by letter, and households that do not know theirs spend the last 12 hours guessing.

One more number: **your home's elevation relative to the nearest water.** If you are in a surge zone and the forecast calls for 8 feet of surge, no amount of sandbagging substitutes for leaving.

## Step 2: Secure the openings, in the right order

The openings are ranked by area, and the biggest one is the one people forget.

1. **Garage door.** A standard 16-foot double garage door is the largest opening in the house and fails at wind speeds well below what the windows handle. A bracing kit  vertical posts plus horizontal beams  is $200 to $400 and installs in an hour.
2. **Windows and glass doors.** Commercial roll-down or accordion shutters are the best option if you are staying long term. Rated plywood, 5/8 inch, cut and labeled per opening with anchors pre-installed, is the practical alternative.
3. **Entry doors.** Most are rated for pressure but not for impact. Add a deadbolt that engages 1 inch into the frame and check that the hinges are through-bolted.
4. **Sliding glass doors.** The track is the weak point. A cut-to-length dowel or a manufactured brace in the inner track stops the panel from being lifted out.

Cut and label the plywood **before** the season, in dry weather, and store it with the anchors in a labeled bundle. Every year, people cut plywood in 40 mph wind with a line at the hardware store, and every year some of them get hurt doing it.

## Step 3: Fix the roof-to-wall connection if you can

This is the structural difference between a house that loses shingles and a house that loses its roof. In older homes, roof trusses are often attached with a few nails rather than hurricane straps.

You can inspect this from the attic  look at where each truss meets the top plate. If you see nails only, a strap retrofit (simpson-style connectors, installed from inside the attic) is a real improvement and does not require re-roofing.

Three cheaper roof items also matter:

- **Check the shingle field for lifted or missing tabs** before the season. A single lifted shingle starts a peel that takes the whole slope.
- **Seal the gable end vents** if they are the unrated louver type. They are a common entry point for wind-driven rain.
- **Clear the gutters and downspouts.** In a 6-inch rain event, a clogged downspout puts water against the foundation instead of away from it.

## Step 4: Deal with everything that can become a projectile

Anything outside becomes airborne at 60 to 80 mph, and a lawn chair does as much damage as a branch when it hits a window.

Two weeks before a storm is forecast, not two days:

- **Furniture:** bring in or strap down. Straps rated for wind, anchored to a wall or ground anchor  not bungee cords.
- **Grills and propane tanks:** inside the garage, valve closed, tank secured upright.
- **Trash bins:** empty, inside, or strapped. A full bin is a sail.
- **Garden pots:** group them against a wall and lay them on their sides.
- **Loose fence panels and gates:** brace or remove. A gate that swings free destroys its own posts.
- **Trees:** remove dead limbs over the roof and driveway now. Arborist schedules fill two weeks before a storm.

## Step 5: Prepare for water you cannot stop

In a surge or flood zone, the goal shifts from keeping water out to limiting what it destroys and being able to leave.

- **Know what is at floor level.** Move documents, electronics, photos, and medications above the expected water line  3 feet is a reasonable default for a single-story home.
- **Elevate the utilities** if you are in a repetitively flooded area: water heater, furnace, and electrical panel above base flood elevation is code in many jurisdictions now, and it is the difference between a repair and a rebuild.
- **Backflow valve** on the sewer line, if your area floods. Sewage backup is the most unpleasant and least prepared-for part of flooding.
- **Sandbags buy hours, not days.** They are useful for a 6-inch sheet of water at a door, and useless against 3 feet of surge.
- **Never store fuel or chemicals at floor level** in a flood zone. They become a contamination problem after the water leaves.

## Step 6: Build a kit sized for seven days, not three

Hurricane aftermath means roads closed, stores shut, and power out for a week or more. Three days of supplies is the federal minimum recommendation; in practice, coastal households should plan for seven.

- **Water:** 1 gallon per person per day, plus pets. Fill the bathtub and every food-grade container before landfall if you are staying.
- **Food:** non-perishables that need no cooking, plus a manual can opener. A camp stove is for outdoors only.
- **Prescriptions:** a full refill before the storm, plus a paper list. Pharmacies close and systems go down.
- **Documents:** IDs, insurance policies, deeds, and a photographed inventory of the house in a waterproof bag. This is the item that determines whether a claim goes smoothly.
- **Cash:** small bills. Card readers fail when power and network both do.
- **Power:** charged banks, a car charger, and if you have a generator, fuel for three days of intermittent use.

## Step 7: Photograph the house before the storm

This takes forty minutes and is the highest-value prep that involves no hardware.

Walk every room and photograph walls, floors, and ceilings. Photograph the exterior from all four sides. Open closets and cabinets and photograph the contents. Photograph serial numbers on appliances and the receipts for anything expensive.

Then email the images to yourself and to your insurer. A pre-storm photo set establishes condition, which is exactly what a claims adjuster needs to distinguish storm damage from pre-existing wear. Households that skip this spend months arguing about a roof that was already 15 years old.

## Step 8: Decide the evacuation trigger now

The worst evacuation decisions are made at 11 p.m. with the wind rising. Decide the trigger in May.

A workable rule set:

- **Evacuate for any mandatory order, immediately.** Orders are issued on surge modeling, not on wind speed, and they are the single most reliable predictor of where people die.
- **Leave voluntarily if you are in a surge zone and the storm is forecast at Category 2 or above**, even without an order, if you have somewhere to go and a way to get there.
- **Leave if you depend on electricity for medical reasons**, regardless of category.
- **Stay only if** you are outside surge zones, your house is rated, you have supplies for seven days, and you can be genuinely self-sufficient  including no expectation of emergency services.

Write the plan down: where you go, which route, who drives, what comes with you, and who you call when you arrive. Include the pets  shelters often do not accept them, and that detail derails more evacuations than any other.

## Step 9: During the storm, do the counterintuitive things

- **Stay away from windows**, even rated ones. Interior room, no windows, lowest floor that is not flood-prone.
- **Fill the bathtub before landfall** if you are staying, for flushing water.
- **Turn the fridge and freezer to the coldest setting** ahead of a likely outage, and do not open them once the power goes.
- **Do not go outside during the eye.** The calm is the center, and the wind returns from the opposite direction within minutes.
- **Keep a battery radio on** and ignore social media video, which is almost always from somewhere else.

## Step 10: After the storm, in this order

1. **Do not drive or walk through floodwater.** Six inches of moving water knocks an adult down; twelve inches moves a car. The water is also full of debris and sewage.
2. **Check for gas leaks and structural damage from outside** before entering. If you smell gas or see a sagging roof line, leave and call.
3. **Photograph everything before cleanup**, including the water line on the walls.
4. **Assume tap water is unsafe** until the utility says otherwise. Boil or use bottled.
5. **Run a generator outdoors only**, 20 feet from openings, and expect CO cases to spike in the days after landfall.
6. **Remove wet drywall and insulation within 48 hours** to prevent mold. The clock on mold starts when the water leaves, not when the storm does.

## Why This Works

The plan separates the two hazards and gives each its own set of actions. Wind is addressed by securing the envelope in order of opening size, because internal pressurization is what actually removes roofs. Water is addressed by knowing the zone and pre-deciding to leave, because no amount of preparation stops 8 feet of surge.

The pre-storm photo set and the document folder are the part that pays off after the event rather than during it. Most hurricane losses are compounded by claims disputes, and disputes are won or lost on dated evidence.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Taping windows | Does nothing structurally and wastes time | Rated shutters or 5/8-inch plywood, anchored |
| Ignoring the garage door | Largest opening; failure pressurizes the house and lifts the roof | Bracing kit installed before the season |
| Judging risk by wind category | Surge and freshwater flooding cause most deaths | Know your flood and evacuation zone |
| Cutting plywood as the storm approaches | Dangerous, and the store is out of materials | Cut, label, and store in May |
| Sandbagging against surge | Buys hours against inches, not feet | Elevate valuables, plan to leave |
| Staying to protect property | Emergency services cannot reach you at peak | Evacuate on the order, not on the forecast |
| Leaving pets out of the plan | Most shelters do not accept animals | Identify pet-friendly lodging in advance |
| No pre-storm photos | Claims disputes over pre-existing damage | Photograph every room and the exterior |
| Driving through floodwater | 12 inches moves a car; water hides debris | Turn around |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the most important thing to do before a hurricane?**

Secure the largest opening first  the garage door  then the windows, and decide your evacuation trigger based on your flood zone rather than the wind category. Structural failure almost always begins at an opening, and deaths overwhelmingly come from water, not wind.

**How do you protect windows from a hurricane?**

Commercial roll-down or accordion shutters if you are staying long term. Otherwise 5/8-inch plywood cut and labeled per opening, with anchors pre-installed, mounted before the storm. Taping glass provides no protection and takes time you may not have.

**Should you open windows during a hurricane to equalize pressure?**

No. That is a myth. Opening a window lets wind-driven rain in and can increase internal pressure. Keep the house closed and stay in an interior room away from glass.

**How much water do you need for a hurricane?**

One gallon per person per day, plus pets, for at least seven days if you are staying through the aftermath. Fill bathtubs and food-grade containers before landfall for flushing and washing. If you evacuate, three days in the car is enough.

**When should you evacuate?**

Immediately on any mandatory order. Voluntarily if you are in a surge zone and the storm is forecast at Category 2 or higher, or if anyone in the household depends on electricity for medical reasons. Do not wait to see how the forecast trends.

**Is it better to stay or leave?**

Leave if you are in a surge zone, in a mobile home, or dependent on powered medical equipment. Stay only if you are outside surge zones, the house is rated, you have seven days of supplies, and you accept that no one is coming to help during the peak.

**How do you prepare a house for a hurricane if you are leaving?**

Shut off the water and gas at the mains, unplug electronics, move valuables above the expected water line, secure or bring in everything outdoors, close and brace the garage door, and take the document folder and photos with you. Turn the fridge to its coldest setting and leave it closed.

**What should you do first after a hurricane?**

Check for gas leaks and structural damage from outside before entering, then photograph everything before cleanup. Do not drive through floodwater. Assume tap water is unsafe until told otherwise, and remove wet drywall within 48 hours to prevent mold.
