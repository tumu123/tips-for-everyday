Most cheese is stored in one of two ways, and both are wrong in opposite directions. Wrapped tightly in plastic, it suffocates and develops ammonia and off flavors. Left uncovered on a shelf, it dries into a husk.

Cheese wants something that sounds contradictory: **it needs to exchange a little air while losing as little water as possible.** Once that clicks, the storage choices become obvious — and cheese that usually lasts a week starts lasting three.

---

## Step 1: Understand why plastic wrap is the problem

Cheese is alive. Even after cutting, the cultures in it keep working and releasing gas and moisture.

Wrap it in impermeable plastic and those compounds have nowhere to go. The cheese sits in its own byproducts:

- **Ammonia and sulfur notes** build up, which people usually describe as "it tastes like the fridge" or "it smells like feet."
- **Surface slime** develops where moisture collects against the cheese.
- **Plastic-derived flavors** actually transfer, particularly with softer, fattier cheeses.

Unwrap the same cheese and let it sit for ten minutes, and a lot of that blows off — proof that it was trapped volatiles rather than spoilage. But repeated cycles take a toll, which is why the fix is not "unwrap it sometimes" but "wrap it in the right material."

At the same time, cheese loses water constantly. A completely open wedge loses about 1% of its weight per day by evaporation, and after two days the cut face has a dry, slightly translucent rind that tastes like nothing.

**So the ideal wrapper breathes and retains moisture at the same time.** That material exists, and it isn't plastic wrap.

## Step 2: Use the two-layer method

This is the core technique and it works for nearly every cheese:

1. **Inner layer — cheese paper, parchment, or wax paper.** Wrap the cut piece snugly. Cheese paper (a laminate designed exactly for this) is ideal, but baking parchment is a fine substitute and much cheaper. Wax paper also works, slightly less well.
2. **Outer layer — a loosely fitting container or bag.** This slows evaporation without creating a seal. Leave it **open just a little**, or use a bag that isn't zipped all the way. You want a humid-but-breathing environment.

The reason the inner paper matters even though there's an outer container: paper wicks the small amount of condensation away from the cheese surface so it doesn't sit in water, while letting gas through.

Avoid stacking different cheeses in one container — see Step 5.

For very small pieces and the last crumbs of a wedge, paper plus a loose bag often isn't worth the fuss. Use those immediately in cooking rather than storing them.

## Step 3: Store each cheese at the temperature it likes

Your fridge is one temperature, but cheeses want different ones. Practically speaking, that means **choosing which shelf you use matters**:

| Type | Where it keeps best | Rough lifespan, well-wrapped |
|---|---|---|
| Hard aged (Parmesan, aged cheddar, Grana) | Coldest regular shelf | 4–6 months |
| Semi-hard (cheddar, gouda, Swiss, Gruyère) | Crisper drawer or lower shelf | 3–4 weeks |
| Semi-soft (havarti, fontina, young gouda) | Crisper drawer | 2–3 weeks |
| Bloomy rind (brie, camembert) | Crisper drawer, wrapped | 1–2 weeks, eat soon |
| Blue (roquefort, gorgonzola) | **Wrapped separately, own container** | 3–4 weeks |
| Fresh (mozzarella, burrata, ricotta, chèvre) | Coldest shelf, original brine if any | Days — plan around it |
| Processed cheese slices | Anywhere | Weeks, and barely worth optimizing |

The **crisper drawer** is usually the best all-purpose spot: it's the most humid part of the fridge and has the least temperature swing from opening the door. Avoid the fridge door for anything — it's the warmest spot and gets the most swings.

Put a fridge thermometer in it and check: cheese wants roughly **35–45°F (2–7°C)**. A fridge running warmer than that shortens every timeline above.

## Step 4: Handle cut faces and cut sizes strategically

How you cut affects how long things last:

- **Cut only what you need.** Each new cut face is another surface losing moisture and collecting mold spores. A 2 lb block keeps far better than four half-pound pieces cut from it.
- **Re-wrap immediately** after each use, with fresh paper if the old piece is wet. Reusing soggy paper defeats the purpose.
- **Use a clean knife per cheese.** This is genuinely important for blues and soft cheeses, where cross-contamination moves cultures around.
- **Trim the cut face** rather than storing it once it dries out. A thin slice off a dry face restores the taste immediately and costs very little.

For hard grating cheeses, don't pre-grate. Grated cheese has enormous surface area and goes stale and clumpy within days. Grate it as you need it.

## Step 5: Keep blues and strong cheeses separate

Blue cheeses are deliberately inoculated with *Penicillium roqueforti*, and they will happily colonize anything nearby. Store them:

- **In their own container**, wrapped first in cheese paper.
- **Away from** milder cheeses, especially soft ones.

Strong, aromatic cheeses (aged Gouda, Époisses, washed-rind cheeses like Taleggio) don't spread cultures the same way, but their volatile compounds absolutely migrate through shared air. A box of several cheeses together will slowly converge on one flavor — usually the strongest one's.

## Step 6: Deal with mold correctly

Mold on cheese is the most over-worried-about problem in the fridge, and also occasionally under-worried-about. The rule depends on **density**:

**Hard and firm cheeses** — cheddar, Parmesan, Gruyère, aged Gouda, firm salami. Mold can't penetrate far because the structure is dense. **Cut off at least one inch (2.5 cm) around and below the mold** and use the rest. Keep the knife out of the mold itself so you don't drag spores through the good part.

**Soft and semi-soft cheeses** — cottage cheese, ricotta, cream cheese, brie, camembert, blue-veined, shredded cheese, anything creamy. **Discard the whole thing.** Mold sends root threads through soft food well beyond what you can see, and the cheese is usually watery enough for those threads to travel the full depth.

**Anything with mold plus an off smell, slime, or pinkish tint** — discard regardless of type.

Note what this does not mean: the white bloom on a natural rind isn't necessarily mold you need to worry about, and blue cheese veining is intentional. Judge the unexpected, not the expected.

## Step 7: Freeze only when you know what you're trading

Freezing cheese is safe and much better than throwing it out, but it **destroys texture**. Ice crystals rupture the protein structure, and on thawing the cheese becomes crumbly and grainy.

- **Freeze for cooking only:** anything destined for a sauce, soup, gratins, or casserole.
- **Freeze hard cheeses best:** Parmesan, aged cheddar, Romano hold up better than soft ones.
- **Don't freeze:** brie, camembert, cottage cheese, ricotta, cream cheese, or anything high-moisture. They come back watery and separated.
- **Grate before freezing** hard cheeses so you can use them straight from frozen, which sidesteps the thawing problem entirely.
- **Portion it** — a 1 lb block of frozen cheddar is awkward to thaw and impossible to refreeze well.

## Why This Works

Cheese storage is a balance between two continuous processes: ongoing microbial activity that produces gas and moisture, and ongoing evaporation that removes water. Perfect storage blocks neither completely — it manages both.

Breathable paper lets volatile compounds escape so they don't accumulate into ammonia and off flavors, and it keeps moisture off the immediate surface so it doesn't slime. A loose outer layer slows evaporation enough that weeks of storage don't dry the cheese out. A tight plastic seal stops one process and accelerates the other, which is why it fails in both directions at once.

Temperature works through reaction rates: everything the cultures do, everything mold does, and everything the enzymes do happens faster when warmer. The crisper drawer is simply the coldest stable, high-humidity spot in most fridges.

The hard-versus-soft mold rule rests on physical structure. Dense cheese physically limits how far mold threads can penetrate; loose, high-moisture cheese offers no such barrier. That difference is real and consistent, which is why it's safe practice rather than folk wisdom.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Wrapping tightly in plastic wrap | Traps ammonia; flavors transfer | Cheese paper or parchment, then a loose container |
| Leaving cheese unwrapped on a shelf | Dries into a husk in a day or two | Same two-layer method |
| Storing cheese in the fridge door | Warmest, least stable part of the fridge | Crisper drawer or low shelf |
| Storing blues with milder cheeses | Blue cultures colonize everything nearby | Separate airtight container |
| Scraping mold off soft cheese and eating it | Roots extend invisibly through soft food | Discard soft cheeses entirely |
| Using one knife for all cheeses | Cross-contamination between them | Clean knife per cheese |
| Pre-grating a big batch | Huge surface area goes stale fast | Grate as needed |
| Freezing brie, ricotta, or cottage cheese | Texture destroyed; comes back separated | Only freeze hard cheeses for cooking |

## Frequently Asked Questions
**Can you eat cheese with mold on it?**
For hard, firm cheeses, yes — cut at least one inch around and below the mold and use the rest, keeping the knife out of the mold itself. For soft or creamy cheeses, discard the whole thing, because mold threads spread invisibly through high-moisture food.

**Why does my cheese smell like ammonia?**
Trapped volatiles from continued fermentation, usually because it was sealed in plastic. Unwrapping it for ten minutes disperses much of that. Store it in breathable paper going forward.

**How long does cheese last in the fridge?**
Wrapped properly: hard aged cheeses four to six months, semi-hard three to four weeks, soft and bloomy-rind one to two weeks, fresh cheeses just a few days. All shorter if the fridge runs above 45°F (7°C).

**Is cheese paper worth it over parchment?**
Cheese paper performs better because it's a specialty laminate tuned for this exact moisture-vs-breathability tradeoff. But parchment gets you most of the benefit for a fraction of the cost. The important change is getting away from plastic wrap, not which paper you buy.

**Should cheese be stored in the fridge door?**
No. The door is the warmest area and swings the most in temperature. Use a crisper drawer — humid and stable — or a low shelf at the back.

**Can I freeze cheese?**
Yes for cooking purposes, and hard cheeses handle it best. Expect a crumbly, grainy texture, so use it in sauces and gratins rather than on a cheese board. Skip freezing high-moisture cheeses entirely.

**What's the white stuff on my cheddar?**
Often tyrosine crystals — harmless amino acid crystals that form during aging and indicate a well-aged cheese. They're a good sign. They're dry and crunchy, not fuzzy. Fuzzy growth is mold.

**Does leaving cheese out at room temperature ruin it?**
An hour or two is fine and improves flavor; most cheese tastes better near 65–70°F (18–21°C). Leaving soft cheeses out overnight is a food safety problem, and hard cheeses will start sweating and staling. Unwrap it, rest it, then rewrap and return it.
