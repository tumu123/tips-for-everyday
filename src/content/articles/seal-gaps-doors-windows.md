Most draft-sealing attempts fail because they seal the gap you can see rather than the gap that leaks. Visible gaps and leaking gaps are not the same set — a hairline crack under a sill can move more air than a gap you can see through.

**Find the leak first, then choose the material.** There are only four materials involved, and each suits a specific situation:

| Gap type | Material | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Moving joint — door stop, window sash | V-strip or adhesive foam | Must compress every time it closes |
| Gap under a door | Door sweep, plus a threshold if needed | The largest single leak in most homes |
| Fixed joint — frame to wall, trim | Caulk | No movement, so a rigid seal lasts |
| Wide fixed gap (over about 1/4 inch) | Backer rod, then caulk | Caulk alone sags or cracks in a wide gap |

**The second thing people get wrong:** using adhesive foam on a high-friction surface. It is cheap and easy, but on a door that opens dozens of times a day it compresses and peels within a season. V-strip costs slightly more and lasts years.

## Step 0: Find Where the Air Actually Comes In

Do this before buying anything.

1. **The paper test.** Close a door or window on a strip of paper. If you can pull the paper out with no resistance, that spot leaks. Test several points around the frame.
2. **The hand test.** On a cold or windy day, run a wet hand slowly around the frame. Evaporation makes leaks obvious.
3. **The light test.** At night, have someone hold a bright light outside while you look from inside. Light showing through means air is getting through.
4. **Mark every leak** with tape as you find it. Do not rely on memory.
5. **Check the obvious ones too** — under the door, around the letterbox, and at the base of the frame where it meets the floor.

## Step 1: Classify Each Gap

Each marked leak goes into one of three categories, and the category determines the material.

1. **Moving joints** — the door stop (the strip the door closes against), the window sash, anything that opens and closes. These need a compressible seal.
2. **The gap under a door** — treat this as its own category. It is usually the biggest leak and needs a sweep.
3. **Fixed joints** — where the frame meets the wall, along trim, around the exterior. These never move, so they can be sealed rigidly.

**Also note the gap width.** Anything over about 1/4 inch (6 mm) needs backer rod before caulk, or the caulk will slump or crack as it cures.

---

## Step 2: Clean Every Surface Before Applying Anything

This is the most-skipped step and the most common cause of weatherstripping that peels off.

1. **Remove old weatherstripping completely** and scrape away the old adhesive.
2. **Wipe all surfaces with rubbing alcohol** and let them dry. Dust, grease, and old adhesive are what cause new strips to fail.
3. **Check that the surface is sound.** Peeling paint or soft wood will not hold adhesive.
4. **Do the work at a reasonable temperature.** Most adhesives bond poorly in the cold, and caulk does not cure properly below its stated range.
5. **Dry the surfaces.** Do not seal over dampness.

---

## Step 3: Seal the Door Frame With V-Strip or Foam

1. **Choose V-strip for a door used constantly.** It is a folded plastic or metal strip that compresses when the door closes and springs back. It outlasts foam by years.
2. **Choose adhesive foam for a door used rarely**, or as a temporary fix.
3. **Fit the strip to the door stop**, the face the door presses against when closed — not to the door itself.
4. **Position it by closing the door** and pressing the strip against where the door makes contact. This is more accurate than measuring.
5. **Fit the top piece first, then the sides**, so the corners overlap rather than leaving a gap.
6. **Do not compress the strip flat.** It should just touch when the door closes. Over-compression makes the door hard to latch and wears the strip quickly.
7. **For foam,** leave it slightly proud so the door compresses it — but check the door still latches without slamming.

**Fit also to the hinge side**, which is commonly missed. The hinge-side gap is often the largest one in the frame.

---

## Step 4: Seal the Gap Under the Door

This is where most of the air comes from, and it is often ignored.

1. **Measure the gap** between the bottom of the door and the threshold. If it is more than about 3/4 inch (2 cm), a sweep alone will not work well.
2. **Fit a door sweep** — a strip with a rubber or silicone blade that mounts to the face or bottom of the door and brushes the threshold.
3. **Choose a sweep with an adjustable or flexible blade** so you can set it to just touch without dragging.
4. **Mark and cut to length** with a utility knife or hacksaw, depending on the material.
5. **Position it so the blade lightly contacts the threshold.** Dragging wears it out and makes the door hard to open.
6. **If there is still a visible gap, add or adjust a threshold strip** beneath it.
7. **Check the door still closes and latches** before final tightening.

---

## Step 5: Seal Fixed Joints With Caulk

1. **Remove the old caulk** with a scraper or a utility knife. Caulking over failed caulk does not work.
2. **Check the gap width.** Under about 1/4 inch: caulk alone. Over that: backer rod first.
3. **Insert backer rod** into wide gaps, pressed in so it sits slightly below the surface. It fills the void so the caulk only has to bridge a shallow channel.
4. **Cut the caulk nozzle at a 45-degree angle**, sized to the gap.
5. **Apply in one continuous bead,** moving steadily and keeping the nozzle in the gap.
6. **Tool the bead** with a wet finger or a caulk tool so it contacts both sides. Caulk seals by adhesion to both faces, not by sitting on top.
7. **Use exterior-grade caulk outside** and paintable caulk where you intend to paint.

**Do not caulk a moving joint.** A window sash or a door stop that moves will tear a rigid bead within weeks.

---

## Step 6: Deal With Windows by Type

1. **Double-hung sashes** — use V-strip in the side channels and a sash lock to pull the sashes together. Tightening the sash against the frame is part of the seal.
2. **Casement windows** — use the compression seal around the sash, and check that the crank pulls it firmly against the frame.
3. **Fixed frame to wall** — caulk the exterior perimeter.
4. **Older single-glazed windows** — sealing the frame helps, but the glass itself is the weak point; an interior film kit or a secondary panel addresses that.
5. **Do not seal weep holes** at the bottom of a window frame. Those drain water out of the frame, and blocking them causes water damage.

---

## Step 7: Verify the Work

1. **Re-run the paper test** at every point you marked. The paper should now have noticeable resistance.
2. **Re-run the light test** at night if you used it initially.
3. **Open and close every door and window** several times. Anything that sticks or will not latch has been over-sealed — reduce the compression.
4. **Check that doors self-close properly** if they did before.
5. **Walk the perimeter outside** and check for gaps you missed at the corners and where different materials meet.

---

## Step 8: Do Not Seal Everything

A house needs some air exchange. This is not optional.

1. **Never seal around combustion appliances** — a furnace, water heater, boiler, or fireplace needs combustion air. Sealing its enclosure is dangerous.
2. **Do not block intentional vents** — dryer vents, bathroom and kitchen extract fans, and any vented soffit.
3. **Keep bathrooms and kitchens ventilated.** Sealing those rooms completely traps humidity, which leads to condensation, mold, and peeling finishes.
4. **Expect some air exchange.** If a home becomes noticeably stuffy, or condensation appears on windows, you have sealed too much and need to restore ventilation.
5. **If in doubt about an appliance,** have it checked rather than sealing around it.

## Why This Works

| Design choice | Problem solved | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Locating leaks before sealing | Sealing visible gaps that do not leak | Leaks and visible gaps are not the same set |
| Material matched to joint type | Strips peeling and beads cracking | Moving joints need compression, fixed joints need rigidity |
| V-strip on high-use doors | Foam compressing flat in a season | Spring-back survives repeated closing |
| Door sweep for the under-door gap | The largest leak left unsealed | The bottom gap usually dominates |
| Backer rod in wide gaps | Caulk slumping or cracking | Caulk needs a shallow channel to bridge |
| Cleaning before applying | Strips peeling off | Adhesive fails on dust and old residue |
| Fitting to the stop, positioned by closing | Misaligned strips | Contact point is found by closing, not measuring |
| Not over-compressing | Doors that will not latch | The seal should just touch |
| Preserving vents and combustion air | Dangerous over-sealing | Some air exchange is required |

The mechanism is **matching the seal to the joint**. A rigid seal on a moving joint tears; a compressible seal on a fixed joint is unnecessary and short-lived. Getting that match right is worth more than the quality of the product.

Outlets and switches on exterior walls are the leaks most people miss; [sealing drafty outlets](/fix-it/maintenance/fix-drafts-from-electrical-outlets/) covers how to seal them safely.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Sealing without finding the leaks | Time spent on gaps that do not leak |
| Using adhesive foam on a busy door | Compresses flat and peels within a season |
| Caulking a moving joint | The bead tears when the sash or door moves |
| Caulking a wide gap without backer rod | The caulk slumps or cracks as it cures |
| Leaving old caulk or adhesive in place | New material will not bond to it |
| Applying in cold or damp conditions | Poor adhesion and failed cure |
| Over-compressing weatherstripping | Door will not latch; the strip wears out fast |
| Sealing window weep holes | Blocks drainage and causes water damage |
| Sealing around combustion appliances | Dangerous — they need combustion air |
| Ignoring the gap under the door | Misses the largest leak in most rooms |
| Sealing bathrooms without ventilation | Traps humidity, leading to condensation and mold |
| Blocking dryer and extract vents | Safety and moisture problems |

## FAQ

**How do I find where air is leaking around doors and windows?**
Use the paper test: close the door or window on a strip of paper and try to pull it out. No resistance means air is leaking at that point. Also try the hand test on a windy day, or the light test at night with someone holding a bright light outside.

**What is the best way to seal a drafty door?**
Fit V-strip weatherstripping to the door stop — including the hinge side — and add a door sweep at the bottom. Set both so they just make contact; over-compression makes the door hard to latch and wears the seals quickly.

**Should I use foam tape or V-strip?**
V-strip for doors used constantly, because it springs back after each close and lasts years. Adhesive foam is cheaper and fine for rarely used doors or as a temporary fix, but it compresses flat on a busy door within a season.

**How do I seal a gap under a door?**
Fit a door sweep with a rubber or silicone blade, set so it lightly touches the threshold without dragging. If the gap is larger than about 3/4 inch, add or adjust a threshold strip as well.

**Can I use caulk to seal windows?**
Only on fixed joints — where the frame meets the wall, or along trim. Never caulk a moving sash or a door stop, because the bead will tear the first time it opens.

**Why did my caulk crack?**
Usually because the gap was too wide for caulk alone. Gaps over about 1/4 inch need backer rod pressed in first, so the caulk only bridges a shallow channel. Also check that the caulk was rated for the location and applied within its temperature range.

**Is it possible to seal a house too tightly?**
Yes. Never seal around combustion appliances, and do not block dryer vents, bathroom or kitchen extract fans, or intentional vents. If condensation appears on windows or the house feels stuffy, ventilation has been reduced too far.

**Should I seal window weep holes?**
No. The small holes at the bottom of a window frame let water drain out. Blocking them traps water in the frame and causes rot and water damage inside the wall.

**How much does sealing doors and windows actually save?**
It depends on how leaky the home was to begin with. An energy audit or a simple draft test tells you where the losses are; sealing is usually one of the cheapest measures per unit of savings, after the under-door gap is addressed.
