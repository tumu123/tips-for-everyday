You wake up with a line of three itchy spots on your forearm, and by lunchtime you have read enough to be frightened. This is the point where most people make an expensive decision on bad evidence — either they assume the worst and start throwing away furniture, or they assume it is mosquitoes and lose six weeks.

The uncomfortable fact is that bites alone cannot identify a bed bug. The reaction is an allergic response to the saliva, it appears hours to days after the bite rather than immediately, and a large share of people never react at all. Two people can share a bed, both be bitten, and only one develop marks. Anyone who tells you they can diagnose an infestation from a photograph of bites is guessing.

What can settle it is physical evidence — the insect itself, its shed skins, its droppings, or blood spotting on the bedding. This guide is about finding that evidence, and about what to do the moment you do. It deliberately does not cover chemical treatment: bed bugs are one of the few household pests where the correct homeowner action is to stop and call a professional, and the identifying-then-acting sequence that applies to other pests is in [the DIY pest control method](/save-money/bills/save-money-on-pest-control-diy/).

## Why the Bites Cannot Tell You

Four reasons, and each one is a reason people get this wrong.

**The reaction is delayed.** A first exposure typically produces no marks at all. Sensitivity develops over one to two weeks of repeated exposure, so the first bites in a new infestation are invisible. By the time you react, the population has been there for weeks.

**Many people never react.** Estimates from clinical literature put the share of people who show no reaction at roughly 30 to 50 percent. In a couple, one partner can be covered in marks while the other has none and believes the first is imagining it.

**The marks are not distinctive.** A bed bug bite is a small red papule, often with a darker center, and it itches. That description also fits a mosquito, a flea, a mite, a spider, and several skin conditions. The classic "three in a row" pattern, sometimes called breakfast-lunch-dinner, is suggestive but not diagnostic — mosquitoes feeding on an exposed arm also produce lines.

**Bites appear on skin that was exposed.** Bed bugs do not crawl under clothing to feed. Marks on the ankles and lower legs, under socks, or in a band around the waistband are more suggestive of fleas, which jump from the floor.

## The Four Pieces of Physical Evidence That Do Settle It

If you find any one of these, you have your answer. If you find none after a careful inspection, you probably do not have bed bugs, whatever the bites look like.

**1. The insects themselves.** Adult bed bugs are about a quarter inch long, oval, flattened, and reddish brown — roughly the size of an apple seed. Before feeding they are flat enough to hide in a mattress seam. After feeding they are longer, darker, and plumper. Immature stages are much smaller and nearly transparent, which is why they are missed. They do not fly and do not jump; they crawl.

**2. Shed skins.** Bed bugs molt five times before reaching adulthood, leaving behind pale, translucent husks in the same places they hide. They look like the insect but empty and papery, and a cluster of them is strong evidence of an established population rather than a single hitchhiker.

**3. Droppings.** Bed bug feces are digested blood, and they appear as small dark spots — like the mark of a felt-tip pen — that soak into fabric rather than sitting on the surface. Look along mattress seams, on the underside of the mattress, on the bed frame joints, and along the baseboard behind the headboard. A cluster of these spots is the single most commonly found evidence.

**4. Blood spotting.** Small rusty smears on the sheet, often near a seam or a pillow, where a bug was crushed during the night or where a bite continued to bleed. Fresh spots are red; older ones darken to brown.

A heavy infestation has a distinctive sweet, musty odor, described as similar to coriander or spoiled raspberries. It is a late-stage sign, not something to wait for.

## The Twelve-Minute Bed Inspection

Do this in daylight with a flashlight. You need a credit card or a thin plastic card, and a white sheet of paper.

**The mattress (4 minutes).**

1. Strip the bed completely. Put the bedding in a sealed bag for now — not because you are treating it yet, but so you do not spread anything.
2. Look along every seam on both sides of the mattress, top and bottom. Run the edge of the credit card along each seam to open it and look inside.
3. Check the mattress label and the handle — both are preferred hiding places.
4. Look at the box spring, especially the seams and the corners, and along the bottom edge where the fabric is stapled.
5. Turn the mattress over and check the underside.

**The frame and headboard (4 minutes).**

6. Look in the joints where the frame rails meet, and at the corner brackets.
7. Remove the headboard if it is detachable, or at least look behind it and along its lower edge.
8. Check any screw holes, staples, or fabric folds on the frame.
9. Look at the wall behind the headboard and the baseboard directly below it.

**The room perimeter (4 minutes).**

10. Look behind picture frames and mirrors on the wall near the bed.
11. Check the seams and the underneath of any upholstered chair or sofa in the room.
12. Remove the cover plates from electrical outlets on walls next to the bed and look inside the box.
13. Check inside the nightstand drawers, along the drawer tracks, and the underside of the drawer box.
14. Look inside the closet at the baseboard line and along the hem of any stored bedding.

If you find nothing, repeat the inspection once a week for three weeks, because a small population and a large one do not produce the same amount of evidence, and evidence accumulates.

One trick worth knowing: place a strip of double-sided tape around the legs of the bed, sticky side out. Bed bugs crawling up from the floor get caught. It is not a treatment and it will not stop an established population, but it is the cheapest way to answer the question of whether they are reaching the bed at all.

## What the Common Confusions Actually Look Like

| Suspect | Bite pattern | What you can find | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bed bugs | Lines or clusters on exposed skin during sleep; delayed reaction | Live insects, husks, dark droppings in seams, blood spots | Mattress seams, frame joints, headboard, nearby baseboards |
| Fleas | Groups of three or four around the ankles, under socks | Black grit (flea dirt) that turns red on a wet tissue; jumping insects | Carpet, pet bedding, floor cracks |
| Mosquitoes | Immediate itch, single welts, exposed skin | Nothing indoors | Nothing — they come in from outside |
| Mites (scabies or bird mite) | Intense itch, often between fingers or in a line | Nothing visible; bird mites are tiny and near a nest | Human skin (scabies) or a bird nest in a vent or eave |
| Spider | Two puncture marks, single lesion | Nothing usually | Anywhere; not a household infestation |
| Carpet beetle larvae | Itchy rash from bristles, not bites | Bristly larvae and pale husks near woolens | Carpet edges, stored wool, under furniture |
| Allergic reaction or eczema | Symmetrical, no punctures, no timing pattern | Nothing | Not a pest |

Two rows deserve emphasis. Flea dirt is distinguished from bed bug droppings by a simple test: place the grit on a damp white tissue. Flea dirt is dried blood and dissolves into a reddish-brown stain; bed bug droppings are already digested and do not do this as distinctly.

And the carpet beetle row matters because carpet beetle larvae cause an itchy rash that people confidently attribute to bites. If the marks are in places clothing covers and there is wool nearby, look for bristly larvae and pale husks rather than for bed bugs — the identification and cleaning route for that is in [carpet beetles in the house](/pest-control/insects/carpet-beetles-in-house/).

## The Moment You Confirm Them: Stop and Reset the Plan

This is the part where most of the damage is done by the homeowner rather than the insect.

**Do not move to sleep in another room.** You will carry them with you and start a second infestation. Sleeping in the bed is part of the solution, because it keeps the population concentrated where the treatment will be.

**Do not throw out the mattress or the furniture.** A discarded mattress in a hallway or on a curb spreads the problem to neighbors, and a new mattress in an untreated room is a new food source. In most cases the existing bed is treatable.

**Do not use a fogger.** Total-release foggers do not penetrate mattress seams or wall voids, they leave residue on every surface, and they drive the population deeper into the room and into adjacent rooms. This is well documented and it is the single most common wasted purchase.

**Do not self-treat with a store-bought spray and consider it done.** Consumer products labeled for bed bugs are contact killers with no residual life; they kill what you hit, and you cannot hit a population hiding in seams, frames, and outlet boxes. Repeated partial treatment also spreads the insects.

**Do call a professional.** Bed bugs are the one household pest where this is the answer rather than a fallback. Effective treatment requires either sustained heat — raising the whole room to roughly 120 to 140°F and holding it — or a professional-grade residual program with follow-up visits, and usually both. A reputable operator will inspect first, give you a written scope, and schedule a follow-up inspection; be cautious about anyone who offers a single visit with a guarantee and no inspection.

**Tell your landlord, in writing, if you rent.** In most of the United States, a landlord is responsible for pest control in a multi-unit building, and in many jurisdictions a tenant is also obliged to report a suspected infestation promptly. Reporting protects you from being charged for a problem that may have come from an adjacent unit, and it is often how the connected units get treated at the same time.

**Bag the bedding and wash it hot.** Everything that can go through a dryer should: 30 minutes on high heat kills all stages, including eggs, and the dryer matters more than the washer. Items that cannot be washed can be dried on high, or sealed in a bag and left for a year — which is effective, and impractical for most people.

**Do not spread the contents of the room.** Do not move books, clothes, or cushions to other rooms before they are treated or dried. The most common way a one-room problem becomes a whole-house problem is a well-meant attempt to protect things.

A note on the health question, because it drives a lot of the panic: bed bugs are not known to transmit disease. The reactions are allergic and the itching can lead to secondary skin infection from scratching, which is a reason to keep the marks clean and to see a clinician if a site becomes hot, swollen, or weeping. The harm bed bugs cause is mostly sleep loss, anxiety, and cost — real harms, but not infectious ones.

## If It Turns Out Not to Be Bed Bugs

The inspection often comes back empty, and that is a useful result rather than a dead end.

- **Fleas.** Treat the pet, treat the carpet, and vacuum daily for two weeks, emptying the canister outdoors each time. Flea pupae in carpet emerge over weeks, so a single treatment fails.
- **Bird or rodent mites.** These come from a nest in a vent, an eave, or a wall void. Removing the nest resolves it; treating the room does not.
- **Carpet beetle larvae.** Steam and vacuum the carpet edges and the dust under furniture, and seal the wool. No pesticide is needed.
- **Mosquitoes or a reaction to something else entirely.** Check the window screens and the bedroom door, and consider whether the marks follow a pattern by night or by activity.

If the bites continue with no evidence after three weekly inspections, the next step is a dermatologist or an allergist rather than another pest control company.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not diagnose from bites.** Half the people bitten do not react, and the reaction is delayed by up to two weeks.

**Do not move bedrooms.** You spread the infestation and lose the concentration that makes treatment work.

**Do not discard furniture before an inspection.** It is the most expensive wrong move available.

**Do not use a fogger or a total-release bomb.** No penetration, no residual, and it disperses the population.

**Do not use alcohol sprays or essential oils as a room-wide treatment.** Surface contact kills only; the fire risk of alcohol mist in a bedroom is real.

**Do not assume a professional is unnecessary because you found only one bug.** One visible insect in a seam usually means a population behind it.

**Do not delay reporting if you rent.** Waiting shifts the cost onto you in many jurisdictions.

## FAQ

**Can I tell bed bugs from the bites alone?**

No. The reaction is an allergic response that appears hours to days later, and an estimated 30 to 50 percent of people never react at all. Identification requires physical evidence: the insect, shed skins, dark droppings in seams, or blood spotting.

**What do bed bug droppings look like?**

Small dark brown or black spots that look like felt-tip pen marks and soak into fabric rather than sitting on top of it. They cluster along mattress seams, on the underside of the mattress, in frame joints, and along the baseboard behind the headboard.

**Do bed bugs spread disease?**

They are not known to transmit disease. The reactions are allergic, and the main health risk is secondary infection from scratching, plus sleep loss and anxiety. That is a reason to keep bites clean, not a reason to panic.

**How do bed bugs get into a house?**

On luggage, on secondhand furniture and clothing, in moving boxes, and from an adjacent apartment through shared walls and pipe chases. They do not fly or jump, and they do not come in from the yard.

**Can I treat bed bugs myself?**

Contact sprays and foggers do not reach the seams, frame joints, and outlet boxes where the population lives, and partial treatment spreads the insects. Effective treatment requires sustained heat or a professional residual program with follow-up. This is the one household pest where calling a professional is the correct first move rather than the last.

**How long can bed bugs live without feeding?**

Adults can survive several months without a blood meal, and eggs can remain viable for weeks. This is why "waiting them out" by leaving the house empty does not work, and why sealed items are held for up to a year.

**Will throwing out the mattress solve it?**

Almost never. The population is in the frame, the headboard, the baseboards, and the outlets as well as the mattress, and a discarded mattress in a shared hallway spreads the problem. Treat the room rather than replacing furniture.

## Bottom Line

Bites are not evidence, because the reaction is delayed and up to half of people never have one. The evidence that settles it is physical: the apple-seed-sized insect, its pale shed skins, its dark pen-mark droppings in the mattress seams, or small blood spots on the sheet. Run the twelve-minute inspection with a flashlight and a credit card, and repeat it weekly for three weeks before you conclude anything. If you find evidence, stop — do not fog, do not throw out the bed, do not move to another room — and call a professional, who will need heat or a residual program with a follow-up visit. Tell your landlord in writing the same day if you rent. If three inspections find nothing, look at fleas, bird mites, and carpet beetle larvae instead, and consider that the answer may not be an insect at all.
