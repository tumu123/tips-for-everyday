The arithmetic of a clogged gutter is more dramatic than it sounds. One inch of rain falling on a 1,000-square-foot roof is **623 gallons of water**. That water has to go somewhere, and a blocked gutter sends all of it over the edge, down the fascia, and into the soil directly against the foundation.

From there the damage is quiet and cumulative: saturated soil against the basement wall, water wicking into the rim joist, mulch washing away, a settled walkway, and eventually a wet basement that nobody can explain because the basement "never used to leak."

The work itself is not complicated. The risk is the ladder, and the timing is what most people get wrong — cleaning gutters in early October means doing it again in November.

## Step 1: Pick the right week, not the right weekend

Gutters should be cleaned **after most of the leaves have fallen but before the first hard freeze**. In most temperate climates that is late October to mid-November, and it is one job, not a weekly chore.

Two additional passes are worth it:

- **Late spring**, after seed pods and blossoms drop — maple helicopters and oak catkins clog a downspout as effectively as leaves.
- **After any major wind event** that strips a tree, if you can see debris on the roof.

Cleaning before the leaves are down is wasted work. Cleaning after the first freeze is unpleasant and, if there is ice in the gutter, dangerous.

## Step 2: Set the ladder correctly, because this is the part that hurts

Ladder falls send hundreds of thousands of people to emergency rooms each year, and gutter cleaning is one of the top reasons. Four rules cover most of the risk:

- **One foot out for every four feet up.** A 16-foot ladder sits 4 feet from the wall at the base.
- **Never stand on the top two rungs.** Your belt buckle should stay below the top of the ladder.
- **Three points of contact**, and move the ladder rather than reaching. Overreaching is the mechanism in most falls.
- **Never work near overhead power lines**, and never in wind above about 15 mph.

Additional specifics for gutters:

- **Use a standoff stabilizer** so the ladder does not rest on the gutter itself. A ladder pressing on an aluminum gutter bends it, and a bent gutter holds water at the wrong end.
- **Set the feet on firm ground**, not on mulch or wet soil. A plywood pad under the feet on soft ground is a two-minute precaution.
- **Wear gloves.** Gutters contain sharp metal edges, screws, and occasionally a wasp nest.

If your roof is more than one story, or the pitch is steep, or the ground is uneven — hire it out. The $150 to $300 is cheaper than a fall.

## Step 3: Scoop dry, then flush

The sequence that works: remove the bulk dry, then test the flow with water.

1. **Scoop by hand** with a gutter scoop or a plastic trowel, working from the downspout end toward the far end. Plastic will not scratch the gutter finish the way a metal tool will.
2. **Bag as you go.** Hanging a contractor bag from the ladder with a hook means one trip down instead of ten. Wet leaves are heavy, so do not overfill.
3. **Clear the downspout mouth** by hand, then check the flow at the bottom.
4. **Flush with a hose** from the far end toward the downspout, at moderate pressure. This tells you two things at once: whether the gutter slopes correctly, and whether the downspout is clear.
5. **If the downspout is blocked**, do not ram a hose into it from the top. Use a plumber's snake from the bottom, or disconnect the downspout and clear it on the ground.
6. **Check for standing water** after flushing. Water that pools in a section means the gutter has lost its slope and needs a hanger re-secured.

## Step 4: Fix the discharge, which matters more than the cleaning

A clean gutter that dumps water at the foundation has solved nothing. This is the most commonly skipped step.

- **Extend every downspout 4 to 6 feet from the wall**, with a splash block angled away from the house. Short extensions are sold in 3-foot lengths and connect in seconds.
- **Check for the buried drain that no longer drains.** A corrugated line running into the yard can be crushed or root-filled. Test it with a hose and see where the water surfaces.
- **Point discharge downhill and away from walkways.** Water that crosses a path in winter becomes ice, and ice becomes a fall.
- **Never discharge onto a neighbor's property or into a sewer line that is not designed for it.** In many jurisdictions, connecting a downspout to the sanitary sewer is prohibited.

## Step 5: Be honest about gutter guards

Gutter guards are sold as a way to never do this again. The honest version:

- **Stainless micro-mesh** works reasonably well and is the only type worth considering in a house with many trees. It still requires an annual check, and fine debris forms a mat on top that has to be brushed off.
- **Reverse-curve (surface tension) guards** work for large leaves and fail for the small stuff — pine needles and shingle grit pass right through and pack the gutter.
- **Foam inserts** trap debris inside the foam and are the worst of the options.
- **Screens and perforated covers** reduce large debris and let grit through, which then accumulates.

If you have heavy tree cover, guards change the job from "scoop twice a year" to "brush the top twice a year." That is an improvement. If you have light tree cover, plain gutters are simpler.

## Step 6: Use the leaves instead of bagging them

Fall leaves are free mulch and free fertilizer. Bagging them for landfill is the default in most households, and it is the worst option.

**Mow them in.** Run a mulching mower over the lawn with the leaves dry and no more than 6 inches deep. Two passes reduce a layer of leaves to fragments small enough to fall between grass blades. This returns nitrogen and organic matter, and it is measurably better for the lawn than bagging.

**Layer them in beds.** Two to three inches of shredded leaves over perennial beds suppresses weeds and insulates roots. Whole leaves mat and smother; shred first with the mower.

**Compost the rest.** A pile of leaves alone breaks down slowly; mixed with grass clippings and kitchen scraps it breaks down in months. Turn it once and keep it as damp as a wrung-out sponge.

**Use paper bags if your municipality collects.** Plastic bags of leaves go to landfill and do not compost. Most collection programs require paper or loose.

## Step 7: Handle the special cases

- **Pine needles** pass through almost every guard and pack into a dense mat. If you have pines, plan on a spring pass as well as a fall one.
- **Shingle grit** accumulates in the gutter and looks like coarse sand. It is a sign the roof is aging; flushing is the only removal.
- **Moss on the roof** holds moisture against the shingles. A light brushing is fine; a pressure washer is not, and will void most shingle warranties.
- **Wasp nests** under the gutter hangers are common in early fall. Check from the ground with a flashlight before putting a hand up there.
- **Ice dams in February** are usually a gutter problem from November. A clean gutter plus a clear downspout is the cheapest ice dam prevention there is.

## Step 8: Do the roof-edge inspection while you are up there

Once a year, from the ladder at the gutter line, you have the best view of the roof edge you will get without climbing on it. Note:

- **Lifted or missing shingle tabs** at the eave, which is where peeling starts.
- **Rusted or loose drip edge**, which lets water behind the fascia.
- **Fascia and soffit rot**, felt as soft wood under the paint.
- **Gaps at the gutter hangers**, which is where the next sagging section will be.

Photograph anything questionable. A phone photo from the ladder is enough for an estimator to quote without a second visit.

## Step 9: Put the tools away properly

The job ends with the equipment, not the gutter:

- **Rinse the gutter scoop and hose nozzle** before storing; grit in a nozzle seizes it.
- **Hang the ladder flat** on wall hooks, not leaning in a garage where it can fall.
- **Drain and coil the hose**, and store it indoors if you are in a freezing climate.
- **Service the mower** after leaf mulching — leaves load the deck and dull the blade faster than grass does.

## Why This Works

The sequence follows water. Clearing the gutter is pointless if the downspout dumps at the foundation, and extending the downspout is pointless if the gutter will not drain. Doing them in order means one pass instead of three.

The timing decision does the rest. One well-chosen week in late fall, after the leaves are down and before the freeze, replaces a season of repeated effort — and the ladder work happens in the safest conditions of the year.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Cleaning before the leaves are down | You repeat the job twice | After most leaves fall, before first freeze |
| Standing on the top two rungs | The most common cause of ladder falls | Belt below the ladder top; move the ladder |
| Resting the ladder on the gutter | Bends the gutter and creates a low spot | Standoff stabilizer against the wall |
| Flushing before scooping | Wet leaves become a heavy, clogging mass | Scoop dry first, then flush |
| Extending downspouts only 12 inches | Water still lands against the foundation | 4 to 6 feet, angled away |
| Ram a hose down a blocked downspout | Packs the clog tighter | Snake from the bottom, or disconnect it |
| Bagging leaves in plastic | Landfill, no compost value | Mulch in place, or paper bags |
| Mowing 12 inches of leaves at once | Smothers the grass and stalls the mower | 6 inches max, two passes |
| Pressure washing shingles | Destroys granules, voids warranty | Light brushing, or a soft wash |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When should gutters be cleaned in the fall?**

After most leaves have fallen but before the first hard freeze — usually late October to mid-November in temperate climates. Cleaning earlier means repeating the job. Add a late-spring pass for seed pods and catkins, and one after any major wind event.

**How often should gutters be cleaned?**

Twice a year for most homes, plus after heavy wind. Homes with many trees or pines may need three passes. Gutter guards change the work to brushing debris off the top rather than scooping, but they do not eliminate the annual check.

**Are gutter guards worth it?**

Stainless micro-mesh is worth it in a house with heavy tree cover, and it still needs an annual brush-off. Reverse-curve guards fail on pine needles and shingle grit, and foam inserts trap debris inside the foam. In a house with little tree cover, plain gutters are simpler and cheaper.

**What is the best way to deal with fall leaves?**

Mulch them into the lawn with a mower, no more than 6 inches deep and in two passes. Layer shredded leaves 2 to 3 inches deep in perennial beds. Compost the rest, and use paper bags if your municipality collects — plastic bags of leaves go to landfill.

**How far should downspouts discharge from the house?**

Four to six feet, angled away from the foundation, ending on a splash block or a graded surface. Water that lands within a foot of the wall saturates the soil against the basement and is the most common cause of a "sudden" basement leak.

**Is it safe to clean gutters yourself?**

On a single story with firm, level ground and a proper ladder setup — one foot out per four feet up, three points of contact, never the top two rungs. Hire it out for two stories, steep roofs, uneven ground, or if working near power lines.

**What do I do if the downspout is clogged?**

Snake it from the bottom, or disconnect the downspout and clear it on the ground. Do not force a hose down from the top, which packs the clog tighter. If a buried drain line is blocked, test it with a hose and consider replacing it with a surface discharge.

**Can I compost leaves in plastic bags?**

No. Plastic blocks air and water, and the leaves will not break down. Use paper yard bags, a compost bin, or a loose pile. Leaves alone compost slowly; mixing with grass clippings and kitchen scraps speeds it up considerably.
