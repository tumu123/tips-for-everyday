Baking failures feel random because the feedback arrives ninety minutes after the mistake. They are not random. Almost every failure maps to one of five variables: flour quantity, leavening, temperature, mixing time, or timing in the oven. The chart below is organised by what you see, because that is the only information you have when the cake comes out.

Before the chart, the two causes that account for more failures than the other three combined.

**Flour measured by volume.** A cup of all-purpose flour weighs about 120 grams when spooned and levelled, and 140 to 150 grams when scooped straight from the bag. That is a 20 percent difference in the flour in the same recipe, and it is the difference between a tender crumb and a brick. If you weigh nothing else, weigh flour.

**Oven temperature is wrong.** Home ovens commonly run 25 to 50°F off the dial, in either direction, and the error changes as the oven ages. A thermometer that stays in the oven costs about as much as a bag of flour and is the single most useful piece of baking equipment after a scale - the same instrument that [tells you when meat is done](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/safe-cooking-temperatures/).

## Working Through the Common Ones

### Sunken centre

The cake rose and then fell, which means the structure set before the interior was cooked. Three causes, in order of likelihood.

Underbaking is first. A cake that looks done — springy, pulling from the sides — can still be liquid at the centre, especially in a deep pan. An instant-read thermometer through the centre reads 200 to 210°F when a butter cake is genuinely done; below 195°F it will sink as it cools.

Too much leavening is second. Excess baking powder or soda produces a large gas volume that the batter cannot hold once it sets, so it collapses. If the recipe uses both baking powder and soda, check that neither is expired — a half-teaspoon of each in hot water should fizz immediately.

Opening the oven in the first 20 minutes is third. The temperature drop is real, and an under-set structure falls. Use the window, not the door.

### Dense and rubbery

Overmixing develops gluten, and gluten is a network that tightens the crumb. For cakes and quick breads, mix until the flour disappears and stop. For yeasted bread, the opposite is true — it cannot be overmixed by hand.

Second cause: leavening that has lost its power. Baking powder loses potency about six months after opening; baking soda lasts longer but absorbs odours. Test before trusting.

Third cause: heavy flour. This is the volume-measurement problem showing up as density — 25 extra grams of flour in a single-layer cake is enough to change the texture noticeably.

### Pale, with no browning

Browning is the Maillard reaction, and it needs temperature and sugar. A cake that stays blond was baked too cool, or has too little sugar, or sat on the wrong rack.

Check the oven first with a thermometer. Then check the rack: the middle is correct for most baking, and the bottom rack browns the base before the top. A dark-coloured pan browns faster; a light one slower. Glass conducts differently and usually needs 25°F lower.

### Burnt outside, raw inside

Heat is arriving too fast from the outside, so the exterior sets and scorches before the interior conducts enough heat inward. Thin dark pans and a too-hot oven both do this. So does putting a deep batter into a pan that is too small — the centre is now further from the metal.

The fix is lower temperature and longer time, plus a lighter pan. A strip of foil over the top after 20 minutes also works for a cake that is browning too fast.

### Cookies that spread flat

Butter temperature is the usual answer. Dough made with butter at 75°F spreads more than dough made with butter at 60°F, and dough that goes into the oven warm spreads more than dough that chilled for 30 minutes.

Too much sugar is second — sugar liquefies and promotes spread. And measuring error cuts both ways: extra butter or too little flour both flatten cookies.

The reverse failure — cookies that barely spread and stay domed — is almost always too much flour, or dough straight from the fridge going onto a cold sheet.

### Bread that will not rise

Yeast is a living organism and there are only four things that stop it.

Dead yeast: dry yeast loses viability after its date, and instant yeast in a sealed bag lasts about a year. Proof it in warm water with a pinch of sugar — if there is no foam after ten minutes, the yeast is the problem.

Water too hot: yeast dies above about 120°F and works best at 100 to 110°F. Water that feels comfortably warm to a finger is roughly 100°F; water that is hot enough to be unpleasant is already too hot.

Too much salt: salt inhibits yeast, and doubling it by accident can stall a dough. Keep salt at roughly 2 percent of flour weight.

Cold kitchen: dough rises in a cool room, just slower. This is a timing problem, not a failure.

### Gummy bread, sliced warm

Bread straight from the oven is still cooking. Starch has not finished setting, and cutting it compresses the crumb into a gummy line that never recovers. Internal temperature should be 200 to 210°F for a lean loaf, and the loaf should cool at least an hour, ideally two, before slicing. Bread that has gone stale rather than gummy is a different problem, covered in [reviving stale bread](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/revive-stale-bread-chips-crackers/).

## The Measurement Fix That Solves Half of This

| Ingredient | 1 cup by volume | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| All-purpose flour (spooned) | 1 cup | 120–125 g |
| All-purpose flour (scooped) | 1 cup | 140–150 g |
| Bread flour | 1 cup | 127 g |
| Granulated sugar | 1 cup | 200 g |
| Brown sugar (packed) | 1 cup | 213 g |
| Butter | 1 cup | 227 g |
| Water, milk | 1 cup | 237 g |

A digital scale costs less than a single failed bake in ingredients, and it removes the largest source of variance in any recipe - it is also the cheapest item on [the list of kitchen tools that earn their space](/kitchen-tips/equipment/kitchen-gadgets-worth-the-space/). If a recipe gives only volume measurements, convert to grams once, write it on the page, and use that from then on.

## Two Systems, Two Failure Sets

Cakes and yeast breads fail differently because they rise for different reasons, and mixing up the diagnosis wastes a lot of flour.

**Chemical leavening** — baking powder and soda — releases gas on contact with liquid and heat, within minutes. The structure has to set while that gas is expanding, which means the timing is short and the margin is narrow. This is why a cake batter cannot rest, why the oven must be at temperature before the pan goes in, and why opening the door in the first twenty minutes is destructive. A cake failure is almost always a timing or measurement failure.

**Yeast** produces gas slowly, over hours, and the dough's gluten network is what holds it. This means the process is far more forgiving of timing and far less forgiving of technique: under-kneaded dough cannot hold the gas however long you proof it, and dough that over-proofs collapses because the gluten has been stretched past its limit. A bread failure is usually a gluten or fermentation failure.

The practical translation:

| Symptom | Cake | Bread |
|---|---|---|
| Did not rise | Dead leavening, cold oven | Dead yeast, under-kneaded, cold room |
| Rose then fell | Too much leavening, door opened | Over-proofed |
| Dense | Overmixed, heavy flour | Under-kneaded, weak flour |
| Large irregular holes | Rare in cake | Under-proofed or under-kneaded |
| Gummy | Underbaked | Underbaked, or sliced hot |

If you bake both, keep the two lists separate. A cake that will not rise has a chemical problem; a loaf that will not rise has a biological one, and the fixes have nothing in common.

## Change One Thing at a Time

The reason baking feels unpredictable is that people change three variables between attempts — a new recipe, a different pan, a different oven setting — and then cannot tell which one mattered.

The method that works: pick one recipe, make it three times without changing anything except the variable you suspect. If the first attempt was pale, bake the second at 25°F higher and change nothing else. Two or three iterations will isolate the cause, and you will end up with a recipe that works in your oven rather than in the author's.

Write the results on the recipe — temperature, time, pan, and weight of flour. In six months that note is worth more than any cookbook.

## High Altitude, Briefly

Above roughly 3,500 feet, lower air pressure means faster evaporation and faster leavening. Cakes rise faster and fall further. The standard adjustments: reduce baking powder by about 15 percent per 1,500 feet, increase oven temperature 15 to 25°F, and add a tablespoon of extra liquid at higher elevations. Bread needs less adjustment than cakes — mostly a longer proof.

## FAQ

**Why does my cake sink in the middle?**

Usually underbaking. A cake needs 200 to 210°F at the centre, not just a springy top. Second cause is too much leavening, third is opening the oven in the first 20 minutes.

**Why is my cake dry?**

Most often overbaked, then too much flour. Pull it five minutes earlier and weigh the flour — 25 extra grams per cup is enough to dry a cake out.

**How do I know if my baking powder is still good?**

Put half a teaspoon in a cup of hot water. It should fizz vigorously within seconds. No fizz means it is spent; baking powder loses potency roughly six months after opening.

**Why do my cookies spread so much?**

Warm butter, warm dough, and too much sugar. Chill the dough 30 minutes and bake on a cool sheet — never put dough onto a sheet that is still warm from the last batch.

**Why didn't my bread rise?**

Proof the yeast first: warm water with a pinch of sugar, ten minutes, look for foam. If it foams, the problem is water temperature above 120°F, too much salt, or a cold kitchen.

**Do I really need to weigh ingredients?**

Flour, yes. The difference between spooned and scooped flour is 20 percent, which is enough to change the outcome. Sugar, butter, and liquids are more forgiving by volume.

**Why is my baking pale even at the right temperature?**

Check the oven with a thermometer first — many run 25°F low. Then check sugar quantity and rack position. A light-coloured pan and a glass pan both brown more slowly than dark metal.

## Bottom Line

Diagnose by symptom rather than by guesswork. A sunken centre is underbaking; density is overmixing or heavy flour; pale is low temperature; a burnt edge with a raw centre is too much heat too fast; flat cookies are warm butter; bread that will not rise is dead yeast or water above 120°F. Fix the scale and the oven thermometer first, and half of these problems stop happening.
