Cleaning supply spending rarely feels excessive because it's spread across many small purchases. A bottle here, a spray there. Then you open the cabinet and find four products that all clean glass.

The money isn't lost on expensive products. It's lost on **redundant products** — and on buying by volume rather than by unit cost.

---

## Step 1: Inventory What You Already Have

Before buying anything, empty the cabinet. Literally take everything out.

**Count your bottles.** A typical household cabinet contains somewhere between ten and twenty cleaning products. Most people are surprised by the actual number.

**Sort by job:**

| Job | How many products do you own for this? |
|---|---|
| Glass and mirrors | |
| Bathroom surfaces | |
| Kitchen degreasing | |
| Floors | |
| General spray cleaning | |
| Disinfecting | |
| Scrubbing / abrasive | |

If any row has more than two, you've found money. Glass cleaner, multi-surface spray, and "granite and stone" spray very often overlap almost entirely.

**Check the dates.** Some products degrade. Hydrogen peroxide loses potency once opened, and disinfectants have expiry dates that matter.

---

## Step 2: Calculate Unit Cost — Not Shelf Price

The shelf price tells you almost nothing. A 32-ounce bottle at $4 is not cheaper than a 16-ounce bottle at $1.50.

```
Price ÷ volume = cost per unit
```

Most stores now print the unit price on the shelf tag, usually in small print. If they don't, calculate it — and be careful to compare the **same units** across products.

| Product | Price | Volume | Cost per ounce |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spray A | $3.99 | 32 oz | $0.125 |
| Spray B | $2.49 | 16 oz | $0.156 |
| Spray C | $5.99 | 64 oz | $0.094 |

Spray C looks expensive. It's actually the cheapest by nearly 25% per ounce.

**The concentrate trap.** Concentrates are usually cheaper per diluted ounce — but only if you dilute them correctly. If the label says 1:10 and you use it at 1:4 because it seems weak, you've erased the savings. Check the dilution ratio on the label and do the math on the *diluted* volume:

```
Price ÷ (container volume × dilution factor) = cost per ready-to-use ounce
```

**The bulk-buying trap.** A larger size is only a saving if you use it before it degrades, and if the unit price is genuinely lower. Some products are sold in larger bottles at a *higher* unit price because buyers assume bigger means cheaper. Always check.

## Step 3: Find the Overlap

This is where most of the savings live.

**Common redundancies:**

| Products | The overlap | Keep |
|---|---|---|
| Glass cleaner + all-purpose spray | Most general sprays handle glass if you wipe with a dry cloth | Multi-purpose |
| Kitchen degreaser + all-purpose spray | Dish soap handles most grease | Dish soap + one spray |
| Tub and tile + bathroom cleaner + shower spray | Usually similar acid or surfactant formulas | One bathroom cleaner |
| Disinfecting spray + disinfecting wipes | Same chemistry, different delivery | Whichever you'll actually use |
| Floor cleaner specific to each surface | Most hard floors need only a dilute neutral cleaner | One floor cleaner |

**A realistic minimum cabinet:**

1. Dish soap (handles most grease)
2. All-purpose spray (DIY or bought)
3. A bathroom descaler for mineral deposits
4. A disinfectant for when it's actually needed
5. An abrasive paste or powder for scrubbing
6. Microfiber cloths

That's it. Six categories covers essentially all routine household cleaning.

## Step 4: Know What's Genuinely Cheaper to Make

DIY is not automatically cheaper. Some things are, dramatically. Some aren't worth the effort.

**Genuinely worth making:**

| Made at home | Rough cost | Comparable product | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| All-purpose spray (vinegar + water) | Cents per bottle | $3–5 | Strong saving |
| Glass cleaner (water, vinegar, alcohol) | Cents per bottle | $3–5 | Strong saving |
| Baking soda scrub paste | Cents | $3–6 | Strong saving |
| Descaler (vinegar soak) | Very low | $4–8 | Strong saving |
| Drain maintenance (boiling water + soda) | Near zero | $5–10 | Strong saving, preventive only |

**Usually worth buying:**

| Product | Why DIY falls short |
|---|---|
| Enzyme cleaners (pet urine, organic stains) | Require specific live cultures; cannot be made at home |
| Disinfectants with verified claims | Kill-rate claims require testing and registration |
| Specialized stone or wood cleaners | Wrong pH causes permanent damage; formulation matters |
| Laundry detergent | Home formulations often leave residue and under-perform |
| Dishwasher detergent | Home recipes commonly cause filming and residue |

We cover the all-purpose recipe and its limits in detail in [How to Make an All-Purpose Cleaner](/home-hacks/cleaning/diy-all-purpose-cleaner/) — including the surfaces where vinegar should never be used.

## Step 5: Stop Paying for Water

A large share of what you pay for in a spray bottle is water.

**Concentrates and refills** address this directly — you buy the active ingredient and add water at home. The saving is real, provided you follow the dilution ratio.

**For products you use a lot of,** the refill format is usually the cheapest per use. For products you use once a month, the convenience of ready-to-use may be worth the premium — a bottle that sits for a year isn't saving you anything.

**Microfiber matters more than chemistry.** Most cleaning is mechanical removal, not chemical. Good microfiber cloths used slightly damp remove more than most sprays do, and they replace a lot of product. Wash them without fabric softener, which coats the fibers and destroys their function.

---

## Step 6: Buy Smarter

**Never buy a cleaner for a job you'll do twice a year.** One-off jobs get handled by something you already own.

**Try the smallest size before committing to bulk.** A product that doesn't suit you is a total loss at any unit price.

**Store brands for simple chemistry.** For straightforward products — bleach, vinegar, baking soda, isopropyl alcohol — the chemistry is identical and the store brand is usually cheaper. Reserve branded purchases for formulated products where performance actually differs.

**Don't stockpile degradables.** Hydrogen peroxide, some disinfectants, and anything with live cultures lose effectiveness over time.

**Buy on actual need, not on a sale.** A bargain you wouldn't otherwise have bought is spending, not saving.

---

## Why This Works

**Most cleaning is mechanical, not chemical.** Surfactants and solvents help, but the dominant mechanism is physical removal with a cloth and water. Once you accept that, the number of distinct chemicals required drops substantially — and consumable cloths replace consumable liquids.

**Unit price is the only comparable number.** Package sizes are chosen for shelf appeal, not for comparability. Normalizing to a per-ounce basis is the only way to compare two products — and it frequently reverses the apparent ranking.

**Functional overlap is invisible until you sort it.** Products are marketed by room and by surface, not by chemistry, so a cabinet accumulates several formulations doing the same job. Sorting by job rather than by label exposes the duplication immediately.

**Not everything is worth making.** DIY substitutes well for simple acids, bases, and solvents. It does not substitute for enzymes, registered disinfectants, or products where pH tolerance is critical. Knowing the boundary is what keeps the savings real.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Buying by shelf price | You pay more per ounce | Always compare unit price |
| Assuming concentrates are cheaper | Wrong dilution erases the saving | Calculate ready-to-use cost |
| Buying bulk you won't finish | Product degrades before use | Match size to actual usage |
| Mixing vinegar and bleach | Produces toxic chlorine gas | Never mix — ever |
| Using vinegar on stone | Etches marble, granite, travertine | Use a pH-neutral stone cleaner |
| Making laundry detergent at home | Residue, poor cleaning, washer buildup | Buy a formulated detergent |
| Stockpiling hydrogen peroxide | Loses potency after opening | Buy small, replace as needed |
| Buying a specialty cleaner for a rare job | Product expires before it's used again | Use what you already have |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much can I realistically save?**
Cutting the cabinet down to six functional categories typically halves routine cleaning supply spending. The exact figure depends on how much duplication you started with.

**Are DIY cleaners as effective as commercial ones?**
For glass, general surface cleaning, and descaling — yes. For disinfecting, enzymes, and delicate stone — no.

**Is vinegar safe on all surfaces?**
No. It damages natural stone, can dull some finishes, and should never be mixed with bleach.

**Do I really need a separate product for each room?**
Almost never. Marketing drives that segmentation more than chemistry does.

**Are store-brand cleaners lower quality?**
For simple formulations, the chemistry is the same. For complex formulated products, there can be real differences.

**What should I always buy rather than make?**
Enzyme cleaners for pet accidents, registered disinfectants, and pH-appropriate stone or wood cleaners.

**Are microfiber cloths worth it?**
Yes, and they're the highest-leverage purchase in this whole guide. They reduce how much product you need at all.

**How often should I clean out the cabinet?**
Once or twice a year. Duplication accumulates gradually, and you won't notice it without looking.
