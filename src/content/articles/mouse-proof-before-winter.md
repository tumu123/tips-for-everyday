The annual mouse problem has a start date, and it is not random. As overnight temperatures fall into the 40s and 50s°F, mice that spent the summer outdoors begin looking for somewhere warmer, and a house with an unsealed sill plate is warmer than a field. The pressure peaks from September through November and then holds through the winter, because once a mouse is inside it has no reason to leave.

This is the reason mouse-proofing is a fall job rather than an emergency response: the work is the same either way, but done in October it prevents the problem, and done in January it interrupts one. If you are not yet sure you have a rodent at all, the diagnostic sequence — sound, droppings, grease marks, powder test — is in [how to tell if mice are in the walls](/pest-control/rodents/mice-in-the-walls/); sealing is what you do once you know.

What follows is a route rather than a list. Walking the exterior in a fixed order, from the foundation upward and from one corner around to the other, is what prevents the usual outcome of sealing eleven gaps and missing the twelfth. It takes about four to six hours for a typical single-family house, and it is mostly low-cost materials.

The underlying logic — exclusion before treatment, seal before bait — is the general case set out in [the DIY pest control method](/save-money/bills/save-money-on-pest-control-diy/). This guide is the execution: where to look, what to put in each gap, and how to check the result.

## Step 1: Walk the Exterior With a Flashlight and a Notebook

**The dimension that matters is 1/4 inch** — about the width of a pencil. A house mouse needs a gap of roughly that size to pass; a rat needs about 1/2 inch. Almost every "I checked and there was nothing" conclusion comes from looking for gaps the size of a fist, when the relevant gaps are the size of a pencil.

The walk has a fixed order, so nothing is missed:

1. Start at the front door, work clockwise around the house at ground level.
2. Note every gap, penetration, vent, and door in a notebook or on your phone, with its location.
3. Then do a second pass **from inside**, in the basement or crawl space, looking outward.

The second pass is the one that finds the real problems. Standing inside a basement with the lights off and looking toward the exterior wall, any gap that admits daylight is a gap an animal can use. This is the **light test**, and it is more reliable than any visual inspection from outside, because it reveals gaps hidden behind siding, vegetation, or a downspout.

**Potential entry points to check, in the order you will encounter them:**

| Zone | What to inspect |
|---|---|
| Foundation | Sill plate joint, foundation-to-frame gap, cracks in masonry, weep holes |
| Utilities | Every pipe, cable, and conduit entering the wall; gas line; electrical service |
| Dryer and HVAC | Dryer vent hood, AC refrigerant line set, condensate drain |
| Doors | Bottom sweep, sides, threshold, garage door base and corners, basement door |
| Windows | Frame-to-wall joint at the sill, especially at basement windows |
| Vents | Crawl space vents, foundation vents, gable vents, soffit vents |
| Roof line | Soffit-to-wall joint, fascia gaps, chimney flashing (for squirrels) |

## Step 2: Start at the Sill Plate

The sill plate is the joint where the wooden frame sits on the foundation, and it is where a large share of mouse entries occur. It is also where the joint is least visible, because it sits just above grade and behind the siding edge.

Work around the entire perimeter. For each gap:

- Clear away soil, mulch, or vegetation that hides the joint. This is not cosmetic — a gap you cannot see is a gap you cannot seal, and vegetation against the foundation also holds moisture against the wall.
- If the gap is a hairline, a bead of exterior-grade polyurethane sealant is enough.
- If the gap is wider than about 1/4 inch, **pack it first**. Copper mesh or stainless steel wool, pushed in with a putty knife, then covered with sealant.
- If the gap is wider than 1/2 inch, insert backer rod and then seal over it, or fill with a piece of 1/4-inch hardware cloth folded to fit.

The packing step is the one that matters. Sealant alone over a wide gap is a barrier a mouse can chew through or push out of. The metal mesh is what stops the teeth; the sealant stops the air.

## Step 3: Do Every Pipe and Cable Penetration

Utilities are the second-largest category, and they are the easiest to fix properly because the gaps are small and accessible.

Check and seal around:

- The electrical service entry and the meter box
- Any conduit entering the wall
- Water supply lines, hose bibs, and irrigation lines
- The gas line entry
- Cable, telephone, and fibre entries
- The condensate drain from the furnace or air handler
- Any pipe passing through a rim joist

For each: pack copper mesh around the pipe, leaving the pipe itself free, then seal the face. A gap around a 1/2-inch pipe is a 1/4-inch gap on each side, which is enough.

Hose bibs deserve a specific note because they are commonly left with a visible gap between the pipe and the siding, and because the penetration is at ground level where mice travel.

## Step 4: The Dryer Vent and the AC Line Set

Two penetrations are large and frequently left open.

**The dryer vent.** The hood on the exterior should have a **self-closing flap** and should sit flush against the siding. A missing flap, a flap stuck open with lint, or a hood that has pulled away from the wall is an open 4-inch pipe into the house — the single largest hole in most exterior walls. Replace the hood with a self-closing metal type, seat it against the wall with sealant, and check the flap moves freely. A dryer vent is also a fire path, so this is worth doing for two reasons.

**The air-conditioning line set.** Where the refrigerant lines and condensate pipe enter the wall, the hole is often drilled generously and sealed with a bit of putty that has since fallen out. Pack copper mesh around the lines and seal the face with exterior sealant. Do not seal the condensate drain closed — it needs to drain.

## Step 5: Doors, Sweeps, and Thresholds

A gap under an exterior door is the most-used mouse entry in a house, and it is also the one that costs the most in heating — the same seal does both jobs, and the insulation side of it is in [cheap ways to insulate windows and doors](/save-money/bills/cheap-window-insulation/).

**Check the gap.** Close the door and look at the bottom. A gap you can see daylight through is too large; the target is 1/4 inch or less, and ideally a seal that touches.

**Fit a door sweep.** A surface-mounted sweep with a rubber or brush seal, screwed to the inside face of the door, closes the gap. A brush seal is more forgiving of an uneven floor; a rubber blade seals better on a flat threshold.

**Fix the sides and top.** Weatherstripping along the jambs, replaced if it is compressed or torn. The corner where the sweep meets the jamb is the usual remaining gap — check it specifically.

**Basement and garage doors.** These are the two most commonly neglected. A garage door with a gap at the base and at the corners is an open door for anything at ground level. Fit a threshold seal and check the corner where the door meets the frame.

**The door between the garage and the house.** It should be solid, self-closing, and sealed on all four sides. This door is the single most important barrier in a house with an attached garage.

## Step 6: Foundation Vents and the Crawl Space

A crawl space is a warm, dry, sheltered space with an open connection to the house, and an unscreened foundation vent is an invitation.

- **Every foundation vent should have 1/4-inch mesh** behind the louvres. Hardware cloth cut to size and fastened behind the frame is the standard. Louvres alone do not exclude mice.
- **Check for damaged or missing vent covers** and replace them.
- **Screen the crawl space access hatch** and make sure the hatch sits flush with a seal.
- **Seal the rim joist** from inside the crawl space if it is accessible — this is the band of framing between the foundation and the floor above, and gaps there lead directly into the floor system.

While you are in the crawl space, check the vapour barrier and the insulation for rodent nesting. A crawl space that is being used as a nest site will show droppings and disturbed insulation; the storage and moisture side of keeping that space sound is in [how to store things in a basement without ruining them](/organize/home-systems/basement-storage-without-ruining/), which applies to crawl spaces as well.

## Step 7: The Garage, Which Is the Weak Point

An attached garage is the most common route for mice to reach a house, for a simple reason: garage doors have large gaps, they are opened and closed constantly, and the garage is often the warmest structure on the property in early winter.

- **Garage door base:** fit a threshold seal across the full width. The corners need attention separately, because the seal compresses there.
- **Garage door sides:** the vertical tracks and the frame often leave a gap of 1/4 to 1/2 inch. A brush seal fitted to the door edge closes it.
- **The service door:** treat it like an exterior door — sweep, weatherstripping, and a self-closer.
- **The wall between garage and house:** seal every penetration, including any pipe or cable, and check the drywall for holes. A mouse in the garage will find a 1/4-inch gap in that wall within a season.

## Step 8: Verify With the Light Test

After the work is done, repeat the interior pass in the dark. Lights off inside, daylight outside, walk the perimeter and look for any remaining light. Seal whatever still shows.

Then run a second verification over the following week: set a few unbaited snap traps along the exterior walls of the basement or garage and check them daily. A trap that catches nothing in a week in a previously active area is evidence that the exclusion worked. A trap that catches something tells you a gap remains, and the location of the catch tells you roughly where.

Do not bait the traps during this check. The point is to detect traffic, not to attract it.

## Materials That Actually Stop a Mouse

The material choice is where most DIY attempts fail, because two popular materials do not work.

| Material | Works? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Copper mesh | Yes | Does not rust; the standard for exterior gaps. Pack and then seal over it |
| Stainless steel wool | Yes | Same function, more expensive, better in wet locations |
| Plain steel wool | Short term | Rusts away within a season outdoors, leaving the gap open again. Acceptable only in dry interior locations |
| 1/4-inch hardware cloth | Yes | For larger openings and vents; the mesh size is what excludes mice |
| Exterior polyurethane or silicone sealant | Yes, over a packed gap | Not a barrier on its own over a wide gap |
| Expanding foam alone | No | Mice chew through cured foam. Use it only to back a mesh-packed gap |
| Caulk over a wide gap | No | It shrinks, cracks, and pulls away |

The rule is simple: **metal stops teeth, sealant stops air.** Every gap wider than a hairline needs metal first.

Foam is chewed through and hardware cloth is not. For the animal that needs a two-inch hole, the litter timing, and the one-way door, see [the materials that stop a squirrel](/pest-control/rodents/squirrels-in-the-attic/).

Exclusion and removal are different jobs with different materials. For the nests you should not touch, and the species that decides it, see [materials that resist chewing and stinging](/pest-control/insects/wasp-nest-when-to-call-a-professional/).

## What NOT to Do

**Do not seal while there is activity inside.** Sealing with a mouse in the house traps it in and it will find a route into the living space. Confirm with the powder test that activity has stopped, or trap first.

**Do not use expanding foam as the only barrier.** It is chewable. Mesh plus foam or mesh plus sealant is what holds.

**Do not ignore the garage because "the door is always closed".** The gaps remain when the door is closed, and the garage-to-house wall is the barrier that matters.

**Do not seal the condensate drain or the dryer vent opening.** Both need to drain or vent; screen and hood them instead of closing them.

**Do not rely on ultrasonic repellers.** Evidence for their effectiveness in field conditions is weak, and habituation is common. Sealing is a permanent fix; a plug-in device is not.

**Do not put bait outside as the primary strategy.** Bait outside attracts rodents to the building rather than away from it. Exclusion first; bait only in specific, contained situations.

**Do not skip the light test.** It is the only check that finds the gaps hidden behind siding and vegetation.

## FAQ

**How small a gap can a mouse get through?**

About 1/4 inch, roughly a pencil's width. Rats need about 1/2 inch. That is why the inspection has to be done at that scale, close up, with a flashlight.

**When should I mouse-proof my house?**

September through November, before overnight temperatures settle in the 40s. The work is identical in spring, but doing it in autumn prevents the problem instead of interrupting it.

**Is steel wool or copper mesh better?**

Copper mesh is better for exterior use because it does not rust. Plain steel wool rusts away within a season when exposed to weather, which reopens the gap. Stainless steel wool performs like copper mesh and is a reasonable substitute.

**Can mice chew through expanding foam?**

Yes. Cured foam is not a barrier on its own. It works as a backing when combined with metal mesh, and it is useful for filling volume, but a foam-only seal will be opened.

**How do I know if the sealing worked?**

Two checks: the light test from inside in the dark, and a week of unbaited traps along the exterior walls of the basement or garage. No catch and no light means the envelope is closed.

**Do ultrasonic repellents work?**

Field evidence is weak, and rodents appear to habituate to them within days. They are not a substitute for sealing, and they are not a reason to skip the walkthrough.

**What if I find activity after sealing everything?**

Then an entry was missed, or the animal was already inside when you sealed. Check the garage-to-house wall, the sill plate behind vegetation, and the dryer vent hood — those three account for most missed entries. Also confirm that the powder test showed no activity before you sealed.

## Bottom Line

The work is a route, not a list: walk the perimeter clockwise with a flashlight, then repeat from inside in the dark and look for daylight. Seal the sill plate first, then every utility penetration, then the dryer vent and the AC line set, then doors and thresholds, then the foundation vents, then the garage — which is where most entries actually happen. Pack every gap wider than a hairline with copper mesh before sealing it, because metal stops teeth and sealant only stops air. Finish with the light test and a week of unbaited traps. Four to six hours in October is worth more than any amount of trapping in January.
