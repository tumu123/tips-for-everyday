An open bottle of wine doesn't go off because time passed. It goes off because **oxygen got to it**. Once you hold that distinction, most of the confusion about leftover wine disappears, including why the same bottle lasts two days in one kitchen and a week in another.

Oxygen does two things. Immediately, it lets aroma compounds escape and flatten — this is why wine "tastes dull" the next day even when it isn't spoiled yet. Over a longer window, it feeds bacteria that convert alcohol into acetic acid, which is vinegar. That second process is irreversible and it's what "gone bad" actually means.

Two variables control the speed: **how much air is in contact with the wine**, and **how cold it's kept**. Cold slows both the chemical oxidation and the bacterial activity. That's why the fridge is the single most effective thing you can do, and why a half-empty bottle dies faster than a nearly full one — the headspace is full of air.

The other factor is the wine itself, and it matters more than most people assume. Not all wine has the same shelf life after opening, and the differences are large.

## Step 1: Know how long each type actually lasts

Opened and refrigerated with the cork back in, these are the working ranges:

| Wine type | Refrigerated, recorked | What happens as it declines |
|---|---|---|
| Sparkling wine | 1 to 3 days | Loses bubbles first; flavor holds longer |
| Light white, rosé | 3 to 5 days | Flattens, then dulls |
| Full-bodied white (oaked Chardonnay) | 3 to 5 days | Oxidation is less obvious; some last longer |
| Light red | 3 to 5 days | Fruit fades, then turns stewed |
| Full-bodied red (Cabernet, Syrah) | 3 to 6 days | Tannins buffer oxidation better |
| Fortified (Port, Sherry, Madeira) | 2 to 4 weeks | High alcohol resists spoilage |
| Dessert / sweet wine | 1 to 2 weeks | Sugar and alcohol both preserve |

Note that **fortified and sweet wines are in a different category entirely.** Port and sherry were built to survive exposure; a bottle of port left on the counter for weeks will be fine long after an open Cabernet has turned.

The counterintuitive entry is sparkling wine, which has the *shortest* life for quality reasons rather than spoilage reasons. It rarely becomes unsafe — it just goes flat, and flat sparkling wine is a disappointment rather than a hazard.

## Step 2: Recork immediately, every time

The clock starts the moment the bottle is opened, and the biggest avoidable loss is the hour or two a bottle sits open on the table.

1. **Put the cork or stopper back in as soon as you've poured**, not at the end of the evening. An hour of open exposure is worth several hours of storage life.
2. **Use the cork, inserted cork-first if possible.** If you pulled a real cork, it goes back in more easily the way it came out in terms of fit. A cheap rubber stopper with a good seal is fine and often more reliable than a swollen cork.
3. **Store the bottle upright.** Lying an opened bottle on its side increases the surface area of wine touching the air pocket, which speeds oxidation. This is the opposite of unopened wine, which is traditionally stored on its side to keep the cork moist.
4. **Keep the bottle away from light and heat** while you're still drinking from it. A bottle sitting in afternoon sun on a table is oxidizing noticeably faster than one in the shade.

## Step 3: Refrigerate it — including red wine

This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that matters most.

- **White, rosé and sparkling: straight to the fridge.** Uncontroversial and obviously right.
- **Red wine: also the fridge.** Chilling red wine does not harm it. Take it out 20 to 30 minutes before you want another glass and let it come back up toward room temperature, or pour and let it warm in the glass. Refrigerated red lasts noticeably longer than red left on the counter — often twice as long.
- **The only caveat is serving temperature, not storage temperature.** Cold mutes red wine's aromatics, which is why you let it warm before drinking. That's a serving issue, not a storage problem.

The rule to internalize: **the fridge is for storage, the counter is for serving.** They're not in conflict.

## Step 4: Reduce the air in the bottle

Since oxygen is the enemy and headspace is where the oxygen lives, the second lever is physical.

- **Transfer to a smaller bottle.** This is the most effective low-cost method. Half a bottle of wine poured into a clean 375 ml bottle has almost no headspace and will outlast the same wine left in the half-empty 750 ml bottle by days. Keep a couple of small bottles with screw caps for this.
- **Vacuum pumps help, with limits.** They remove some air and extend life meaningfully for still wines. They do *not* work well on sparkling wine — the vacuum pulls the dissolved CO₂ out and you end up with flat champagne faster than if you'd done nothing.
- **Inert gas sprays work best of the gadgets.** A blanket of heavier-than-air gas on the wine's surface blocks contact. Effective, but you're buying a consumable.
- **Skip the "wine saver" gimmicks** that don't seal, don't remove air, and don't displace oxygen.

If you're choosing one thing: a smaller bottle and the fridge beat any gadget.

## Step 5: Judge whether it's still drinkable

Learn the difference between "declined" and "spoiled," because they call for different responses.

**Declined but drinkable** — fine for cooking, sometimes fine for drinking:
- Flatter aroma, less fruit
- Slightly dull or muted on the palate
- Still tastes recognizably like wine

**Spoiled — discard or use only where the flavor won't show:**
- **Sharp, vinegary smell.** The giveaway. Acetic acid is unmistakable once you notice it.
- **Sharp, sour taste** unrelated to the wine's normal acidity.
- **Brownish-orange tint** in a white wine that was pale; **brick-brown dullness** in a red that was bright. Oxidation has advanced.
- **Nail polish remover or bruised-apple smell.** Volatile acidity has developed.

The intermediate case — wine that's merely dull — is excellent for cooking. Reduce it into a pan sauce, use it for braising, or make a wine vinegar by leaving it open deliberately.

## Step 6: Freeze what you won't drink

If you can't finish a bottle within the window in Step 1, freezing beats pouring it out.

- **Freeze in portions.** Ice cube trays are ideal: one cube is roughly two tablespoons, so you can drop a couple straight into a pan. Once frozen, transfer the cubes to a bag.
- **Leave headspace** in any container you freeze — wine expands as it freezes. A full, sealed bottle in the freezer can crack.
- **Don't freeze sparkling wine.** It loses carbonation entirely and the result isn't worth the space.
- **Use frozen wine for cooking only.** Thawed wine is drinkable but noticeably degraded; the aromatics don't survive freezing well. In a braise or a reduction, nobody will notice.

Freezing buys months rather than days, and it means an open bottle never has to be a waste.

## Why This Works

Oxidation is the whole story, and it's a surface-area problem rather than a volume problem. The reaction happens where wine meets air, so a bottle with a large headspace exposes a large surface and oxidizes quickly regardless of how much wine remains. That's the mechanism behind the small-bottle trick: less air above the wine means less oxygen available, and less surface for the reaction to run on.

Temperature works on both fronts. Chemical oxidation roughly halves in speed for every significant drop in temperature, and the acetic acid bacteria that turn ethanol into vinegar are sluggish when cold. Cooling attacks the same problem through two independent paths, which is why refrigeration outperforms every gadget on the market for the money.

The variation between wine types comes down to natural preservatives. Alcohol, sugar, acid and tannin all inhibit the bacteria and slow the chemistry, so a fortified wine at 20% alcohol with high residual sugar resists spoilage for weeks, while a delicate low-alcohol white with neither has nothing defending it. Tannin in particular acts as an antioxidant buffer, which is why big reds outlast delicate whites by a day or two.

Upright storage for opened bottles is a direct consequence of the surface-area point. On its side, the wine's contact area with the headspace becomes a long oval up the length of the bottle; standing up, it's just the circle at the neck. Same volume of air, far less contact.

Sparkling wine's short window is a different failure mode again. Dissolved CO₂ is under pressure and escapes continuously once the bottle is opened; no stopper fully prevents it. The wine may still be perfectly sound as wine while being useless as sparkling wine — which is why the timeline for bubbles is shorter than the timeline for spoilage.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Leaving the bottle open during dinner | An hour of exposure costs more than a day of storage | Recork right after pouring |
| Leaving red wine on the counter because it's red | Room temperature roughly doubles oxidation speed | Fridge for storage; warm before serving |
| Storing an opened bottle on its side | Maximizes wine-to-air contact area | Store upright |
| Using a vacuum pump on sparkling wine | Pulls the dissolved CO₂ out; goes flat faster | Use a proper sparkling stopper |
| Freezing wine in a full sealed bottle | Expansion cracks the bottle | Leave headspace, or freeze in portions |
| Judging by the cork or by elapsed days | Oxygen exposure and storage temperature matter more | Smell and taste it |
| Pouring out wine that's merely dull | It's still good for cooking | Reduce into a sauce, braise, or make vinegar |
| Expecting all wine to last the same | Fortified and sweet wines last weeks; delicate whites last days | Check the type-specific window |

## Frequently Asked Questions
**How long does wine last after opening?**
Refrigerated and recorked: 3 to 5 days for most still wines, 1 to 3 days for sparkling, and 2 to 4 weeks for fortified wines like port and sherry. Left on the counter, cut those roughly in half.

**Should red wine be refrigerated?**
Yes. Cold does not harm red wine; it only mutes the aromatics temporarily. Take it out 20 to 30 minutes before serving, or let it warm in the glass. Refrigerated red lasts about twice as long as red left out.

**How can I tell if wine has gone bad?**
A sharp vinegar smell is the clearest sign, along with a sour taste that isn't the wine's normal acidity. Visual cues are browning in whites and dull brick tones in reds. Dull-but-not-sour wine is fine for cooking.

**Does a vacuum pump really work?**
Yes for still wine — it removes air from the headspace and buys a day or two. It's the wrong tool for sparkling wine, where the vacuum strips the dissolved carbonation and leaves it flat.

**Can I freeze leftover wine?**
Yes. Freeze in ice cube trays so you can drop portions straight into a pan. Leave headspace in any sealed container because wine expands when frozen. Use thawed wine for cooking rather than drinking.

**Why does my wine taste flat the next day even though it isn't spoiled?**
Volatile aroma compounds escape and oxidize quickly, well before actual spoilage begins. The wine is safe and drinkable but has lost the top notes that made it interesting.

**Does the cork have to go back in the same way?**
Fit matters more than orientation. A cork that seals tightly is what counts; if the original is swollen or crumbling, a rubber stopper with a good seal is more reliable.

**Is it worth buying a wine preservation system?**
Usually not for casual use. A clean small bottle plus the fridge gets you most of the benefit for almost nothing. Inert gas sprays and vacuum pumps add convenience, not a step change.
