An air purifier is a filter on the end of a pipe. It does not reduce how much pollution enters the room, it does not remove gases unless it has a specific carbon stage, and it does nothing at all about the source that is producing the pollution in the first place.

That is not an argument against purifiers. It is an argument about sequence. In a house with a gas stove, six candles a week, and windows that never open, a $300 purifier is a $300 bandage. The same money spent on nothing at all — just opening two windows and running the range hood — would produce a larger improvement in measured particle levels.

So this is ordered by what actually moves the numbers: source control, then ventilation, then filtration, then the two things that get oversold.

## What You Are Actually Trying to Remove

"Indoor air quality" bundles three different problems with three different solutions, and mixing them up is why people buy the wrong thing.

| Pollutant | Examples | What removes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Particulates** | Dust, pollen, smoke, cooking aerosol, skin flakes | Ventilation at the source, then mechanical filtration |
| **Combustion gases** | NO₂, CO, ultrafine particles from gas cooking | Exhaust hood vented outdoors, or not burning |
| **VOCs and odors** | Formaldehyde, terpenes from cleaners and air fresheners, cooking odors | Source removal, ventilation; only carbon filtration adsorbs them |
| **Moisture** | Condensation, damp surfaces | Humidity control — a separate problem, covered in the [moisture checklist](/fix-it/maintenance/find-moisture-problems-early/) |

A HEPA purifier does the particulates column and nothing else. If your complaint is "the house smells like last night's fish," a HEPA filter will not fix it, and neither will a plant.

## Layer 1: Source Control

This is where the largest gains are, and almost all of it is free.

**Cooking is the biggest single indoor source in most homes.** Frying generates more ultrafine particles than a busy road outside does, in a much smaller volume. Three changes:

- **Run the range hood every time you cook, including boiling.** Turn it on two minutes before the burner and leave it running ten to fifteen minutes after.
- **Check that the hood actually vents outdoors.** Recirculating hoods — the ones with no duct, just a filter — remove grease but return the combustion gases and most of the particles to the room. Hold a tissue against the filter while the fan runs; if it is not pulled toward the hood, it is not capturing.
- **Clean the hood filter** monthly. A clogged grease filter drops capture efficiency sharply, and it is one of the few maintenance tasks with a measurable payoff. The method is in [cleaning range hood filters](/home-hacks/appliances/clean-range-hood-filters/).

**Candles and incense** are small open combustion sources with no exhaust at all. A single scented candle can raise indoor particle counts to levels comparable to a cigarette in a small room, and it does it within minutes. If you burn them, burn them near an open window or a running extractor — or accept the trade.

**Scented products** — plug-ins, aerosol sprays, "air fresheners," scented laundry beads — are VOC delivery systems. They are the single easiest source to eliminate, and the improvement in how the house smells is usually immediate once they are gone, because you are no longer layering a fragrance over the actual air.

**Dust reservoirs.** Soft furnishings, carpets, and clutter are where settled dust gets re-suspended every time someone walks. Pet hair behaves the same way and is harder to get out once it is in a weave — [removing pet hair from everything](/home-hacks/cleaning/remove-pet-hair-everywhere/) is the mechanical counterpart to this section. This is why [reducing dust](/home-hacks/cleaning/reduce-dust-in-home/) works better as a strategy than dusting does as a chore: fewer surfaces holding dust means less of it in the air.

**What comes in on shoes.** A walk-off mat at every exterior door and a no-shoes rule cuts tracked-in soil substantially — the standard estimate is 70 to 85% of household dust originating outdoors, and shoes are the main vector. Two strides of mat (about six feet) captures most of it; a single small mat does almost nothing.

## Layer 2: Ventilation

Ventilation is the only measure that removes pollutants without needing to know what they are. It is free, and it is the one most people do badly.

**Cross ventilation, not a single open window.** Two openings on opposite or adjacent walls create a flow path. One open window in a closed room mostly just equalizes pressure and moves very little air. If the room has only one window, a fan blowing **out** of it pulls replacement air in through the rest of the house, which is far better than a fan blowing in.

**Ten to fifteen minutes is enough.** A full air exchange in a typical house takes about 10 to 20 minutes with two windows open, depending on wind and temperature difference. You do not need to leave windows open all day in winter — a short burst at the right time does most of the work.

**When to ventilate, in order of value:**

1. **While cooking** (and ten minutes after).
2. **After a shower** — run the bathroom fan 20 minutes afterward, not during only.
3. **After cleaning** with any product that has a smell.
4. **When the outdoor air is cleanest** — early morning in summer, midday in winter, and check a local AQI reading rather than guessing. Never ventilate during wildfire smoke or a high pollen period; that is when you close up and filter instead.

**Timing the windows to the outdoor air is the whole skill.** On a still, humid day, opening windows adds moisture. On a windy, dry day, it clears the house in minutes. Same action, opposite result.

## Layer 3: Filtration

Filtration matters when ventilation is not available or not enough — winter in a cold climate, wildfire season, an urban street, an allergy household.

**Start with the HVAC filter, not a portable unit.** It is already installed, it runs whenever the fan runs, and it costs $15 to $30 to upgrade.

| Rating | What it captures | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **MERV 6–8** | Pollen, dust, lint | Minimal airflow restriction — the safe default |
| **MERV 11** | Fine dust, mold spores, some smoke | Small pressure drop |
| **MERV 13** | Fine smoke particles, bacteria, most pollen | Meaningful restriction; check the system can handle it |
| **MERV 16 / HEPA** | Near-HEPA | Too restrictive for most residential ducted systems |

Two practical points. **MERV 13 is the practical ceiling for most homes** — pushing a standard blower against a denser filter reduces airflow, which can freeze a coil in summer and raise energy use year-round. And **change it on schedule**: a loaded filter has both low airflow and low capture, so a MERV 13 filter left in for a year performs worse than a MERV 8 changed quarterly. Every one to three months is the usual range, more often with pets.

**The run-the-fan trick.** Most people run the HVAC fan only when heating or cooling. Setting the fan to "on" or "circulate" for a few hours a day pushes air through the filter continuously, which measurably reduces particle counts between cycles. It costs a little electricity, and it extends filter life less than you would expect because the filter is doing more work.

**A box fan with a filter is real, but modest.** A 20-inch box fan with a MERV 13 filter taped to the intake produces a measurable reduction in a single closed room. It is loud and it is ugly, and its clean air delivery rate is a fraction of a purpose-built unit — but for one bedroom during wildfire season it does work, and it costs about $40.

Filtration is the third layer, and it only works if the first two are already in place. For the smoke-specific sequence — sealing, recirculation, one clean room — see [how to keep wildfire smoke out of the house](/seasonal/seasons/keep-wildfire-smoke-out/).

## Layer 4: The Two Things That Get Oversold

**Houseplants.** The famous NASA study that plants clean indoor air was conducted in sealed chambers with no air exchange, using volatile compounds at concentrations far above anything in a home. Scaling the result to a real room requires something in the range of hundreds of plants per square meter to match even a weak mechanical filter. Plants are good for other reasons; air purification is not one of them. If you like them, keep them — just do not count them.

**Ionizers and "air sanitizers."** Many generate ozone, which is itself a respiratory irritant, and the ones that do not still produce negligible particle removal compared with a filter. Ozone-generating devices are explicitly not recommended for occupied spaces by health agencies. If a device's marketing emphasizes "ions" or "active purification," it is usually working by a mechanism you do not want to breathe. The same skepticism is warranted for gadget fixes aimed at damp rather than particles, which is a different problem with a different checklist: [finding moisture problems early](/fix-it/maintenance/find-moisture-problems-early/).

## When a Purifier Is the Right Answer

Buy one when the source cannot be removed and ventilation is not possible. Concretely:

- **Wildfire smoke season** in a region where windows must stay shut for weeks.
- **A bedroom in a city** with high traffic exposure and a window that opens onto the street.
- **An allergy household** where pollen makes open windows a net negative.
- **A bedroom shared with a pet** you are not going to evict.

**What to look for:** a **CADR** (clean air delivery rate) at least two-thirds of the room's floor area in square feet, a true HEPA filter (not "HEPA-type"), and a noise level you can sleep through. A carbon stage only if odor or VOCs are the actual complaint — and note that carbon saturates and needs replacing, which most people never do.

**What to skip:** anything with an ionizer you cannot turn off, anything marketed by ozone output, and anything claiming to replace ventilation.

## Measuring Whether It Worked

You do not need a professional. Two cheap checks:

- **A $30 laser particle counter** (PM2.5) gives you a number before and after a change. It is not lab-grade, but it is directionally reliable and it settles arguments about whether the range hood does anything.
- **The overnight test.** Does your nose block in the morning? That is a crude but consistent indicator, and it responds within a week to source removal and ventilation changes.

The changes worth measuring first: range hood use, candles, scented products, and window timing. Those four are free and usually produce a larger change than any purchase.

## FAQ

**Do air purifying plants actually clean the air?**

Not meaningfully. The well-known NASA research used sealed chambers with no air exchange and unrealistically high concentrations. Replicating the effect in a real room would need hundreds of plants. Plants are fine to have; they are not a filtration strategy.

**How long should I open windows to air out a house?**

Ten to fifteen minutes with two openings on opposite sides of the house achieves roughly a full air exchange in most conditions. A single window in a closed room moves very little air. Ventilate during and after cooking, after showers, and when outdoor air quality is good.

**What MERV filter should I use at home?**

MERV 8 is the safe default and handles pollen and dust with minimal airflow restriction. MERV 13 captures fine smoke and is usable in most systems, but check that the blower can handle the pressure drop. Going above MERV 13 usually restricts airflow too much for residential ducted systems.

**Is a box fan with a filter as good as an air purifier?**

No, but it is not nothing. A 20-inch fan with a MERV 13 filter taped to the intake measurably reduces particles in one closed room for about $40. Its clean air delivery rate is a fraction of a purpose-built unit, and it is loud, but for one bedroom during a smoke event it works.

**Why does my house smell even after cleaning?**

Because cleaning products are themselves a source of VOCs, and because odors need ventilation rather than filtration. A HEPA filter removes particles and not gases. Open two windows for fifteen minutes, stop using scented products, and check the range hood — those three account for most persistent household smells.

**Are ozone air purifiers safe?**

No. Ozone is a respiratory irritant, and health agencies advise against ozone-generating devices in occupied spaces. Devices that advertise "ionization" or "active purification" often work by producing ozone or other reactive species you then breathe.

**How often should I change the HVAC filter?**

Every one to three months, more often with pets or during high pollen. A loaded filter has both reduced airflow and reduced capture, so an expensive MERV 13 left in for a year performs worse than a cheap MERV 8 changed quarterly.

**Does a range hood really improve indoor air?**

Only if it vents outdoors. Recirculating hoods remove grease and return the combustion gases and most particles to the room. Hold a tissue against the filter while the fan runs — if it is not pulled toward the hood, it is not capturing. Clean the grease filter monthly.

---

## Bottom Line

Stop the source first: run a vented range hood, cut candles and scented products, and put a real mat at every door. Then ventilate properly — two openings, ten to fifteen minutes, timed to when the outdoor air is clean. Then upgrade the HVAC filter to MERV 8 or 13 and change it on schedule. Buy a purifier only when the source cannot be removed and the windows have to stay shut, and skip anything that generates ozone.
