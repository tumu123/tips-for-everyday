A black T-shirt goes grey around the collar after a season. A wool sweater develops a rash of small fuzzy balls under the arms and along the cuffs. Both are described as "wearing out," and both are blamed on cheap fabric, but they are different failures with different mechanisms — and the fixes overlap in a way that makes them worth learning together.

Fading is dye leaving the fiber. Pilling is short fibers migrating to the surface and tangling. Neither is caused by "bad quality" in any useful sense, and neither is permanent or inevitable at the rate most people experience. What accelerates both is the same short list: mechanical friction, heat, and water.

## The Two Mechanisms, Precisely

**Fading** happens when dye molecules are broken down or washed out of the fiber. Four things do it:

- **Water plus heat**, which swells the fiber and lets dye migrate out. Hot water is dramatically worse than cold for this, especially on cotton dyed with reactive dyes.
- **Alkaline detergent in excess**, which raises the pH and accelerates dye hydrolysis in cellulose fibers.
- **Ultraviolet light**, which breaks the dye molecule directly. Sunlight is the single fastest way to grey a black garment.
- **Chlorine**, from bleach or from chlorinated water, which attacks dye and fiber at once. Even a splash of bleach in a wash destroys colour.

There is also a mechanical component that surprises people: dye that has migrated into the wash water can redeposit onto other garments in the same load, which is why a mixed dark load can come out looking duller than any single item went in.

**Pilling** happens when short fibers — staples — work their way out of the yarn structure and tangle into a ball on the surface. Three conditions produce it:

- **Short fiber staple length.** Cotton is spun from staples of a given length. Long-staple cotton (Pima, Egyptian) has fewer fiber ends protruding from the yarn, so fewer ends are available to migrate. Short-staple cotton has many more, and pills sooner.
- **Fiber blend.** This is the biggest factor. In a polyester-cotton blend, the two fibers have very different strength and elongation. The cotton fibers break first, and the stronger polyester filaments anchor the broken ends in place instead of letting the pill fall off. A 50/50 blend pills worse than either fiber alone.
- **Friction during wear and washing.** Under-arm areas, cuffs, and where a bag strap rubs are where pills form first, because that is where the mechanical action is.

The distinction that matters most: **natural-fiber pills eventually fall off; synthetic-fiber pills stay.** Wool and cotton pills shed over time, so a woollen sweater that has pilled may look better after a few more washes. A polyester fleece or an acrylic knit holds its pills indefinitely, which is why synthetic knits look worn sooner even when the fiber is stronger.

The counterintuitive consequence: a cheap cotton shirt often pills less than an expensive poly-cotton one, and a coarse woollen knit pills more than a smooth worsted one. Price correlates with staple length and spinning quality in cotton, but a high-quality blend still pills.

## Step 1: Sort by Colour Depth, Not Just Colour

The "whites, darks, colours" system most people use is not fine enough for dark garments, because the risk is not colour transfer to other clothes — it is dye loss from the dark item itself, and redeposition onto it.

- **New dark garments go in their own load for the first three washes.** A new black or navy item releases dye for several washes. Washing it with established darks dulls the older items and does not help the new one.
- **A colour-catching sheet in those loads** absorbs loose dye from the water. It is cheap and it measurably slows the dulling of a mixed dark load.
- **Wash dark denim and dark cotton separately** from black synthetics. Denim releases indigo heavily; synthetics are dye-receptive.
- **Never wash anything dark with a load that has been bleached**, even after a rinse. Residual hypochlorite in a machine or a detergent cup is enough to spot a black shirt.

## Step 2: Turn the Temperature Down and Leave It There

Cold water is the single highest-value change for colour retention, and the argument is not marginal.

Reactive dyes — the standard for cotton — bond to the fiber chemically, but the unreacted portion hydrolyses in water, and the rate roughly doubles for every 18°F of temperature increase. A wash at 100°F removes noticeably more dye per cycle than a wash at 60°F. Over fifty washes, that difference is the entire visible life of the garment.

- **Wash dark and coloured items at 60 to 75°F.** Modern detergents are formulated to work at these temperatures, and the colour benefit is large.
- **Reserve hot water for whites, towels, and bedding**, where the goal is soil and microbial removal and colour is not a concern.
- **Use the shortest cycle that cleans the load.** Agitation time is cumulative damage. A 30-minute normal cycle on a dark shirt, run fifty times, is 25 hours of tumbling.
- **Skip the extra rinse** unless there is a sensitivity issue. It is more water and more agitation for a marginal benefit.

## Step 3: Dose the Detergent Correctly, and Use Liquid for Darks

Overdosing detergent is the most common laundry mistake and it costs colour and fabric both.

Detergent that is not rinsed away stays in the fiber. It holds soil, it raises the local pH, and it attracts water — all of which accelerate dye loss and fiber degradation. In a high-efficiency machine it also produces excess suds that reduce the mechanical action.

- **Follow the dose for your water hardness and load size**, not the "maximum" line on the cap. For a lightly soiled dark load in soft water, it is often a third of the cap.
- **Use liquid rather than powder for dark loads.** Undissolved powder particles act as an abrasive on the fabric surface, and the brighteners in many powders are designed for whites — they deposit on dark fabric as a visible haze.
- **Avoid detergents with optical brighteners** for black and navy garments. The whitening effect that makes whites look bright makes darks look faded.
- **Skip fabric softener on towels and athletic wear.** It coats the fiber, reduces absorbency, and provides only a modest friction reduction. It does help on static in synthetics, which is a different problem.

## Step 4: Turn Everything Inside Out and Bag the Knits

Friction happens on the surface, so protect the surface.

- **Turn garments inside out** before washing. The outer face — the one that shows colour and the one that pills visibly — is then against itself rather than against zippers, buttons, and the drum.
- **Close zippers and fasten hooks.** An open zipper is a rasp. This is the main reason one garment in a load can ruin several others.
- **Bag knits, sweaters, and anything with a loose weave** in a mesh laundry bag. The bag reduces surface abrasion substantially and prevents stretching.
- **Wash jeans and heavy items separately** from knits. Denim is stiff, heavy, and abrasive when wet.
- **Do not overfill or underfill the drum.** A packed machine cannot agitate properly and rubs garments against each other under pressure; an almost-empty machine tumbles them violently against the drum. Around two-thirds full is the working range.

## Step 5: Get Drying Right — This Is Where Most Damage Happens

The dryer is the harshest environment a garment experiences: heat, tumbling, and a lint screen that is literally collecting the fibers coming off your clothes.

- **Air-dry knits and dark garments.** A drying rack eliminates the tumbling entirely. Turn dark items inside out and keep them out of direct sun; UV is the fastest route to a greyed black. It is also the cheaper option over a season, as the [cost comparison of air drying and a dryer](/save-money/bills/air-dry-vs-dryer-cost/) sets out.
- **If you use a dryer, use low heat** and remove items while slightly damp. Every additional ten minutes of tumbling past dry is pure fiber abrasion with no benefit.
- **Clean the lint screen every load.** A clogged screen extends drying time, which extends tumbling time. The lint in the screen is fiber that used to be in your clothes.
- **Do not tumble dry wool, cashmere, or anything with a brushed surface.** The brushing that gives fleece and flannel their softness is also what makes them pill fastest.
- **Shake out and smooth** before hanging. It reduces the wrinkles that would otherwise need heat and pressure to remove later.

## Step 6: Remove Pills by Cutting, Never by Pulling

When pills have formed, the repair matters as much as the prevention.

- **Use a fabric shaver or a fine-tooth pill comb.** A battery-powered fabric shaver cuts the pills at the surface and leaves the yarn intact. A comb lifts and cuts them.
- **Never pull pills off with your fingers.** Pulling draws more fiber out of the yarn, which produces a new pill in the same place, and over time it thins the fabric.
- **Never use scissors** unless the pill is large and isolated. The risk of cutting into the garment is high, and an accidental snip creates a hole that will grow.
- **Work on a flat surface with the garment stretched slightly**, in a single direction, with light pressure. Pressure pushes the fabric into the blade and cuts too deep.
- **Do one pass, then assess.** Repeated passes on the same area thin the fabric. A sweater can be shaved once or twice a season without harm; weekly shaving will wear a hole.

For wrinkle-prone garments that you would otherwise iron aggressively, note that pressing is also a form of heat damage — the care sequences for wool and silk, where this matters most, are in [how to wash delicates, wool, and silk](/home-hacks/laundry/wash-delicates-wool-silk/).

## What NOT to Do

**Do not use chlorine bleach on anything coloured**, at any dilution, for any reason. It does not "restore" a faded colour and it will not brighten a dark garment. It destroys dye permanently.

**Do not dry dark clothes in direct sun.** UV fading is faster than wash fading and it is irreversible. Shade or indoor drying, inside out.

**Do not wash denim more often than it needs.** Indigo is a surface dye applied by ring-dyeing, so the colour sits on the outer layer of the yarn by design. Every wash removes some. Spot-clean where you can — [the stain-by-stain chart](/home-hacks/laundry/remove-laundry-stains/) covers which marks come out without a full cycle — air between wears, and wash when it actually smells or is soiled.

**Do not use hot water to "sanitize" a normal load.** Unless someone in the household is ill or the load is bedding or towels, hot water buys you very little and costs you colour and fiber life.

**Do not rely on fabric softener to stop pilling.** It reduces surface friction slightly, but pills are formed by fiber migration from inside the yarn, which a surface coating does not address. It also reduces absorbency, which matters for towels and workout clothing.

**Do not assume an expensive garment will not pill.** Staple length and spinning quality help, but a soft, loosely spun luxury knit will still pill. The care routine is the variable you control.

## FAQ

**Why do black clothes turn grey instead of fading evenly?**

Because the failure is not uniform. Dye loss is concentrated where friction is highest — collars, cuffs, underarms, and anywhere a strap rubs — and the rest of the garment also picks up redeposited dye and detergent haze. The result is a mottled grey rather than an even fade.

**Does cold water really prevent fading?**

It substantially slows it. Dye hydrolysis and dye migration both accelerate with temperature, roughly doubling for every 18°F. Cold washing will not make a garment last forever, but over dozens of cycles the difference in colour retention is clearly visible.

**Why do only some sweaters pill?**

Staple length, yarn twist, and fiber blend. Short-staple fibers and loose spins produce more protruding fiber ends, and blends of a strong fiber with a weak one trap the pills instead of letting them shed. Wool and cotton pills shed naturally; acrylic and polyester pills stay.

**Can pills be prevented completely?**

No, but they can be reduced a great deal: wash inside out, bag the knits, keep the drum two-thirds full, use cold water and low heat, and air-dry. Pilling is a natural consequence of wearing and washing a fabric; the goal is to slow it, not to eliminate it.

**Should I use a fabric shaver on a wool sweater?**

Yes, with light pressure and a single pass. A fabric shaver removes the pills without pulling more fiber out. Do not use scissors, and do not shave the same area repeatedly — that thins the fabric.

**Is it better to dry clothes in the sun or in a dryer?**

For colour, shade or indoor air-drying is better than both sun and a hot dryer. Sun is worst for dark colours because of UV; a hot dryer is worst for fiber abrasion. If you must choose, low-heat drying is gentler on colour than direct sun.

**Why does my new dark shirt fade faster than an old one?**

New garments release unfixed dye for the first several washes, and washing them with other darks spreads that dye onto everything. Wash new dark items alone for the first three cycles, with a colour-catching sheet, and the loss drops sharply afterward.

## Bottom Line

Fading and pilling look like two problems and come from the same three inputs: friction, heat, and water. Wash darks and knits cold, inside out, in a two-thirds-full drum, with the correct dose of liquid detergent and no optical brighteners. Bag the knits, skip the softener on anything that needs to absorb water, and air-dry dark garments out of the sun. When pills appear, cut them with a shaver in one light pass rather than pulling them off. None of this is expensive, and all of it is cumulative — the garment that lasts five years instead of two is usually the one that was washed correctly, not the one that cost more.
