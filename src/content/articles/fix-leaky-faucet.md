The standard advice — "just replace the washer" — comes from an era when nearly every faucet used a compression valve with a rubber washer. Many still do, but if your faucet is a single-handle model, that advice sends you looking for a part that doesn't exist in it.

**Identify the faucet type first.** Everything else follows from that, and it takes about thirty seconds.

---

## Step 0: Which Faucet Do You Have?

| Type | How to recognize it | What wears out | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Compression** | Two separate handles (hot and cold). You turn them several times, and they feel firm to tighten | Rubber washer at the bottom of the stem | Replace the washer, sometimes the O-ring |
| **Ceramic disc cartridge** | Single handle that lifts up and swings. Smooth, short travel | The ceramic cartridge, or its seals | Replace the cartridge |
| **Ball valve** | Single handle over a visible round ball-shaped cap | Springs, seals, or the ball assembly | Replace springs and seals, or the whole assembly |
| **Cartridge (non-ceramic)** | Single handle; the internal cartridge is a cylinder or a flat plate | The cartridge body or its seals | Replace the cartridge |

**The fastest tell:** two handles almost always means compression. One handle means one of the three single-handle designs, and for leak purposes the treatment is similar — you're replacing a cartridge or its seals.

**If you're unsure which single-handle type you have,** you'll find out when you remove the handle and look inside. Take a photo at that point before disassembling further.

Low pressure at one faucet is a different fault from low pressure everywhere — [restoring pressure at a single faucet](/fix-it/plumbing/fix-low-water-pressure-faucet/) separates the two.

## Step 1: Identify Where It's Leaking

Location is diagnostic. Different symptoms mean different failed parts.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Drip from the spout, handle is off | Worn washer (compression) or failed cartridge seals | Replace washer or cartridge |
| Drip from the spout only when hot side is used | Hot-side washer or hot-side cartridge | Replace that side's part |
| Water pooling around the base of the handle | Worn O-ring on the stem, or a loose packing nut | Replace O-ring, or tighten the packing nut |
| Water at the base of the spout | Worn spout O-rings | Replace spout O-rings |
| Water leaking under the sink | Supply line connections, or a cracked body | Tighten or replace supply lines |
| Spray from the aerator | Debris in the aerator, or a failed aerator washer | Clean or replace the aerator |
| Low pressure, no leak | Clogged aerator | Clean the aerator |

**Start with the cheapest possibility.** A clogged or failed aerator is a two-minute fix and it's responsible for a meaningful share of "leaky faucet" complaints. Unscrew it, check the screen, and clean it with vinegar if it's mineral-coated.

## Step 2: Shut Off the Water

Do this before disassembling anything.

**Look for shutoff valves under the sink** — small oval handles or levers on the supply lines feeding the faucet. Turn them clockwise until snug. There's usually one for hot and one for cold.

**Test before proceeding.** Turn the faucet on. If nothing comes out, you're good. If water still flows, the valve isn't fully closed or has failed.

**If there are no shutoff valves, or they don't hold,** close the main water supply to the house. Know where that is before you start this job — it's usually near the water meter, in a basement, or in a utility closet.

**Plug the drain.** Screws, washers, and small parts go down the drain constantly otherwise. Put a towel or a drain plug over the opening.

---

## Step 3: Disassemble and Repair

**Document as you go.** Take a photo at each stage. Faucet reassembly is where most DIY jobs go wrong, and a few photos are worth more than trying to remember the order of eight small parts.

### Compression (two handles)

1. **Pry off the decorative cap** on the handle to expose the screw
2. **Remove the screw** and lift the handle off
3. **Unscrew the packing nut** with a wrench
4. **Lift out the stem** — the whole assembly comes up
5. **Remove the screw holding the washer** at the bottom of the stem, and replace the washer
6. **Check the O-ring** on the stem while it's out; replace it if it's flattened or cracked
7. **Inspect the valve seat** inside the faucet body with a flashlight. If it's scored or rough, it will chew through the new washer quickly
8. **Reassemble,** applying a thin film of plumber's grease to the new washer and O-ring
9. **Tighten the packing nut** only until the leak stops — overtightening makes the handle stiff

### Single-handle (cartridge, ceramic disc, ball)

1. **Locate the set screw** on the handle — often a small hex screw at the back or under a decorative cap
2. **Remove it with the Allen key** and lift the handle straight up
3. **Remove the retaining clip or nut** holding the cartridge. Ball valves have a collar and a cap; ceramic disc types have screws or a clip
4. **Lift out the cartridge**, noting its orientation — this matters on reassembly
5. **Take the old cartridge to the store** to match it exactly. Cartridges look similar but differ in height, spline count, and port configuration
6. **Install the new cartridge,** lubricating the seals with plumber's grease
7. **Reassemble** and test before fully tightening everything

### Mineral buildup

If you're in a hard water area and parts are coated white or green, soak removable components in white vinegar for 30 minutes and scrub gently. Scale damages seals and makes reassembly difficult.

If the drip continues with the handle fully off, the cartridge is worn — [replacing a dripping shower valve](/fix-it/plumbing/fix-dripping-shower-valve/) walks through the swap.

---

## Step 4: Reassemble and Test

1. **Turn the water back on slowly** — a gradual opening avoids pressure shock
2. **Leave the aerator off for the first test,** so debris flushes out rather than clogging it
3. **Run both hot and cold** for a minute each
4. **Check under the sink** with a dry cloth for any moisture at the connections
5. **Reinstall the aerator**
6. **Operate the handle through its full range** and check for drips at each position

## When to Replace the Faucet Instead

Repair isn't always the right call. Replace when:

| Situation | Why replace |
|---|---|
| The faucet body is corroded or cracked | Repair won't hold |
| Parts are discontinued | Finding a match costs more time than it's worth |
| The finish is badly degraded | Cosmetic, but often the tipping point |
| Multiple parts have failed | Cumulative cost approaches replacement |
| It's over 15–20 years old | Efficiency and parts availability both argue for replacement |
| You can't identify the model | Guessing at cartridges wastes trips |

**The cost comparison:** a cartridge typically costs a modest amount; a mid-range faucet costs substantially more but includes new supply lines and a warranty. If you've replaced the cartridge once and the faucet leaks again within a year, replacement is usually the better value.

Sort the job by what it costs to get wrong rather than by how hard it looks — [deciding whether a repair is DIY or a professional job](/fix-it/home-repair/diy-or-call-a-professional/) applies that rule to twenty common repairs.

## Why This Matters Financially

A slow drip is worth more than it looks. A faucet dripping roughly once per second wastes a surprising volume over a month, and if it's the hot side, you're paying to heat that water as well. A drip that seems cosmetic typically costs real money on both the water and the energy line — which is why utility companies flag it as one of the most common household wastes.

Beyond the bill, a persistent drip in a bathroom or kitchen sink accelerates mineral staining in the basin, which then becomes a separate cleaning problem.

---

## Why This Works

**Design determines the failure mode.** A compression faucet seals by pressing rubber against a metal seat, so rubber is the wear item. A ceramic disc faucet seals with two polished ceramic faces sliding against each other, so the cartridge is the wear item. The repair follows directly from which mechanism is doing the sealing.

**Water follows the path of least resistance, so leak location identifies the failed seal.** A drip from the spout means the seal at the valve isn't holding. Water at the handle base means the stem seal has failed. Water at the spout base means the spout seal has failed. Each symptom maps to a specific part.

**Mineral scale is both a cause and a complication.** Hard water deposits abrade seals and jam threads, which is why disassembly in hard-water areas often requires soaking parts rather than forcing them.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Assuming every faucet has a washer | You search for a part that isn't there | Identify the type first |
| Not shutting off the water | Flooding and lost parts | Close the angle valves, then test |
| Not plugging the drain | Small parts go down the pipe | Cover the drain |
| Forcing a corroded handle | Broken handle, damaged stem | Soak in vinegar, work gradually |
| Overtightening the packing nut | Stiff handle; the leak gets worse | Tighten only until the drip stops |
| Buying a cartridge by appearance | Wrong height or port layout | Bring the old one to match |
| Reassembling from memory | Parts left over, new leak | Photograph each stage |
| Skipping the under-sink check | Slow leak damages the cabinet | Check connections with a dry cloth |
| Ignoring a scored valve seat | The new washer wears out in weeks | Inspect and clean the seat |

## Frequently Asked Questions
**How do I know what type of faucet I have?**
Two handles almost always means compression. One handle means a cartridge, ceramic disc, or ball design — you'll confirm which once the handle is off.

**Why does my faucet drip only from the hot side?**
The hot-side washer or cartridge has failed. Heat and thermal cycling wear seals faster, so the hot side usually fails first.

**Can I fix it without turning off the water?**
No. Turn off the angle valves under the sink, or the main supply if there aren't any.

**How much water does a dripping faucet waste?**
More than most people expect — a one-per-second drip adds up to a substantial volume over a month, and if it's hot water you pay to heat it too.

**Is it worth repairing an old faucet?**
If parts are available and the body is sound, yes. If it's corroded, discontinued, or over 15–20 years old, replacement is usually better value.

**Why does my faucet still drip after replacing the cartridge?**
Most often the wrong cartridge, a scored valve seat, or debris caught in the seal. Check the seat and flush the line before reassembling.

**Do I need plumber's tape?**
On threaded pipe connections, yes. On compression fittings and cartridges, no — tape where it doesn't belong can prevent proper seating.

**Should I replace the supply lines while I'm there?**
If the faucet is old and the lines are rubber or show any corrosion, yes. It's inexpensive insurance against a leak under the sink.
