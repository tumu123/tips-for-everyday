Spring cleaning fails for one reason more than any other: people clean the rooms in the wrong order. They do the bedroom, then walk through the hallway with a full vacuum and a bucket, then clean the kitchen and carry dust and crumbs back into rooms that were already finished.

The order below fixes that. Dust and dirt travel **down and outward**, so cleaning always moves top-to-bottom and from the back of the house toward the exit.

**Time budget:** roughly 8 to 10 hours of actual work, best split across two days. Doing it in one push is possible but you'll start cutting corners by hour six.

---

## Step 1: Set up before you clean anything

Nothing you clean stays clean without a container for the things that don't belong.

1. **Put three boxes or bags by the front door** — one for trash, one for donation, one for "belongs in another room."
2. **Open the windows on the first warm day.** Ventilation isn't cosmetic: cleaning products and stirred-up dust both need air exchange, and a house that has been closed all winter smells noticeably different once it airs.
3. **Assemble your supplies in one caddy** so you're not jogging to the kitchen every ten minutes. Check what you already have before buying anything — most spring cleaning shopping lists are half unnecessary. See [How to Save Money on Cleaning Supplies](/save-money/shopping/save-money-cleaning-supplies/).
4. **Decide what you're skipping.** Be honest. A 12-hour plan you abandon at hour three leaves the house half-done.

## Step 2: Work top-to-bottom in every single room

Dust settles downward. Any surface you clean before the ceiling above it will need cleaning again.

In each room, in this order:

1. **Ceiling corners and ceiling fans.** A [wobbly or dust-caked ceiling fan](/fix-it/home-repair/fix-wobbly-ceiling-fan/) should be wiped with a damp cloth, not a dry duster, so dust sticks instead of launching into the air.
2. **Light fixtures and lampshades.** Dry-dust shades; wash glass globes only if they come off easily.
3. **Window frames, tracks, and blinds.** Skip taking blinds down — there's a faster method that gets the same result in [How to Clean Blinds Without Removing Them](/home-hacks/cleaning/clean-blinds-without-removing/).
4. **Wall art, mirrors, shelves, and the tops of frames.** The top edge of a picture frame is one of the dustiest surfaces in a house and one of the most ignored.
5. **Wall-washing where marked.** Only where you see real grime — around light switches, door handles, and behind furniture. Don't wall-wash whole rooms; it's slow and streaks.
6. **Furniture surfaces and under-furniture.** Move what you can. Vacuum or mop where it sat.
7. **Floors last.** Always.

## Step 3: Go room by room, back of the house to the front

Sequence matters more than effort. Start farthest from your exit so you never re-cross a finished floor.

**Bedrooms first.**

- **Strip and wash every bedding layer**, including mattress pads and pillow protectors.
- **Vacuum the mattress on both sides**, then spot-treat. A full method with dwell times is in [How to Deep Clean a Mattress](/home-hacks/cleaning/deep-clean-mattress/).
- **Pull furniture away from walls.** You'll find the winter's dust drifts and whatever slid under the bed.
- **Under the bed and behind the headboard** are the two biggest dust reservoirs in a bedroom.
- **Flip or rotate the mattress** if it isn't a no-flip model.

**Bathrooms second.**

- **Ventilation first:** run the exhaust fan or open the window while you work. Mold spores and cleaning fumes both need it.
- **Soak the showerhead and any fixtures** in vinegar while you clean elsewhere — contact time does the work.
- **Treat caulk lines and grout before scrubbing surfaces.** Mold in bathroom caulk needs contact time, not elbow grease. See [How to Remove Mold from Bathroom Caulk](/home-hacks/stains/remove-mold-bathroom-caulk/).
- **Wash the shower curtain or liner.** Machine-wash on gentle with a couple of towels; most mildew on liners is removable.
- **Then do sink, toilet, mirror, and floor** in that order.

**Living areas third.**

- **Upholstery and cushions** before floors, always.
- **Under couch cushions** — vacuum the crevice, and deal with any odor rather than masking it. See [How to Remove Kitchen Odors](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/remove-kitchen-odors/) for the approach, which transfers.
- **Electronics:** dust with a dry microfiber only, screen-safe. Never spray directly onto a screen.
- **Baseboards last in the room**, since you'll push dust down onto them anyway.

**Kitchen fourth.**

- **Empty and clean the refrigerator completely**, including shelves, drawers, and door gaskets. This is a good moment to reset what's in there — [How to Organize a Fridge to Waste Less Food](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/organize-fridge-less-food-waste/) covers the system.
- **Clean the range hood filters.** Degreasing, not just washing — greasy filters need a surfactant. See [How to Clean Range Hood Filters](/home-hacks/appliances/clean-range-hood-filters/).
- **Behind and under the fridge and stove**, if they're movable. This is the single dirtiest spot most kitchens have.
- **Wipe cabinet fronts**, especially above the handles and along the top edge where grease films form.
- **Descale the kettle and coffee maker** while you're at it — [How to Descale a Kettle and Coffee Maker](/home-hacks/appliances/descale-kettle-coffee-maker/).

**Entryway and hallway last.** Sweep, mop, and shake out mats. You finish here because this is the floor you'll walk on carrying everything out.

## Step 4: Handle the four things spring cleaning actually exists for

Seasonal cleaning earns its name by doing what weekly cleaning never touches:

1. **Windows, inside and out.** Do the exterior on a cloudy day — direct sun dries the glass before you can wipe it, leaving streaks.
2. **Under heavy furniture and appliances.** Once or twice a year is enough, and this is the once.
3. **Allergen reservoirs:** mattresses, pillows, upholstery, curtains, and rugs. Winter is spent with the house closed; these hold the season's dust.
4. **The air paths:** vents, returns, exhaust fans, and dryer vents. A clogged dryer vent is both an efficiency problem and a fire risk — see [How to Dry Laundry Without a Dryer](/save-money/bills/air-dry-vs-dryer-cost/) for the airflow angle.

## Step 5: Reset the storage, don't just clean around it

Cleaning without decluttering means putting everything back where it was, which is why it drifts again. Do these three passes before the boxes leave the house:

- **Clothes:** anything not worn this past season goes into proper storage, not the back of the closet. [How to Store Off-Season Clothes](/organize/closet/store-off-season-clothes/) covers the container and climate requirements — plastic bags are the wrong answer for most fabrics.
- **Closet:** pull everything out once and rebuild it. [How to Organize a Closet](/organize/closet/organize-a-closet/) uses a frequency-zoning method that survives the next season.
- **Cleaning supplies:** consolidate, discard anything separated or unlabeled, and store them where you actually use them. [How to Organize Cleaning Supplies](/organize/home-systems/organize-cleaning-supplies/).

Then get the donation boxes out of the house the same day. A box of donations that sits in the garage for a month becomes clutter again.

## Step 6: Do the maintenance items while the house is open

Spring cleaning is the natural time for the seasonal maintenance pass, and doing them together saves a second setup. The full sequence lives in [Seasonal Home Maintenance Checklist](/fix-it/maintenance/seasonal-home-maintenance-checklist/). The spring-specific items:

- **Test smoke and carbon monoxide detectors**, replace batteries.
- **Check for winter damage:** cracked caulk, loose flashing, damaged roof shingles, and popped gutters.
- **Inspect exterior for water damage** after the freeze-thaw cycle of winter.
- **Service or at minimum clean the AC** before the first hot week, not during it.
- **Check the water heater** — sediment settles over winter. See [Water Heater Maintenance](/fix-it/maintenance/water-heater-maintenance/).

## Step 7: Know when you're done

A spring clean is finished when:

- Every floor has been cleaned after every surface above it.
- Windows are done inside and out.
- Beds, upholstery, and rugs have been treated for allergens.
- There are no boxes left sitting around.
- You wrote down what still needs fixing.

That last point matters. Spring cleaning surfaces a list of repairs — a sticking door, a dripping faucet, a cracked tile. Write it down instead of trusting memory, then work through it. [How to Fix a Sticking Door](/fix-it/home-repair/fix-sticking-door/) and [How to Fix a Leaky Faucet](/fix-it/plumbing/fix-leaky-faucet/) are the two most common entries.

## Why This Works

The top-to-bottom rule isn't tradition — it's physics. Dust moves with gravity and air currents, so anything cleaned below an uncleaned surface gets re-soiled. Cleaning a ceiling fan after wiping a shelf means redoing the shelf.

Back-to-front sequencing follows the same logic applied to movement. Every trip through a room deposits whatever is on your shoes and whatever falls off what you're carrying. Working from the far end toward the exit means each room is crossed one fewer time after being cleaned.

Doing bedrooms and bathrooms before kitchens isn't arbitrary either. Bedrooms and bathrooms hold the allergen load (dust mites, skin cells, mildew), and they take the most time. Kitchens and entries are grease-and-grit problems that resolve quickly. Front-loading the heavy rooms means you finish them while your attention is best.

The declutter-before-clean order is the part most people get wrong, and it's the reason results don't last. Cleaning around objects leaves the same quantity of stuff in the same locations; the reduction in surface dust buys a few weeks at best. Removing items changes how fast the space re-accumulates mess, because every object is a surface that collects dust and obstructs cleaning.

Finally, grouping maintenance with cleaning works because both need the same conditions — house opened up, furniture moved, and time set aside. Separating them means duplicating all three.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Cleaning bottom-up in a room | Dust settles onto surfaces you already finished | Ceiling → walls → furniture → floor |
| Starting with the kitchen or entryway | You track grease and grit into clean rooms | Bedrooms and bathrooms first, entryway last |
| Wall-washing entire rooms | Slow, streaks, and usually unnecessary | Spot-wash around switches, handles, and behind furniture |
| Taking all the blinds down | High effort for little gain over in-place cleaning | Clean them hung, see the linked method |
| Using a dry duster on ceiling fans | Launches dust across the room | Damp cloth, or a pillowcase over each blade |
| Skipping the mattress and upholstery | Leaves winter's allergen load in place | Vacuum both; this is the seasonal point |
| Cleaning windows in direct sun | Glass dries before you wipe → streaks | Shade or overcast, and work in sections |
| Decluttering after cleaning | You put everything back and it re-drifts | Declutter first, remove donations same day |
| Skipping the maintenance pass | Duplicates setup later | Do detectors and damage checks now |
| Buying a full new supplies kit | Half of it won't get used | Inventory first, then buy only what's missing |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What order should I spring clean a house in?**
Work top-to-bottom within each room (ceiling, walls, surfaces, floor) and room-by-room from the back of the house toward the entryway. Do bedrooms and bathrooms first since they hold the most allergen load, and the entryway last since you'll walk through it carrying things out.

**How long does spring cleaning take?**
Roughly 8 to 10 hours of work for an average three-bedroom home — realistically two days. A single-room spring clean takes 45 to 90 minutes depending on how much decluttering is involved.

**Should I declutter before or after cleaning?**
Before. Cleaning around objects leaves the same quantity of stuff in place, and every object is a surface that collects dust. Declutter first, then clean the emptied space — the result lasts substantially longer.

**Do I need to take blinds down to clean them?**
No. Most blinds can be cleaned in place by closing the slats and wiping both sides with a damp microfiber wrap, which is faster and avoids re-hanging. Take them down only if they're greasy or moldy.

**Is spring cleaning the same as deep cleaning?**
Not quite. Deep cleaning is the intensive method applied to one area at a time. Spring cleaning is the whole-house pass that incorporates deep cleaning plus seasonal tasks like windows, allergen reservoirs, and maintenance checks.

**What should I clean first, windows or floors?**
Windows first, always. Glass cleaner and water drip downward and will mark a freshly mopped floor. Do all glass, then all floors.

**How often do I actually need to spring clean?**
The whole-house version once a year is right for most homes. If you have allergies, pets, or a humid climate, add a second full pass in the fall — which is the natural time for it anyway.

**Can I spring clean without harsh chemicals?**
Yes. Vinegar handles scale and light mineral film, baking soda handles abrasion and odor, and dish soap handles grease. What vinegar won't do is kill mold or disinfect — for those, use an appropriate product. There's a full recipe in [DIY All-Purpose Cleaner](/home-hacks/cleaning/diy-all-purpose-cleaner/).
