**"The freezer is fine but the fridge is warm" is the most diagnostically useful refrigerator symptom there is.** It rules out a large category of failures immediately and points at a small one.

If the compressor had failed, or the system had lost refrigerant, both compartments would be warm. The fact that the freezer still holds sub-zero temperature proves the compressor runs, the refrigerant circuit is intact, and the sealed system is doing its job.

What's left is the machinery that *moves* cold air from the freezer into the fridge, and the machinery that keeps the evaporator from icing over. On most refrigerators those are the same system, and its two common failures produce exactly this symptom.

---

## Step 1: Understand the Layout Before You Open Anything

Almost every refrigerator sold in the last 40 years is a **single-evaporator** design. There is one evaporator coil, located in the freezer compartment. The freezer gets cold directly from that coil. The refrigerator compartment gets cold from air that is blown from the freezer through a duct, controlled by a flap called the **air damper**.

That single fact explains the symptom. If anything blocks or stops the airflow from freezer to fridge — ice on the coil, a stuck damper, a dead fan — the freezer stays cold (it's next to the coil) and the fridge goes warm (it's downstream).

**Three components sit in that air path:**

1. **The evaporator fan** — blows air across the coil and through the duct
2. **The air damper** — a motorised flap that opens to let cold air into the fridge
3. **The defrost system** — timer or control board, defrost heater, and terminating thermostat, which periodically melts ice off the coil

Most "freezer fine, fridge warm" failures are one of these three.

**Some refrigerators are dual-evaporator or dual-compressor** — typically higher-end French-door and side-by-side models. Those have independent cooling circuits, and a fridge-side failure can be a sealed-system problem after all. Check your model: if it has two evaporators, this guide's air-path logic doesn't apply, and the diagnosis starts over.

## Step 2: The Five-Minute Checks First

Before tools, rule out the things that look like cooling failure but aren't.

| Check | What to do | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature setting** | Confirm the fridge is set to 37–40 °F, not accidentally on a low setting | A setting knocked during cleaning |
| **Vents inside the fridge** | Find the cold-air outlet and confirm it isn't blocked by a shelf, bin, or a package pushed against it | Airflow blocked inside the compartment |
| **Door seal** | Close the door on a dollar bill; if it slides out easily, the gasket isn't sealing | Warm air infiltration |
| **Door left ajar** | Check for something preventing full closure | Operator error, which is common |
| **Recent power outage** | A fridge takes 12–24 hours to recover | Not a failure at all |
| **Frost visible on the back wall of the freezer** | Note where and how much | Confirms the defrost diagnosis |

**A dollar-bill test that fails at one spot** means a gasket problem, not a cooling problem. Gaskets are replaceable and cheap. A gasket failure on the fridge door produces a warm fridge and a healthy freezer, so it mimics the exact symptom.

**Look for ice on the freezer's back wall.** Heavy frost, especially a solid block, is the signature of a defrost failure. A clean back wall with no frost means the defrost system is working and the problem is airflow or the damper.

## Step 3: The Defrost System — The Most Common Cause

Here is the mechanism. Every refrigerator cycles through a defrost period — typically every 8 to 12 hours of compressor runtime — during which a heater melts the frost that has accumulated on the evaporator coil. If that cycle fails, frost builds up until the coil is encased in ice. The ice blocks the air passages, and the fridge stops getting cold while the freezer, sitting right next to the coil, stays cold for days.

**Three parts can fail, and they fail in a predictable order:**

| Part | Function | Failure symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **Defrost heater** | Melts frost off the coil | Coil iced solid; heater tests open |
| **Defrost thermostat / bimetal** | Ends the defrost cycle at a set temperature | Heater never runs, or runs too long |
| **Defrost timer or control board** | Starts the defrost cycle on schedule | No defrost ever; sometimes erratic cooling |

**The manual defrost test — the single most useful diagnostic here:**

1. **Empty the freezer** and put the food in a cooler with ice.
2. **Unplug the refrigerator** and leave both doors open for 24 hours, with towels down.
3. **Plug it back in** and let it run 24 hours.
4. **Check the fridge temperature.**

If the fridge returns to normal, the sealed system is fine and you have a defrost system failure. The fridge will work correctly for 5–10 days and then go warm again as ice rebuilds — which confirms the diagnosis completely.

**The test is not a fix.** It's a way of proving where the fault is with no tools. If it works, you know to test the heater, the thermostat, and the timer.

**Testing the parts:**

- **Heater:** unplug, disconnect the leads, test continuity. Open = failed. Heaters are usually $30–$70.
- **Defrost thermostat:** should have continuity when cold (it's a normally-closed bimetal). No continuity when cold = failed. About $15–$30.
- **Timer or board:** if heater and thermostat both test good, the timer isn't initiating defrost. Mechanical timers are cheap; control boards are not.

**The heater and thermostat fail together more often than people expect** — a failed thermostat can let the heater overrun and burn out, or a failed heater leaves the thermostat untested for years. Replacing both at once is common practice and rarely wasteful.

## Step 4: The Evaporator Fan

The fan that blows air across the coil and into the fridge is in the freezer, behind a panel at the back. If it stops, the freezer still gets cold by conduction and radiation, but no cold air reaches the fridge.

**How to tell:** open the freezer door and listen. A running evaporator fan produces a distinct whir that stops when you press the door switch. If you hear nothing, or hear a grinding or squealing, the fan is the problem.

**Causes, in order:**

1. **Ice jamming the fan blade** — the defrost failure again, presenting as a fan failure. Clear the ice and the fan turns freely.
2. **Failed fan motor** — the motor seizes or the windings open. $30–$80.
3. **Obstruction** — a package or a piece of ice in the blade.

**A fan that runs only intermittently** is normal on many models — the control cycles it with the compressor. Don't diagnose a fan as failed because it isn't running at the moment you look.

---

## Step 5: The Air Damper

The damper is a motorised flap at the top of the fridge compartment, where the cold air enters. It opens and closes to regulate fridge temperature based on a sensor.

**Failure modes:**

- **Stuck closed** — freezer cold, fridge warm, and you can feel no cold air coming from the vent. This is the classic damper symptom.
- **Stuck open** — fridge too cold, freezing food near the vent. Opposite complaint, same part.
- **Motor failed** — the flap doesn't move when the control tells it to.

**How to check:** with the fridge running, feel for airflow at the cold-air vent inside the fridge. Then locate the damper — usually behind a cover at the top rear of the fridge compartment — and manually move the flap. If it moves freely but never actuates on its own, the motor or the control signal has failed.

Damper assemblies are model-specific and range from $40 to $150. Some are replaceable as a unit; others require the whole air-duct assembly.

---

## Step 6: The Thermistor — The Sensor Nobody Tests

Modern refrigerators use thermistors — temperature-sensitive resistors — to report compartment temperatures to the control board. A thermistor that reads wrong makes the board think the fridge is colder than it is, so it never calls for more cooling.

**Symptoms:** fridge consistently warm by a fixed amount, no ice on the coil, fan and damper both working, defrost working. Nothing is mechanically broken; the system just doesn't know it's warm.

**Testing:** thermistors have a resistance that varies with temperature, and the resistance values are published in service documentation. At room temperature most read in the 5–10 kΩ range. A reading far outside that, or an open circuit, indicates failure.

**This is the failure that gets missed** because it produces no visible evidence. If every mechanical check passes and the fridge is still warm, test the thermistors — a $15 part that ends a long hunt.

## Step 7: When It Really Is the Sealed System

The one case where "freezer fine, fridge warm" can still be a refrigerant problem is a **dual-evaporator refrigerator** with a fridge-side leak, or a single-evaporator unit in the early stages of a partial restriction.

**Signs the sealed system is the problem:**

- The compressor runs constantly and never shuts off
- The condenser coils (back or bottom) are barely warm
- The evaporator coil has frost only in one small area, not evenly
- Both compartments slowly warm over weeks rather than the fridge failing suddenly
- A hissing or gurgling sound from the back

**This is a professional repair, without exception.** Refrigerant handling requires certification, and a sealed-system repair on a domestic refrigerator routinely costs more than half the price of a new one. On a machine past 10 years, replacement is usually the better answer.

## Step 8: Food Safety While You Diagnose

A refrigerator that's been above 40 °F for more than two hours has a food safety problem, independent of the repair.

| Food | Safe above 40 °F for |
|---|---|
| Raw meat, poultry, fish | Under 2 hours — discard beyond that |
| Dairy | 2 hours, then evaluate |
| Cooked leftovers | 2 hours |
| Hard cheese, butter | Longer — these tolerate warmth better |
| Produce | Mostly fine for a day |

**Don't taste-test to decide.** [How to tell if food has gone bad](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/how-to-tell-if-food-is-bad/) covers the sensory checks, but the time-and-temperature rule is more reliable than smell. When in doubt, discard — the cost of replacing food is lower than the cost of the alternative.

If the fridge will be off for a day or more, [safe thawing and refreezing](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/thaw-food-safely/) explains what can be kept and how.

## Why This Works

**One evaporator, two compartments, one air path.** The freezer is cold because it surrounds the coil. The fridge is cold only because air is pushed to it. Everything downstream of the coil can fail without affecting the freezer's temperature at all — which is why this symptom narrows the diagnosis so sharply.

**Ice is an insulator and a barrier at the same time.** A frosted coil loses heat transfer efficiency, and the ice physically blocks the air passages between the fins. Both effects compound: less cooling produced, and less of it delivered.

**Defrost runs on compressor runtime, not wall-clock time.** That's why a defrost failure takes days to show. The refrigerator works normally until enough frost has accumulated to block airflow, typically 5 to 10 days. This delay is why people think the problem is intermittent — and why the manual defrost test is so conclusive.

**The compressor's behaviour is the tell for the sealed system.** A compressor that runs continuously and never cycles off is working against a system that isn't transferring heat. Combined with a cool condenser, that points at refrigerant rather than air movement.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Chipping ice off the evaporator with a knife | Punctures the coil; sealed-system failure | Melt it with a hair dryer or let it defrost |
| Replacing the defrost heater without testing the thermostat | New heater burns out the same way | Test both, replace as a pair |
| Assuming the compressor failed because the fridge is warm | Paying for a system that's fine | Check whether the freezer is cold first |
| Blocking the cold-air vent with bins | Warm fridge, working appliance | Keep 2 inches clear around vents |
| Turning the fridge to maximum to compensate | Compressor runs constantly; freezes food near the vent | Find the fault |
| Ignoring a gasket that fails the dollar-bill test | Constant warm-air infiltration | Replace the gasket |
| Running a fridge on an extension cord | Voltage drop damages the compressor | Dedicated outlet |
| Packing the fridge completely full | Blocks airflow; uneven cooling | Leave space for air to move |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Why is my freezer cold but the fridge warm?**
Almost always a defrost failure, a stuck air damper, or a failed evaporator fan. All three block the path that carries cold air from the freezer to the fridge.

**How can I tell if it's the defrost system?**
Look for heavy frost on the freezer's back wall. If there's ice, defrost is failing. A 24-hour manual defrost that restores normal operation confirms it.

**Is a warm fridge an emergency?**
For food safety, yes — 40 °F for over two hours means raw meat and dairy are at risk. For the appliance, no; a few days of diagnosis won't make the repair worse.

**Can I just turn the fridge setting colder?**
It won't help if the air path is blocked. It will freeze food near the vent and make the compressor run constantly.

**How long should a manual defrost take?**
24 hours with the doors open, or about an hour with a hair dryer on the coil (never a heat gun, never a knife).

**My fridge works after defrosting but fails again in a week. What next?**
That's a confirmed defrost system failure. Test the heater, the bimetal thermostat, and the timer or control board, and replace the failed part.

**Is it worth repairing a 12-year-old fridge?**
Depends on the part. A $50 heater or damper, yes. A sealed-system repair or a control board on a 12-year-old machine, usually no — put the money toward a new one.

**How long should a refrigerator last?**
12–15 years on average. Compressor and sealed-system life is longer than the electronics and the plastic air-path components.

## Bottom Line

"Freezer fine, fridge warm" is a gift of a symptom. It eliminates the compressor and the refrigerant circuit, and leaves a short list: defrost system, evaporator fan, air damper, thermistor, gasket.

Check in that order, and use the manual defrost test to confirm the defrost diagnosis before buying any parts. If the fridge comes back to normal after 24 hours off and fails again five days later, you know exactly what's wrong — and it's a $50 part, not a $900 appliance.
