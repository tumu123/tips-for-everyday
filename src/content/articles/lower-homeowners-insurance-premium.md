Most advice about homeowners insurance stops at "shop around." That is worth doing, but it treats the premium as a price tag when it is really the output of a formula. If you change the inputs, the output changes — and you can often get a materially lower premium from the *same* carrier, with the *same* coverage.

**The levers, roughly ranked by how much control you have:**

| Lever | Your control | Typical effect | Catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deductible | Full | Noticeable, but not linear | You self-insure the difference |
| Mitigation features | High | Moderate | Must be documented and requested |
| Coverage accuracy | High | Moderate | Requires an honest rebuild-cost number |
| Claims frequency | Partial | Large over time | Small claims cost more than they pay |
| Bundling | Full | Moderate | Can raise the other policy's price |
| Credit-based factors | Slow | Large in most states | Takes time to move |
| Replacement cost | Low | Sets the base | You cannot safely understate it |

The theme underneath all of these: insurers price *risk they can measure*. Your job is to give them better numbers, and to stop triggering the signals that raise them.

## 1. Understand What Actually Sets the Number

A homeowners premium is built from a small set of variables. Most are not negotiable, but knowing which is which stops you wasting effort.

**Largely outside your control:**

- **Replacement cost** — the estimated cost to rebuild the structure. This is the single biggest driver. It moves with local construction costs and material prices, which is why premiums rise in years when you did nothing at all.
- **Location** — weather exposure, wildfire or hail zones, distance to a fire hydrant or station, and local claim history.
- **Construction** — roof material and age, foundation type, wiring and plumbing type, year built.

**Partly in your control:**

- **Roof age and material** — the highest-weighted single feature in most models. A roof replacement can move a premium meaningfully, and impact-resistant materials can qualify for a credit.
- **Protective devices** — monitored alarms, water-leak shutoff sensors, sprinklers, storm shutters, reinforced garage doors.
- **Claims history** — both yours and, in some models, the prior owner's for the same address.

**Fully in your control:**

- Deductible, coverage limits, endorsements you may not need, payment method, bundling, and who you ask for discounts.

Start by pulling your **declarations page** and reading it once, properly. Most people have never read it. It lists your deductible, your limits, and every discount currently applied — which tells you exactly which credits you are missing.

The premium is only one number the county sets — [the assessment behind the tax](/save-money/big-decisions/appeal-property-tax-assessment/) covers the other, and the short window for disagreeing with it.

## 2. Raise the Deductible — but Understand the Curve

Raising the deductible is the most direct lever, and the one most often oversold.

The relationship is **not linear**. Carriers price the deductible as a discount on the portion of risk they no longer cover. Moving from $500 to $1,000 typically produces a larger premium reduction than moving from $2,500 to $5,000, because the dollar amounts of claims you absorb grow much faster than the risk you remove.

**The decision rule:**

1. Find the annual premium difference between deductible options. Your carrier or agent can quote two or three in minutes.
2. Compare that annual saving against the *additional* amount you would pay out of pocket in a claim.
3. Ask how many claim-free years it takes for the savings to cover the gap.

If a $500-to-$2,500 change saves you $220/year, you need roughly nine claim-free years to break even on the $2,000 difference. That is often a good bet — but it is only a good bet if a $2,500 surprise payment would not hurt you. **A deductible is a decision about your emergency fund, not about insurance.**

Also check whether your policy uses a **percentage deductible** for wind or hail rather than a flat dollar amount. A 2% wind deductible on a $400,000 dwelling limit is $8,000, and this is the detail that surprises people after a storm.

## 3. Ask for Every Credit — Most Do Not Apply Themselves

This is the highest-value step per minute spent, and the one people skip.

Mitigation credits generally require you to **provide evidence and ask**. Carriers do not send an inspector to your house on a hunch. Typical credits include:

- Monitored burglar or fire alarm (central station monitoring, not just a local siren)
- Water leak detection with automatic shutoff — increasingly valuable as claims data accumulates
- Interior sprinkler systems
- Impact-resistant roofing or a roof under a certain age
- Storm shutters or impact-rated glazing
- Reinforced garage door or roof-to-wall connectors in wind zones
- Smart home devices that monitor for smoke, freeze, or water
- Being claim-free for a set number of years
- Paying annually rather than monthly, and using autopay
- Bundling home with auto
- Loyalty or renewal tenure
- Non-smoker household, in some states
- Professional affiliation, alumni, or employer group programs

**How to do it efficiently:** call and ask for a **coverage review**, not a quote. Ask specifically: "Which discounts am I not currently receiving, and what documentation do you need for each?" Then send the documentation — a photo of the alarm panel, the roof invoice with the material listed, the shutoff valve installation receipt. A single email with three attachments is often worth more than an hour of comparison shopping.

## 4. Make Sure the Coverage Number Is Honest

Two errors cost money in opposite directions.

**Over-insuring** — if your dwelling limit is based on an outdated estimate that includes finished square footage you do not have, or uses a market value rather than a rebuild cost, you may be paying to insure value that cannot be claimed. Rebuild cost and sale price are different numbers, and a good policy is priced on the former.

**Under-insuring** — more dangerous, and more common after renovations. If your limit no longer reflects what rebuilding would actually cost, you may face a coinsurance penalty at claim time, where the carrier pays a reduced proportion of the loss.

Ask your carrier to re-run the replacement cost estimate every two or three years, and after any significant renovation. Confirm what **ordinance or law coverage** you have — this pays for bringing a damaged structure up to current code, which is frequently where budgets break during a rebuild.

Also look at the endorsements. Some are genuinely useful; others duplicate coverage you already carry elsewhere. **Scheduled personal property** for jewelry or instruments is usually better value than raising your general contents limit, because it can remove the sub-limit and the deductible on those items.

## 5. Stop Filing Small Claims

Insurers price on **frequency** more heavily than severity, because frequency is the better predictor of future loss.

If a repair costs $1,200 and your deductible is $1,000, filing puts $200 in your pocket and a claim on a record that follows the property — and in some underwriting models, follows you. Two of those in three years can cost far more than $400 in increased premiums and lost claim-free credits.

**A practical threshold:** consider handling repairs yourself when the cost is less than roughly two to three times your deductible. Use insurance for losses you genuinely cannot absorb, which is what it is for.

Keep a simple home inventory regardless — photos or a short video walkthrough of each room, stored somewhere other than the house. It does not lower your premium, but it is the difference between a fast settlement and a disputed one.

Deciding not to file is only half the strategy — [filing the claim properly](/save-money/big-decisions/file-home-insurance-claim/) covers what to photograph, what to say to the adjuster, and why the first day decides most of the payout.

## 6. Shop Strategically, Not Constantly

Re-shopping every year sounds disciplined but can work against you. Frequent switching can forfeit loyalty credits, and some carriers price new business aggressively and then rebuild the premium at renewal.

A reasonable cadence:

- **Every two to three years**, get two or three competing quotes with identical limits and deductibles. Identical is the important word — a lower quote for less coverage is not a saving.
- **At a renewal increase you cannot explain**, ask why. Premiums rise for reasons you can sometimes address (a roof reaching a certain age, a credit-based factor changing, a regional rate filing). The same tactics that work when [negotiating household bills](/save-money/bills/negotiate-household-bills/) apply to a renewal conversation.
- **After a major improvement** — new roof, alarm, shutoff valve — re-shop immediately, because that is when you are most attractive to a new carrier.

When comparing, weigh the **financial strength rating** of the carrier, not just the price. A policy is a promise to pay years from now.

---

## 7. Check the Two Things That Quietly Raise Bills

**Escrow and payment handling:** if your mortgage escrows the premium, a rise in premium also raises your monthly payment, and an escrow shortage can make it feel like a second increase. Confirm whether the change came from the policy or the escrow.

**Automatic coverage escalators:** many policies include an inflation-guard clause that raises your dwelling limit — and premium — each year, sometimes well above actual local construction inflation. It is not a bad feature, but it should be reviewed rather than left on autopilot.

## Why This Works

Premiums are arithmetic. Carriers apply discounts they can verify and charge for risk they can measure, which means the two things a policyholder can do are: supply verifiable evidence of lower risk, and stop supplying evidence of higher risk.

The reason "shop around" alone underperforms is that you carry the same inputs to the next carrier. If your roof age is wrong, your deductible is mispriced for your situation, and three credits are unclaimed, the next quote inherits all of it. Fixing the inputs lowers every quote you ask for — including the one from the carrier you already have.

The deductible point deserves emphasis because it is the most commonly misused lever. The saving is real but sub-linear, and it converts an insured loss into an uninsured one. It works when you have the reserves to absorb the gap, and it backfires when you do not.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Dropping the dwelling limit to shrink the premium | Sets up a coinsurance penalty at claim time. Under-insuring converts a manageable loss into a serious one. |
| Raising the deductible without the cash to cover it | Turns a monthly saving into a potential financial emergency. The premium reduction is small compared with the exposure. |
| Assuming discounts are applied automatically | Most mitigation credits require documentation and a request. Unclaimed credits are the most common source of overpayment. |
| Filing a claim just above the deductible | Frequency drives pricing more than severity. A small recovery can cost more in future premium than it pays. |
| Comparing quotes with different deductibles or limits | Produces a meaningless comparison. Match every variable except price before deciding. |
| Ignoring the wind or hail deductible | Percentage deductives can be many thousands of dollars. This is discovered during a claim, when it is too late to change. |
| Choosing on price alone | A policy is a promise to pay years later. Carrier financial strength matters as much as the number. |
| Letting the replacement cost estimate go stale | Both directions cost money — you either pay for value you cannot claim or risk a coinsurance penalty. |

## FAQ

**How much does raising the deductible actually save?**
Typically a meaningful percentage of the premium, but the savings diminish as you go higher. The $500-to-$1,000 step usually gives a larger discount than a $2,500-to-$5,000 step. Ask your carrier to quote two options side by side rather than assuming a rule of thumb.

**Why did my premium go up when I never filed a claim?**
The most common reasons are regional rate filings after a bad weather year, rising local construction costs that raise your replacement cost estimate, a roof crossing an age threshold, and changes to credit-based insurance factors where those are permitted. None of these require anything you did.

**Does bundling home and auto always save money?**
Usually, but not always. Confirm the total of both policies rather than the bundle discount alone, and re-check at each renewal, because discounts can shrink while individual rates rise.

**Will a new roof lower my premium?**
Often yes, and it is one of the highest-weighted features in most pricing models. Ask for the credit and provide the invoice showing the installation date and material, since impact-resistant materials can qualify for more than standard ones.

**Are water leak sensors worth installing for the discount?**
Increasingly yes. Water damage is among the most frequent and expensive claim types, so credits for monitored shutoff systems have grown. Confirm with your carrier which devices qualify before buying.

**How often should I shop for home insurance?**
Every two to three years is reasonable, plus immediately after a major improvement or an unexplained renewal increase. Annual switching can cost you loyalty credits and only sometimes pays off.

**Does my credit affect my home insurance rate?**
In most states, yes — insurers use credit-based insurance scores as a pricing factor. Improving payment history and reducing high utilisation can help over time, though it is a slow lever compared with deductible or discount changes.

**Is a home inventory worth making if it does not lower the premium?**
Yes. It does not reduce what you pay, but it dramatically speeds up and strengthens a contents claim. A room-by-room video stored off-site takes under an hour.

**Should I pay monthly or annually?**
Paying in full is usually cheaper, because monthly plans often carry installment or service fees. If your carrier escrows through your mortgage, ask whether a lump-sum payment is accepted and how it affects escrow.

**What is ordinance or law coverage, and do I need it?**
It pays the extra cost of rebuilding to current building code after a covered loss. Older homes are the ones most affected, since codes change substantially between the year a house was built and the year it is repaired.
