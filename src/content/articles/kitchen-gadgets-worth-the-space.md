A kitchen gadget has three costs, and the price tag is the smallest of them.

The first is money. The second is space — a drawer has a fixed volume, and every object in it displaces something else. The third, and the one nobody thinks about, is **minutes of washing per use**. A gadget that takes four minutes to disassemble, wash, and dry has a lifetime cost of hours, and that is what determines whether it goes back in the drawer or into the donation box.

Sort any gadget by those three numbers and the answer is usually obvious.

## The Two Tests

**The 20-use test.** Will you use this twenty times in the next year? Not "someday" — in the next twelve months. Twenty uses is roughly once every two and a half weeks. Below that, a $40 gadget costs more than $2 per use, which is not insane, but it also means the object is occupying space for 350 days a year.

**The barrier test.** Does it remove a real obstacle, or a small annoyance? A rice cooker removes the obstacle of watching a pot if you eat rice four times a week. A garlic press removes the annoyance of crushing garlic with the flat of a knife — which takes eight seconds and no washing-up. One of those is a tool; the other is a solution to a problem that did not exist.

The knife and the thermometer are the two highest-leverage objects in any kitchen, and neither has a motor.

## Gadgets That Do Not

| Item | The problem |
|---|---|
| Garlic press | Knife is faster; the press takes 90 seconds to clean |
| Avocado slicer | One fruit, one shape, one job |
| Electric can opener | Manual is 3 seconds and never jams |
| Egg cooker | A pot does the same thing and holds more |
| Banana slicer | A knife exists |
| Strawberry huller | A paring knife does it in two seconds |
| Sandwich sealer | Produces a sealed sandwich nobody asked for |
| Spiralizer | Used twice, then the novelty ends |
| Juicer | 15 minutes of cleaning for one glass |
| Bread machine | The loaf is worse than the bakery's and the paddle is fiddly |
| Panini press | A skillet and a weight do the same thing |
| Single-serve pod machine | Per-cup cost is the real problem, not the machine |

None of these are frauds. They work. They simply fail the 20-use test badly enough that the space they take is worth more than the convenience they add — and in a kitchen, counter space is the scarcest resource you own.

## The Ambiguous Ones, Decided

Some gadgets are genuinely worth it for some households and not others. Four that come up most often:

**Stand mixer.** Worth it if you bake bread or cakes weekly. A $300 mixer used twice a month is $12.50 per use, plus the counter footprint of a small appliance that cannot be put away easily. If you bake monthly, a hand mixer at $40 does the same work and lives in a drawer.

**[Air fryer](/kitchen-tips/equipment/what-an-air-fryer-actually-does/).** Worth it if you have no convection oven, cook for one or two, and reheat fried food often. If you have a convection oven, the overlap is substantial — it is a speed and small-batch convenience, not a new capability.

**[Instant Pot](/kitchen-tips/equipment/slow-cooker-vs-instant-pot/).** Worth it if the schedule fits — dinner decided at 6 p.m., eaten at 7. Not worth it if your cooking happens in the morning and finishes unattended, where a slow cooker does the job for a fifth of the price.

**Blender.** A high-powered blender earns its space if you make smoothies or soups regularly. An immersion blender covers soup, sauce, and small batches for a tenth of the cost and washes in a minute. Buy the big one only if you have a specific job the small one cannot do.

## The Cleanup Math

This is the number that predicts abandonment better than anything else.

| Cleanup time | Uses per year | Hours per year spent cleaning |
|---|---|---|
| 30 seconds | 200 | 1.7 |
| 2 minutes | 100 | 3.3 |
| 5 minutes | 50 | 4.2 |
| 15 minutes | 20 | 5.0 |

Five hours a year is not catastrophic in isolation, but it is the reason a juicer ends up in a cupboard. The object is not rejected in a single decision; it is rejected twenty times, one washing-up at a time, until reaching for it stops occurring to you.

Anything with more than three parts, a sieve, or a blade guard that must be disassembled sits in the danger zone.

## What Counter Space Is Actually Worth

A typical apartment kitchen has eight to twelve square feet of usable counter. A small appliance occupies about one square foot — a tenth of the total — and it occupies it permanently, not only while in use.

That is the number to hold in mind. One square foot of counter is the difference between having a clear chopping area and not, which is why [small kitchens](/organize/kitchen/organize-small-kitchen/) are organized around the counter rather than the cupboards. An appliance that earns it has to be used often enough that moving it in and out of a cupboard every time would be a real friction — which is roughly twice a week.

Below that frequency, the honest options are two: put it away (and accept that you will rarely take it out), or do not buy it. The appliance that lives on the counter and is used monthly is the worst outcome of all — it takes the space and provides none of the convenience.

There is a rule that captures this: **anything that needs to be plugged in either lives on the counter or lives in a cupboard, and the cupboard is where things go to die.** Buy accordingly, and prefer appliances light enough to move if you are unsure.

## How to Judge Build Quality in the Shop

Three minutes with the box tells you most of what a review will.

**Weight.** Heavier is usually better for anything with a motor or a blade — it means metal where it matters. A food processor that slides across the counter when running is too light.

**Seams and joints.** A one-piece handle on a pot or a knife with a full tang is stronger than anything screwed together. Run a finger along the joints; a visible gap collects food.

**Parts you can buy.** Check whether the manufacturer sells replacement blades, jugs, gaskets, and lids. A machine with no spare parts is a disposable object with a five-year price; one with spares can last fifteen.

**Dishwasher claims.** "Top rack safe" and "dishwasher safe" are different promises. If a part must be hand-washed, that is a real cost per use — and it is the reason most juicers and many blenders sit unused.

**Cord length.** A one-metre cord means the appliance lives where the socket is, whether or not that is where you want it.

**Warranty terms.** Two years on the motor is normal; one year on the whole machine is a signal about expected life.

None of this requires expertise. It requires picking the object up and turning it over, which almost nobody does.

## The Drawer Audit

Twelve months is the only honest test. Empty one drawer onto the counter and put back only what you have used since this time last year. Everything else goes into a box in the garage for six months; if you have not gone looking for it by then, donate it.

Two rules keep the drawer from refilling:

**One in, one out.** A new gadget enters only when something leaves. This forces the comparison at the moment of purchase, which is the only moment you are honest about it.

**Nothing single-task.** If the object does exactly one thing and that thing takes under a minute by hand, it does not come in.

## The Gift Problem

Most useless gadgets arrive as gifts, which is why they are hard to discard — the objection is social, not practical. The useful response is to keep the box for a month, use it twice, and then donate it quietly. A gift that is never used has already failed at its purpose; storing it does not redeem it.

If you are the one buying: a better version of something the person already uses daily — a sharper knife, a heavier pan, a second sheet pan — will be used for a decade. A novel single-task gadget will be used twice.

## What to Buy Instead

If the goal is a better kitchen rather than more equipment, the four upgrades with the highest return are unglamorous:

1. **A knife that is sharp and comfortable**, plus [a sharpening method](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/sharpen-kitchen-knives/).
2. **A thermometer you trust**, which turns cooking into a measurable process.
3. **A scale**, which removes the largest error source in baking.
4. **Two heavy sheet pans**, which make one-pan dinners possible.

That is under $150 total and it changes more meals than any appliance.

## FAQ

**Is a stand mixer worth the counter space?**

Only if you bake at least twice a month. Below that, a hand mixer does the same job for a tenth of the price and stores in a drawer.

**What is the most overrated kitchen gadget?**

Anything single-task with more than two parts — garlic presses, avocado slicers, and strawberry hullers are the usual examples. They work, but a knife is faster and rinses in three seconds.

**How do I decide whether to keep something?**

The twelve-month rule: if you have not used it since this time last year, it goes. Put it in a box for six months before donating, to test whether you actually miss it.

**Do I need both a blender and an immersion blender?**

No. An immersion blender covers soup, sauce, and smoothies in small batches and washes in a minute. A full blender is worth adding only if you make large batches or crush ice regularly.

**How many kitchen tools is too many?**

There is no number, but there is a symptom: a drawer you cannot close, or a counter where you have to move things to cook. Both mean the tools have exceeded the space allocated to them.

**Are expensive knives worth it?**

A $40 knife that is sharp outperforms a $150 knife that is dull. Spend on the sharpening method and a comfortable handle; the steel matters less than the edge.

---

## Bottom Line

Judge gadgets on three numbers: purchase price, space occupied, and minutes of washing per use. Cheap tools used daily — a knife, a thermometer, a scale, sheet pans — earn their place many times over. Motorised single-task devices almost never do, unless they remove a barrier you hit several times a week. Audit the drawer once a year with the twelve-month rule, and buy upgrades rather than novelties.
