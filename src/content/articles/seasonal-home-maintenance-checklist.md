Home maintenance has an unusual property: **the cheapest tasks prevent the most expensive repairs.** A clogged gutter that costs twenty minutes to clear can cause thousands in water damage. A failed caulk line that costs a few dollars to replace can rot the framing behind it.

The problem is that nothing fails immediately, so nothing feels urgent. A seasonal schedule solves that by removing the decision entirely — the task is done because it is that season, not because a problem became visible.

This is the full four-season checklist, ordered by what each task protects.

---

## Why seasonal and not monthly

Home systems are driven by weather and by temperature change. Most failures happen at the transitions — the first freeze, the first thaw, the first heavy rain, the first heat wave. Aligning maintenance with seasons means the preparation happens before the failure, which is the entire point.

**The four transition points:** the first freeze of autumn, the wet season, the first warm weather of spring, and the peak heat of summer.

---

## Autumn (before the first freeze) — the most important list

Autumn is the highest-value checklist, because water and freezing temperatures cause the most expensive damage.

### Drainage and water

| Task | What it protects |
|---|---|
| **Clean the gutters** | Foundation, fascia, siding, and basement flooding |
| **Check downspouts direct water away** | Foundation, grading, crawlspace moisture |
| **Clear the downspout extensions** | Same as above; extensions must carry water 4–6 feet from the foundation |
| **Check the grading slopes away from the house** | Foundation and basement |
| **Inspect the roof** — broken shingles, flashing, seals | Interior water damage, ceilings, attic |
| **Check the attic for previous water stains** | Confirms whether roof work is needed before winter |
| **Test the sump pump** by pouring water into the pit | Basement flooding |

**The gutter task heads this list for a reason.** Clogged gutters cause water to back up under the roof edge, saturate the fascia, and run down the foundation. Roof and foundation repairs are among the most expensive in a home, and gutters are among the cheapest to maintain.

### Freeze preparation

| Task | What it protects |
|---|---|
| **Turn off and drain exterior hose bibs** | Burst pipes inside the wall |
| **Disconnect garden hoses** | Trapped water freezing in the bib and pipe |
| **Drain the irrigation system** | Burst irrigation lines and valves |
| **Insulate exposed pipes** in the crawlspace, garage, or attic | Burst pipes |
| **Check the furnace** and replace the filter | Heating cost and system life |
| **Have the heating system serviced** | Winter breakdown at the worst time |
| **Test smoke and CO detectors**, replace batteries | Life safety — CO risk rises with heating |

**The frozen pipe task is the one people skip.** A hose left connected to a bib traps water in the pipe. When it freezes, it expands and splits the pipe inside the wall. The resulting leak is discovered when the wall thaws, and the repair involves opening the wall.

### Exterior

| Task | What it protects |
|---|---|
| **Seal cracks in the driveway and walkway** | Freeze-thaw damage that splits concrete |
| **Check exterior caulk around windows and doors** | Water intrusion, drafts, heating cost |
| **Touch up exterior paint** | Siding rot |
| **Store or cover outdoor furniture** | Equipment life |
| **Trim tree branches away from the roof** | Roof damage from ice and wind |

## Winter (during the season)

Winter tasks are mostly about monitoring rather than action.

| Task | Why |
|---|---|
| **Check for ice dams after snow** | Prevents water backing under the roof and into the ceiling |
| **Keep an eye on pipes during deep freezes** | Run a trickle if a pipe is at risk |
| **Keep the furnace filter clean** | Peak heating season means the filter loads fastest |
| **Clear snow from vents** — furnace, dryer, and intake vents | Blocked furnace vents can cause CO buildup |
| **Check for drafts** and address weatherstripping | Heating cost |
| **Test the sump pump if a thaw occurs** | Snowmelt is the biggest flooding risk |

**The blocked furnace vent deserves emphasis.** A high-efficiency furnace vents outside, and a snowdrift over the vent can cause the system to shut down — or worse, cause carbon monoxide to enter the home. Clear vents after every significant snowfall.

**Ice dam prevention:** ice dams form when heat escapes into the attic, melts snow on the roof, and the water refreezes at the colder eave. The prevention is attic insulation and ventilation, not roof work. If ice dams recur, the fix is above, in the attic.

---

## Spring (before warm weather)

Spring is about discovering and repairing winter damage.

### Inspect for winter damage

| Task | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Check the roof** for lifted or missing shingles | Wind and ice damage from winter |
| **Check the attic** for water stains or dampness | Whether the roof leaked |
| **Inspect exterior caulk and paint** | Winter expansion opened gaps |
| **Check the foundation for cracks** | Freeze-thaw movement |
| **Look for frost heave** in walkways and steps | Tripping hazard and further damage |
| **Inspect the deck** for rotted boards and loose railings | Safety, especially railings |

### Prepare for warm weather

| Task | What it protects |
|---|---|
| **Service the air conditioner** or at least clean the condenser coils | Cooling cost and system life |
| **Replace the air filter** | Airflow and efficiency |
| **Turn hose bibs back on and check for leaks** | Finding damage from a freeze |
| **Clean the window screens** and check for tears | Insects, function |
| **Wash the exterior of windows** | Visibility; also a chance to inspect caulk |
| **Check the irrigation system** for leaks and broken heads | Water waste and landscape damage |
| **Clear dryer vents** | Dryer life and fire safety |

**The air conditioner service task pays for itself.** A unit with dirty condenser coils runs longer and uses more electricity, and an unserviced system is likelier to fail at the peak of summer when repair waits are longest and prices are highest.

---

## Summer (during the season)

| Task | Why |
|---|---|
| **Check the attic ventilation and insulation** | Prepares for next winter's ice dams and heat |
| **Inspect the deck** for loose fasteners, splinters, and sealing needs | Safety, and staining protects the wood |
| **Check for insect and pest entry points** | Sealing small gaps now prevents an infestation |
| **Look for termite evidence** — mud tubes, hollow wood, wings | Early detection is far cheaper than repair |
| **Check the exterior for peeling paint** | Prepares for fall painting |
| **Test the sump pump ahead of heavy rain** | Flooding prevention |
| **Clean and inspect the chimney if you use it** | Fire safety before the heating season |
| **Check the water heater for leaks or corrosion** | Prevents a flood and a cold failure |

---

## The monthly tasks (year round)

A handful of tasks are better done monthly regardless of season.

| Task | Frequency | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Replace or clean the furnace/AC filter** | 1–3 months | Airflow, efficiency, system life; monthly with pets or heavy dust |
| **Test smoke and CO detectors** | Monthly | Confirms they work when needed |
| **Check for plumbing leaks** under sinks and around the water heater | Monthly | Small drips become rot and mold |
| **Clean the garbage disposal** | Monthly | Odor and function |
| **Check the range hood filter** | Monthly | Grease buildup, fire risk |
| **Run water in unused drains** (guest baths, utility sinks) | Monthly | Prevents trap seals drying out and sewer gas |
| **Vacuum refrigerator condenser coils** (or every six months) | 6–12 months | Cooling efficiency |

**The unused-drain task is genuinely important.** A floor drain or a guest bathroom sink that is never used will have its trap seal evaporate, allowing sewer gas into the home. Running water monthly refills the seal.

---

## The annual tasks

These are best tied to a season so they are not forgotten.

| Task | Best season | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Professional furnace service** | Autumn | Prevents winter failure |
| **Professional AC service** | Spring | Prevents summer failure |
| **Chimney sweep and inspection** | Autumn, before use | Fire and CO safety |
| **Water heater flush** | Any | Removes sediment, extending life and improving efficiency |
| **Roof inspection** | Autumn and Spring | Two checkpoints for the year |
| **Inspect the attic** | Spring and Autumn | Finds leaks and insulation problems |
| **Test and service the sump pump** | Spring and Autumn | Flood prevention at both wet seasons |
| **Clean dryer vent ductwork** | Spring | Fire safety |
| **Check and clean gutters** | Autumn (and Spring if you have trees) | Water damage prevention |
| **Inspect the water heater anode rod** | Every few years | Prevents tank corrosion and failure |
| **Seal or stain the deck** | Every 2–3 years | Wood preservation |
| **Have the septic tank pumped** | Every 3–5 years, per use | Prevents system failure |

## The tasks ranked by return

If you only do a few things, do these. They prevent the most expensive failures per unit of effort.

| Rank | Task | Task cost | Failure cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clean gutters and check drainage** | Minutes | Foundation and roof damage |
| 2 | **Drain exterior hose bibs before freeze** | Minutes | Burst pipe inside a wall |
| 3 | **Replace HVAC filters** | Minutes | System efficiency loss and premature failure |
| 4 | **Test smoke and CO detectors** | Minutes | Life safety |
| 5 | **Clean the dryer vent** | An hour | Fire |
| 6 | **Check for plumbing leaks** | Minutes | Rot, mold, water damage |
| 7 | **Service the furnace and AC** | Moderate | Mid-season failure at peak cost |
| 8 | **Inspect and maintain exterior caulk** | Moderate | Water intrusion and rot |
| 9 | **Keep attic insulation and ventilation right** | Moderate | Ice dams and heat loss |
| 10 | **Test the sump pump** | Minutes | Basement flooding |

**Note the pattern:** the highest-return tasks are the cheapest ones. This is why maintenance is a better investment than almost any repair.

## Build the habit

A checklist only works if it gets executed. Three mechanisms make it happen:

1. **Fix the dates.** Tie each season's list to a calendar marker — the first freeze forecast, the first warm week, the start of the school year. Vague intentions fail.
2. **Batch the tasks.** Group by location: walk the exterior once, walk the interior once. Walking the same circuit doing five tasks takes a fraction of the time of five separate trips.
3. **Keep a maintenance log.** A simple notebook recording what was done and when prevents both duplication and neglect. It also helps a buyer or an inspector later.

**The one-hour seasonal walk** is the core habit. Once per season, walk the entire exterior and interior with the checklist and a notebook. Set a timer if needed. Most years it takes under an hour, and it catches nearly everything.

---

## Why This Works

Home systems fail through **cumulative small damage** rather than sudden catastrophic events. Water works its way in through a failed caulk line over months and rots framing slowly. A partially clogged gutter sends water under the roof edge, and the fascia softens incrementally. A dirty filter drives up pressure drop, and the blower motor wears faster.

The reason seasonal maintenance works is that it aligns the intervention with the failure mechanism. Water intrusion is prevented before the wet season. Freeze damage is prevented before the first freeze. Cooling failures are prevented before the peak heat, when repair availability is worst and prices highest.

The ranking by return follows from the asymmetry between task cost and failure cost. Cleaning a gutter costs minutes of effort. Replacing a section of rotted fascia or repairing a foundation crack costs orders of magnitude more. The same asymmetry appears in life safety: testing a CO detector costs seconds and the failure can be fatal.

There is also a detection component. Most of the expensive failures are visible early if someone is looking — a water stain in the attic, a cracked caulk line, a damp patch under a sink. A scheduled walk forces the looking, which converts a hidden slow leak into a small repair.

## What NOT to Do

| Common mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Waiting for a visible problem | The expensive failures are already underway by then | Work the seasonal checklist |
| Skipping the autumn freeze prep | A burst pipe inside a wall is a major repair | Drain bibs and hoses every autumn |
| Assuming gutters are fine because they look fine | Clogs are often at the downspout, out of sight | Clean them, and check the downspouts |
| Leaving a hose connected in winter | Traps water that splits the pipe in the wall | Disconnect and drain hoses |
| Changing the HVAC filter rarely | Reduces efficiency and shortens system life | Every one to three months |
| Ignoring ice dams as a roof problem | The cause is attic heat loss | Fix attic insulation and ventilation |
| Neglecting the sump pump until it rains | It fails when you need it most | Test it in autumn and spring |
| Barely looking at caulk and paint | Water enters through the smallest gaps | Inspect and refresh annually |
| Never running unused drains | Trap seals evaporate and sewer gas enters | Run water monthly in unused fixtures |
| Covering furnace vents with snow | Can cause CO buildup in the home | Clear vents after each snowfall |
| No maintenance log | Tasks get duplicated or forgotten | Keep a simple written record |
| Doing everything in one marathon | It is skipped the following year | One hour per season |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the single most important home maintenance task?**
Cleaning the gutters and confirming water drains away from the foundation. It is cheap, quick, and prevents foundation, fascia, and basement damage that costs many times more to repair.

**How often should I change my HVAC filter?**
Every one to three months for most homes. Monthly if you have pets, if someone has allergies, if you run the system constantly, or if the home is dusty. A loaded filter increases pressure drop, which raises energy use and wears the blower motor.

**Do I need to drain exterior hose bibs if it does not freeze much where I live?**
If you get any freeze at all, yes. It takes minutes and prevents a burst pipe inside the wall, which is expensive and awkward to repair. If you truly never freeze, it is still worth doing if a cold snap is forecast.

**Why does my house get ice dams?**
Ice dams come from warm air escaping into the attic, melting snow on the upper roof, which then refreezes at the colder eave. The fix is attic insulation and ventilation, not roof work. Removing the snow helps temporarily but does not address the cause.

**How often should I service the furnace and air conditioner?**
Once a year for each — furnace in autumn before heating season, air conditioner in spring before cooling season. This timing matters because it catches problems before the season when a failure is costliest.

**Should I hire a professional or do maintenance myself?**
Most of the checklist is homeowner work: filters, gutters, caulk, detectors, running water in unused drains, snow clearing, and visual inspections. Gas appliances, electrical panels, chimney work, and anything involving a steep roof are better handled professionally.

**What should go in a home maintenance log?**
The date, the task, who did it, and any observation worth remembering — a stain that appeared, a crack that widened, a part that seems near the end of its life. The log is most valuable years later, when a pattern becomes visible.

**How do I remember to do all this?**
Tie each season's list to a fixed calendar marker — the first freeze forecast, the first warm week — and batch tasks by location so an exterior walk covers several items at once. One hour per season is enough.

**What are the warning signs I should not ignore?**
Water stains on ceilings or in the attic, a musty smell in a basement or crawlspace, a spike in the water or energy bill, a soft or spongy spot in flooring, and any new crack that is widening. Each typically indicates an ongoing problem rather than a one-time event.
