**The short version:** in a fixture that's on three hours or more a day, an LED typically pays for itself in **12 to 24 months** and then keeps saving for a decade or more. In a closet that's on for five minutes a day, the payback stretches past ten years — and replacing a perfectly good bulb early actually costs you money.

The second case is the one nobody mentions. Here is how to tell which fixture you're looking at.

---

## The Core Calculation

Take the most common comparison in a US home: a **60-watt incandescent** versus the **9-watt LED** that replaces it.

```
Incandescent:  60 W
LED:            9 W
Difference:    51 W saved per hour of use
```

Now convert to energy over a year, at three hours a day:

```
51 W × 3 h/day × 365 days = 55,845 Wh/year ≈ 55.8 kWh/year
```

At $0.16/kWh (roughly the US residential average; rates vary from about half to nearly double this):

```
55.8 × $0.16 = about $8.95 saved per year, per bulb
```

With an LED costing roughly $2–4:

**Payback: under six months.**

That's the best-case number and it's genuinely typical for a well-used fixture. But the result is extremely sensitive to daily hours — and that sensitivity is the whole point.

| Daily use | Annual saving per bulb | Payback on a $3 LED |
|---|---|---|
| 6 hours | ~$17.85 | ~2 months |
| 3 hours | ~$8.95 | ~4 months |
| 1 hour | ~$2.98 | ~12 months |
| 0.25 hours (15 min) | ~$0.75 | ~4 years |
| 0.1 hours (6 min) | ~$0.30 | ~10 years |

**Read the bottom two rows again.** A closet light, a pantry light, or a guest-room lamp that's barely used will not pay back an LED purchase in any reasonable timeframe. If the existing incandescent still works, leaving it there is the correct financial decision.

## The Ten-Year Table (Where LED Really Wins)

The energy saving is only part of it. The replacement cost difference is what makes LED decisive in high-use fixtures.

Over ten years at three hours a day (about 10,950 hours of use):

| | Incandescent (60W) | LED (9W) |
|---|---|---|
| Rated life | ~1,000 hours | ~15,000–25,000 hours |
| Bulbs needed in 10 yrs | ~11 | ~1 |
| Purchase cost | ~$11 (at $1/bulb) | ~$3 |
| Energy used | ~657 kWh | ~99 kWh |
| Energy cost | ~$105 | ~$16 |
| **Ten-year total** | **~$116** | **~$19** |
| Ladder trips to replace it | 10 | 0 |

**Difference: roughly $97 per bulb over ten years.** Multiply by the twenty to thirty fixtures in a typical home and the number becomes meaningful — but again, only for the fixtures that are actually used.

The replacement interval is underrated. A bulb in a stairwell fixture or a vaulted ceiling is genuinely annoying to change. LED effectively removes that task from your life.

## The Variables That Change the Answer

Daily hours is the dominant variable, but these matter too:

**Your electricity rate.** At $0.30/kWh the payback halves; at $0.10/kWh it stretches by half. Pull the real rate off your bill including delivery charges.

**Enclosed fixtures.** This is the one that quietly kills LED bulbs. An LED in a fully enclosed glass globe or a recessed can without ventilation can overheat and fail years early. If you're putting LED into an enclosed fixture, buy bulbs **rated for enclosed fixtures** — it's printed on the package.

**Dimmer compatibility.** Older dimmers designed for incandescent loads often cause LED flickering, buzzing, drop-out at low settings, or failure to turn off completely. If you have dimmers, buy bulbs labeled **dimmable** and check the manufacturer's compatibility list, or budget for a modern LED-rated dimmer. This is the most common cause of "LEDs are bad" complaints.

**Climate and heating season.** In a heating-dominated climate, the waste heat from incandescent bulbs is not entirely wasted during winter — it slightly offsets heating. The effect is small but real, and it means the savings calculation is marginally less favorable in cold climates with electric heat than in warm ones.

**Usage patterns.** Lights in a hallway on a motion sensor, or a porch light on a timer, accumulate hours faster than people estimate.

## What to Actually Buy

Skip most of the marketing and check four things.

**Lumens, not watts.** Watts tell you what the bulb consumes; lumens tell you how much light it produces. Match lumens to the old bulb:

| Old incandescent | Look for |
|---|---|
| 40 W | ~450 lumens |
| 60 W | ~800 lumens |
| 75 W | ~1,100 lumens |
| 100 W | ~1,600 lumens |

**Color temperature, in Kelvin.** This is the difference between "warm and cozy" and "cold and clinical."

| Kelvin | Appearance | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| 2700 K | Warm, yellowish | Living rooms, bedrooms |
| 3000 K | Soft white | Kitchens, general use |
| 3500–4000 K | Neutral white | Bathrooms, work areas |
| 5000 K+ | Cool, bluish | Garages, task lighting |

**CRI (Color Rendering Index).** How accurately colors appear. For living spaces, **90+** looks noticeably better than the 80 that is common on cheap bulbs. It matters most where you're judging appearance — getting dressed, applying makeup, food prep.

**Rated life and the enclosure rating.** Look for the L70 figure (hours until output drops to 70%) and the enclosed-fixture rating if you need it.

## A Sensible Replacement Order

You do not need to replace everything at once. Work down this list:

1. **Every fixture on more than two hours a day** — highest return, do these first
2. **Fixtures that are difficult to reach** — the labor saving is the point
3. **Fixtures in enclosed globes** — use enclosed-rated LEDs, expect shorter life
4. **Dimmable circuits** — verify compatibility before buying in bulk
5. **Rarely used closets and pantries** — only when the current bulb burns out

That last item is deliberate. **Replacing a working incandescent in a barely used fixture is a net loss.** Run it until it dies, then put in an LED.

## The Honest Caveats

**Quality varies a lot.** Cheap no-name LED bulbs frequently fail well before their claimed life, shift color over time, or produce poor-quality light. Buying from established lighting brands costs more per bulb and is usually worth it.

**The "22-year lifespan" claim assumes ideal conditions.** Enclosed fixtures, high ambient temperatures, and frequent on-off cycling all shorten real-world life.

**Lumen depreciation is real.** An LED at the end of its rated life is producing roughly 70% of its original output — not zero. It dims gradually rather than burning out, which is a different failure mode than people expect.

**Very cheap LEDs can have poor power factor and flicker,** which some people find genuinely uncomfortable. If LED light gives you headaches, this is usually why — try a higher-quality bulb before concluding LEDs aren't for you.

The payback logic that decides a light bulb also decides a roof full of panels — [the solar payback calculation](/save-money/big-decisions/solar-panel-payback/) does the arithmetic without the twenty-five-year savings headline.

## Why This Works

**Incandescent bulbs are heaters that happen to emit light.** Roughly 90% of the energy going into one comes out as heat, not visible light. An LED produces the same lumens with a fraction of the input, and the difference is large enough that it shows up on a bill even though a single bulb is a trivial load.

**Rated life differs by more than an order of magnitude.** A 1,000-hour incandescent against a 15,000–25,000-hour LED means roughly ten to twenty fewer purchases per fixture over a decade. This is the term that dominates the ten-year total, not the electricity.

**Payback is inversely proportional to usage — so targeting matters.** Because the annual saving scales directly with hours of operation, replacing the most-used fixtures first produces most of the available benefit for a fraction of the cost. The last few fixtures in the house contribute almost nothing.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Replacing rarely used bulbs early | Payback stretches past a decade | Wait until the old bulb fails |
| Putting standard LED in enclosed fixtures | Heat shortens life dramatically | Buy enclosed-fixture-rated bulbs |
| Using old dimmers with LED | Flicker, buzz, drop-out | Match bulbs to dimmer, or upgrade |
| Shopping by watts | You can't tell how bright it is | Match lumens instead |
| Buying all 5000 K | Living spaces feel harsh and cold | 2700–3000 K for living areas |
| Buying the cheapest multipack | Early failures, poor light quality | Established brands, check CRI |
| Throwing working bulbs away | Pure waste | Use them up first |
| Ignoring lumens-per-watt | Some LEDs are much less efficient than others | Higher lm/W means lower running cost |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does one LED bulb actually save per year?**
At three hours a day and average US rates, roughly $6–12 per bulb replacing a 60 W incandescent. It scales with your rate and your daily hours.

**What is the payback period?**
Months for high-use fixtures, several years for low-use ones. The daily hours is the variable that matters most.

**Do LED bulbs really last 20 years?**
The rating is in hours — typically 15,000–25,000 — and assumes reasonable operating conditions. In an enclosed, hot fixture, expect substantially less.

**Why do my LEDs flicker on the dimmer?**
The dimmer was designed for incandescent loads. Use bulbs labeled dimmable and check compatibility, or replace the dimmer with an LED-rated model.

**Are LEDs worth it in a closet that's rarely used?**
No. Let the existing bulb burn out first — the payback would be many years.

**Does the heat from incandescent bulbs help in winter?**
Marginally, if you heat with electricity. The effect is small and it does not change the overall conclusion.

**What lumen output replaces a 60 W bulb?**
About 800 lumens. Match lumens, not watts.

**Should I replace all bulbs at once?**
Not necessary. Prioritize high-use fixtures and replace the rest as they fail — that captures nearly all the financial benefit for much less upfront cost.
