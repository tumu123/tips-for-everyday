Water expands when it freezes, and the expansion is powerful enough to split copper, PVC, and even steel. The damage is not really the freeze — it is the **thaw**. A split pipe stays sealed while the ice is in place, then releases water as soon as it melts.

**Why this is worth the effort:** a burst pipe inside a wall or ceiling causes water damage across multiple rooms, and the repair involves opening the wall, drying the structure, and often replacing flooring and drywall. Prevention costs a few dollars in insulation.

**Where pipes freeze:** almost always at the exterior. The vulnerable locations are exterior walls, unheated basements and crawlspaces, attics, garages, and any pipe running along a rim joist or an exterior sill.

---

## Step 1: Find your vulnerable pipes

Walk the home with a flashlight before the first freeze and identify every pipe at risk.

| Location | Risk level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Exterior wall cavities** | High | Insulation may not cover the pipe, and cold air reaches it |
| **Unheated basement or crawlspace** | High | Ambient temperature tracks outdoor temperature |
| **Attic** | High | Very cold and often uninsulated |
| **Garage** | High | Often unheated but contains plumbing |
| **Against a rim joist or sill** | High | The coldest framing in the house |
| **Under a kitchen or bathroom sink on an exterior wall** | High | Cabinet blocks heat, wall exposed to cold |
| **Running near a foundation vent** | High | Direct cold air path |
| **Behind baseboards on an exterior wall** | Moderate | Convection along the wall |
| **Interior walls** | Low | Usually protected |
| **Pipes with a history of freezing** | Highest | It happened once, it will happen again |

**A simple exposure test:** on a cold day, touch or aim an infrared thermometer at the pipe. If the pipe is noticeably colder than the room air, it is at risk. A pipe anywhere in the low 40s or below during a cold snap should be insulated.

**The history rule:** if a pipe has frozen before, it will freeze again. Mark those runs and treat them as the priority. Prevention effort should go where the failure has already happened.

---

## Step 2: Insulate everything you can reach

Insulation slows heat loss from the pipe and adds a barrier. It is inexpensive and it is the primary defense.

### Pipe insulation

1. **Buy foam pipe insulation** sized to your pipe diameter. Measure the outside diameter first.
2. **Slide it over the pipe** and seal the seams.
3. **Butt sections tightly** — a gap in the middle defeats the purpose.
4. **Tape the seams** with the manufacturer's tape or duct tape.
5. **Insulate both hot and cold pipes.** Cold pipes matter more for freezing, but hot pipes losing heat is waste.

**The critical caveat: insulation does not add heat.** It only slows heat loss. A pipe that is already in an environment below freezing will still freeze, just more slowly. Insulation must be paired with a heat source or with keeping the ambient temperature above freezing.

### Air sealing

This is where the biggest gains usually are, and it is often overlooked.

1. **Seal gaps where pipes enter exterior walls** with caulk or spray foam.
2. **Seal and insulate rim joists** — the band around the top of the foundation where the floor framing meets the sill. This is typically the coldest, draftiest part of a basement, and it is where many pipes run.
3. **Seal foundation vents** with covers in winter, if your area does not require them open.
4. **Seal crawlspace access doors** and any other opening to outside air.

**Why air sealing matters more than insulation in many homes:** cold outdoor air entering through a gap can be far more damaging than ambient cold. A single unsealed pipe penetration on a windy night can freeze a pipe that is otherwise well insulated.

## Step 3: Add heat where insulation alone is not enough

For pipes that run through genuinely cold spaces, insulation is not sufficient. Add a heat source.

| Method | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pipe heating cable (heat tape)** | Long runs in cold basements, crawlspaces, attics | Self-regulating types are safest; follow the manufacturer's installation exactly |
| **Keep the cabinet doors open** | Under-sink pipes on exterior walls | Lets room heat circulate around the pipes |
| **Space heater in a basement or garage** | Small unheated spaces | Only with safety features; never unattended near combustibles |
| **Leave the heat on** | Whole home | Never below 55°F (13°C), even when away |
| **Allow a trickle** | Extreme cold spells on vulnerable runs | A slow drip keeps water moving and reduces freeze risk |

### Heat cable safety

- **Use only products designed for pipes**, and follow the instructions about what to wrap and what not to.
- **Never wrap a cable over itself** unless the product permits it.
- **Do not install heat cable on plastic pipes** without confirming compatibility — some plastics are damaged by the cable's heat.
- **Plug into a GFCI outlet** for any cable in a wet location.
- **Self-regulating heat cable** adjusts output to temperature and is much safer than constant-wattage types.

### The cabinet-door trick

For a kitchen or bathroom sink on an exterior wall, simply **leaving the cabinet doors open** during cold snaps lets warm room air reach the pipes. This costs nothing and is remarkably effective.

## Step 4: Cold-weather habits

A few habits during a cold snap prevent most problems.

| Habit | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| **Keep the thermostat at 55°F (13°C) minimum** | Prevents whole-house freezing while you are away |
| **Leave cabinet doors open** on exterior-wall plumbing | Lets warm air reach the pipes |
| **Let a vulnerable faucet drip** | Moving water resists freezing; relieves pressure |
| **Disconnect garden hoses** | Prevents a burst bib and the pipe behind it |
| **Drain and shut off exterior hose bibs** | Removes water from exterior runs |
| **Drain the irrigation system** | Prevents burst lines and valves |
| **Keep the garage door closed** | Protects plumbing in or near the garage |
| **Tell someone if you are away** | Allows a leak to be caught early |

**The trickle deserves a note.** A slow drip does two things: it keeps water moving, which makes freezing slower, and it relieves pressure if ice does form, so the pipe is less likely to split. It is not a substitute for insulation, but it is a useful last resort.

**The away-from-home rule is absolute:** never shut the heat off completely when leaving in winter. Set it to 55°F (13°C) at minimum. A frozen and burst pipe discovered weeks later is a catastrophic repair.

## Step 5: What to do if a pipe freezes

**If water stops flowing when you turn on a tap during cold weather, assume a frozen pipe.**

### Immediate steps

1. **Leave the faucet open.** As the ice melts, water and released pressure need somewhere to go. This is critical — trapped pressure is what bursts pipes.
2. **Locate the frozen section.** Feel along the pipe for a very cold or frosted section. Trace back from the faucet toward the supply.
3. **Open cabinet doors** to let warm room air in.
4. **Raise the room temperature** by turning up the thermostat.

### Thawing safely

| Method | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Hair dryer** | Best. Apply moving warm air along the pipe, starting at the faucet end |
| **Heat gun on low setting** | Acceptable, but keep it moving and never aim at one spot |
| **Electric heating pad or hot towels** | Safe and effective |
| **Space heater aimed at the area** | Effective in a basement, with safety precautions |
| **Warm wet towels wrapped around the pipe** | Safe, slower |
| **Open flame (torch, candle)** | **Never.** Fire risk and can damage the pipe |
| **Boiling water poured on a pipe** | Not recommended — thermal shock can crack the pipe |

**Work from the faucet end backward** toward the frozen section, so melting water can drain out the open faucet rather than being trapped behind the ice.

**Warning signs you should stop and call a professional:**
- The pipe is visibly split or bulging
- Water starts spraying or leaking as the ice melts
- The frozen section is inside a wall or ceiling with no access
- You cannot locate the frozen section
- The pipe is in a crawlspace or attic that is unsafe to access

### If a pipe bursts

1. **Shut off the main water valve immediately.**
2. **Open all faucets** to drain the remaining water and relieve pressure.
3. **Shut off the electricity** to affected areas if water is near outlets or the panel.
4. **Call a plumber**, and contact your insurance if damage is significant.
5. **Document the damage** with photos before cleanup, for the insurance claim.

## Step 6: Prevent the next freeze

| Action | When |
|---|---|
| **Inspect and re-insulate** before the first freeze | Autumn |
| **Check heat cable** for damage | Autumn |
| **Test the thermostat's low setting** | Autumn |
| **Mark pipes with a history of freezing** | Ongoing |
| **Re-seal any new penetrations** after renovations | After any work |
| **Confirm the basement/crawlspace stays above freezing** with a thermometer | During the first cold snap |

**Keep a thermometer in the basement or crawlspace** during the first cold snap of the year. If the space is dropping toward freezing, you have time to act before a pipe fails. This is a five-dollar investment that gives early warning.

## Why This Works

Water expands by roughly **9 percent** when it freezes, and the force is sufficient to split metal and plastic pipe. The split typically occurs along the length of the pipe rather than at a fitting, and the pipe often stays sealed until the ice thaws, which is why the damage appears suddenly.

Three mechanisms prevent it, and they map onto the steps above:

**Insulation slows heat loss.** It does not generate heat, so it works only when there is some heat to retain — water in the pipe, or ambient warmth in the space. In a genuinely below-freezing space, insulation alone delays the freeze but does not prevent it. This is why insulation must be paired with either heat or air sealing.

**Air sealing stops the cold from arriving.** Cold air leaking through an unsealed penetration can chill a pipe far below the ambient room temperature. Sealing is often more effective than adding insulation, because it removes the localized cold source.

**Heat sources raise the pipe above freezing.** Heat cable adds heat directly. Keeping cabinet doors open lets room heat circulate. A trickle keeps water moving and relieves pressure. These are complementary to insulation, not alternatives.

**The faucet-open instruction during thawing** is about pressure rather than temperature. If ice melts while both ends of the pipe remain sealed, pressure builds between the ice and the closed faucet. Opening the faucet gives the water and pressure an escape route, which is why the instruction comes before any attempt to thaw.

## What NOT to Do

| Common mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Insulating a pipe in a freezing space with no heat | Insulation only slows heat loss, it adds none | Pair insulation with air sealing or heat |
| Leaving a garden hose attached in winter | Traps water that splits the bib and interior pipe | Disconnect and drain hoses |
| Shutting off the heat entirely when away | The whole house can freeze | Set to 55°F minimum |
| Using an open flame to thaw a pipe | Fire risk, and can damage or crack the pipe | Hair dryer or heat gun on low |
| Pouring boiling water on a frozen pipe | Thermal shock can crack it | Warm towels or moving warm air |
| Thawing with the faucet closed | Trapped pressure can burst the pipe | Open the faucet first, and leave it open |
| Ignoring a pipe that froze once before | The conditions that caused it remain | Mark and fix that run |
| Sealing foundation vents in code-required areas | Can create moisture or combustion air problems | Follow local requirements |
| Wrapping heat cable over itself | Overheating and fire risk | Follow the manufacturer's layout |
| Not checking the crawlspace temperature | You have no warning before failure | Keep a thermometer there |
| Assuming plastic pipe will not burst | Plastic splits as well, sometimes more easily | Insulate and heat plastic too |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**At what temperature do pipes freeze?**
Water freezes at 32°F (0°C), but pipes typically freeze when the surrounding air stays below about 20°F (−7°C) for several hours, especially in an unheated space or an exterior wall. Wind and unsealed gaps make freezing happen faster.

**Can insulation alone prevent a pipe from freezing?**
No. Insulation slows heat loss but does not add heat. A pipe in a space below freezing will still freeze, just more slowly. Insulation must be combined with air sealing or a heat source.

**How do I know which pipe is frozen?**
Turn on each tap in the house one at a time. The location with no water or reduced flow points to the affected line. Feel along the pipe from that fixture back toward the supply for an unusually cold or frosted section.

**Is it safe to use a hair dryer on a frozen pipe?**
Yes, and it is the safest common method. Keep the dryer moving along the pipe and start from the faucet end. Never use an open flame.

**Should I leave a faucet dripping in cold weather?**
It helps for vulnerable runs during a severe cold snap. Moving water freezes more slowly, and an open faucet relieves pressure if ice does form. It is a supplement to insulation, not a replacement.

**Why does a pipe burst after it thaws?**
Because the ice expanded and split the pipe while it was frozen, and the ice plugs the split. When it thaws, the plug melts and water escapes through the crack. The damage usually appears during the thaw, not the freeze.

**What do I do if a pipe bursts?**
Shut off the main water valve immediately, open all faucets to drain and relieve pressure, turn off electricity to affected areas if water is near outlets, and call a plumber. Photograph the damage before cleanup for insurance.

**Does leaving cabinet doors open really help?**
Yes, for under-sink plumbing on an exterior wall. The cabinet blocks room heat from reaching the pipes, so opening the doors lets warm air circulate. It costs nothing and is effective during cold snaps.

**How much does a burst pipe repair cost?**
The pipe repair itself is usually modest. The water damage is what becomes expensive — drying, drywall, flooring, and sometimes mold remediation. This is why prevention is worth far more than the pipe repair.
