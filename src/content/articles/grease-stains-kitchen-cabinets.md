Kitchen cabinet grease is not the same problem as a spilled oil stain. Cooking vapors carry aerosolized fat that settles on cabinet surfaces, then gradually **polymerizes** — the same chemical process that cures paint and seasoning on cast iron. Over months it becomes a sticky, amber film that ordinary surface cleaner smears around instead of removing.

**The method that works:** a grease-cutting dish soap solution to break the film, applied with enough dwell time to soften it, then a second pass to remove the residue the first pass leaves behind. For old, hardened film, add a baking soda paste as a mild abrasive.

The critical variable is **cabinet finish**. Painted, laminate, and wood-veneer cabinets tolerate different cleaners, and using the wrong one causes permanent damage that costs far more than the grease did. Identify your finish first.

---

## Step 0: Identify Your Cabinet Finish First

This is not optional. Run this test before touching any cleaner:

| Finish type | How to identify | Cleaner that is safe | Cleaner to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Painted (latex or oil) | Solid color, no visible grain, often smooth | Dish soap solution, baking soda paste | Solvents, ammonia, scrubbing pads |
| Laminate / thermofoil | Uniform color, plastic feel, seams at edges | Dish soap solution, vinegar | Abrasives, acetone, alcohol |
| Wood veneer with clear coat | Visible grain under a glossy or matte film | Dish soap solution, orange oil | Vinegar in quantity, abrasives |
| Solid wood, oiled or waxed | Visible grain, matte, absorbs water | Orange oil, mild soap | Dish soap in quantity (strips oil) |
| Melamine / MDF with coating | Very uniform, sharp edges | Dish soap solution | Solvents over seams |

**The water test:** Put a drop of water on an inconspicuous spot (inside a cabinet door edge). If it beads, the surface is sealed — most cleaners are safe. If it soaks in and darkens the surface, the finish is oiled or bare wood and should not get wet cleaning at all.

**Test the cleaner too:** Always apply any new cleaner to the inside face of a cabinet door or the bottom edge first. Wait 10 minutes and check for dulling, cloudiness, or color change.

## What You'll Need

- **Grease-cutting dish soap** — the original blue Dawn formula is the reference standard; any detergent labeled "grease cutting" works
- **Warm water** — hot enough to feel warm, not scalding
- **Microfiber cloths (4–5)** — you need clean cloths for each stage
- **Soft-bristle detail brush** — for cabinet door grooves and raised panel edges
- **Baking soda** — for old hardened film
- **White vinegar** — for the final residue rinse on non-wood finishes. **Do not use on oiled or waxed wood.**
- **Cotton swabs** — for hardware crevices

**Do not use:** abrasive powders (Comet, Ajax), steel wool, melamine sponges on painted or glossy finishes, or any solvent (acetone, paint thinner, alcohol) on painted or laminate surfaces.

---

## Step 1: Dry-Dust the Cabinets First

Grease film traps dust. If you spray cleaner directly onto a dusty cabinet, you create a sticky paste that pushes grime into the grain instead of lifting it off.

1. Take a dry microfiber cloth.
2. Wipe the entire cabinet face top to bottom, including the frame and door edges.
3. Shake the cloth out or swap it when it stops picking up dust.
4. Pay attention to the area directly above and beside the stovetop — that is where buildup is thickest.

This takes two minutes and prevents the most common mistake in cabinet cleaning.

---

## Step 2: Apply the Degreasing Solution

1. Mix **1 tablespoon of grease-cutting dish soap with 2 cups of warm water** in a bucket or spray bottle.
2. Dip a microfiber cloth, wring it so it is damp — **not dripping**. Excess water runs into cabinet seams and swells the material.
3. Work in sections of roughly one cabinet door at a time.
4. Wipe the door face, then the edges, then the frame around it.
5. For grooves and raised-panel edges, dip the detail brush in the solution and work along the groove.

**On dwell time:** For light monthly maintenance, wipe and move on. For grease that has been building for months, **let the solution sit for 3–5 minutes** before wiping. The surfactant needs time to penetrate the polymerized film. Wiping immediately is the reason most people conclude "dish soap doesn't work."

---

## Step 3: Remove the Lifted Residue

This step is what separates a clean cabinet from a streaky one, and most guides skip it.

The first pass lifts grease off the surface but leaves a soap-and-grease emulsion behind. If that residue dries, it becomes a new film — slightly duller and stickier than before.

1. Take a **clean** microfiber cloth (not the one you used to apply).
2. Wet it with plain warm water and wring it well.
3. Wipe the same section again, top to bottom.
4. Rinse the cloth frequently. **When the cloth looks gray, swap it.** Using a dirty rinse cloth just redistributes the film.

---

## Step 4: Repeat Until the Cloth Comes Away Clean

One pass rarely finishes the job on heavy buildup. This is normal.

1. Repeat Steps 2 and 3 on the same section.
2. Check the cloth after the rinse pass. **The job is done when the rinse cloth comes away with no visible transfer.**
3. Expect **two to three passes** on cabinets that have not been cleaned in a year or more, and one pass for monthly maintenance.

Note that the cabinet may look slightly duller when wet. Do not judge the result until it is fully dry.

---

## Step 5: Treat Hardened Film with a Baking Soda Paste

If grease has hardened near the stovetop into a tacky, amber layer that resists the soap solution:

1. Mix **3 parts baking soda with 1 part water** to form a thick paste. Add 1/2 teaspoon dish soap.
2. Apply the paste to the affected area with your fingers or a cloth.
3. **Let it sit for 10 minutes.** The paste holds the cleaner against the surface instead of letting it run off.
4. Work it in with a soft-bristle brush or a corner of a microfiber cloth using light circular motion.
5. Wipe away the paste with a damp cloth, then do a clean-water rinse pass.
6. **Do not use this paste on high-gloss painted cabinets** — baking soda is a mild abrasive and will dull the sheen.

---

## Step 6: Clean the Hardware and Grooves

Cabinet hardware collects the same grease, plus hand oils. It is usually the dirtiest part of the door.

**Metal handles and knobs:**

1. Remove them if they are screwed on. This is worth the five minutes — you cannot clean the surface behind them otherwise.
2. Soak them for 10 minutes in the dish soap solution.
3. Scrub crevices with a detail brush.
4. **Do not use vinegar or acidic cleaners on brass, unlacquered bronze, or antique finishes** — they strip the patina. Use mild soap only.
5. Dry thoroughly before reinstalling. Moisture trapped under hardware corrodes the finish.

**Door grooves and raised-panel edges:**

1. Dip a cotton swab or detail brush in the soap solution.
2. Run it along the groove to lift the accumulated film.
3. Follow with a clean damp swab.

---

## Step 7: Rinse, Dry, and Protect

1. Do a final pass with a clean cloth dampened with plain water.
2. **Dry immediately with a dry microfiber cloth.** Do not let cabinets air-dry — water spots on dark finishes are difficult to remove.
3. Leave cabinet doors **open for 30 minutes** to let any moisture in the seams evaporate.
4. Optional: apply a thin coat of cabinet wax or a specialty furniture polish on wood cabinets. This creates a barrier that makes future grease removal much easier. **Skip this on laminate and thermofoil.**

---

## Why This Works

The chemistry behind each step:

| Component | Function | Why it is necessary |
|---|---|---|
| Dish soap (surfactants) | Emulsifies fat — bonds to grease molecules on one end and water on the other | Grease is nonpolar and will not dissolve in water alone |
| Warm water | Lowers grease viscosity and improves surfactant action | Cold water makes the film harder to lift |
| Dwell time | Allows surfactant to penetrate the polymerized layer | Polymerized grease is not a liquid film — it must be softened |
| Baking soda | Mild abrasive (Mohs hardness ~2.5) | Provides mechanical lift for hardened film without scratching most finishes |
| Second clean-water pass | Removes the soap-grease emulsion | Residue left behind dries into a new, duller film |

The polymerization point is the key insight. Fresh grease wipes off in seconds. Grease that has been baking onto a cabinet for a year is a solid film that must be chemically softened and then mechanically lifted. That is why "just wipe it with a degreaser" fails on old buildup and works fine on last week's spatter.

---

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Spraying cleaner onto dusty cabinets | Creates sticky paste that pushes grime into the grain |
| Soaking the cabinets with a dripping cloth | Water enters seams and swells MDF, delaminates veneer, and warps doors |
| Using vinegar on oiled or waxed wood | Acid strips the oil finish, leaving dry, uneven patches |
| Using abrasive pads on painted cabinets | Scrubs through the paint layer in seconds |
| Skipping the clean-water rinse | Soap residue dries into a new film and attracts dust |
| Cleaning near the stovetop first and working outward | Spreads concentrated grease across clean surfaces |
| Not removing hardware | Leaves a ring of grime behind the handle that is visible from three feet away |
| Using ammonia on laminate | Causes hazing and, over repeated use, cracking at the seams |

---

## Preventing Grease Buildup

The goal is to never let grease reach the polymerized stage, because fresh grease takes seconds to remove and polymerized grease takes an hour.

1. **Wipe the cabinets above the stovetop weekly.** This single habit eliminates 80% of the problem. Thirty seconds with a damp cloth.
2. **Run the range hood every time you cook**, and **replace or clean the filter monthly**. A clogged filter recirculates grease onto the cabinets. See our guide on cleaning range hood filters.
3. **Wipe down once a month** with the dish soap solution to catch anything the weekly wipe missed.
4. **Use a splatter screen** when frying. It is the cheapest grease-control device in a kitchen.
5. **Wipe down the cabinet fronts after any high-heat cooking** (searing, deep frying, stir-frying) — these produce the most airborne fat.

A realistic maintenance schedule:

| Task | Frequency | Time required |
|---|---|---|
| Wipe cabinets above stovetop | Weekly | 30 seconds |
| Quick wipe of all cabinet fronts | Monthly | 5 minutes |
| Full degrease and rinse | Every 3–6 months | 40 minutes |
| Clean range hood filter | Monthly | 10 minutes (or dishwasher cycle) |

## FAQ

**What is the best degreaser for kitchen cabinets?**
For most finishes, a solution of 1 tablespoon grease-cutting dish soap in 2 cups of warm water is as effective as commercial degreasers and significantly safer for delicate finishes. Commercial degreasers containing solvents work faster on heavy buildup but can damage painted and laminate surfaces, so they should be reserved for greasy stainless steel and tested on an inconspicuous area first.

**Can I use vinegar to clean greasy cabinets?**
Vinegar is an acid, not a degreaser — it does not dissolve fat. It helps remove the soap residue in a final rinse and kills odor, but it will not remove grease on its own. Avoid it entirely on oiled or waxed wood cabinets, where it strips the finish.

**Why do my cabinets still feel sticky after cleaning?**
Sticky residue after cleaning almost always means soap film was left behind, not that the grease survived. Go back with a clean cloth and plain warm water, wipe the surface again, and swap the cloth as soon as it looks gray. If it is still sticky after a proper rinse pass, the film is polymerized and needs the baking soda paste step.

**How do I clean greasy cabinets without removing the finish?**
Match the cleaner to the finish (see the table in Step 0), keep the cloth damp rather than wet, avoid abrasives on painted and high-gloss surfaces, and always test on the inside of a door first. The most common cause of finish damage is not the cleaner itself but water sitting in seams and abrasive scrubbing.

**Can I use a steam cleaner on kitchen cabinets?**
Steam can work on painted and laminate cabinets, but the risk of water damage to seams, veneer, and MDF cores is significant. If you use steam, hold the nozzle several inches back, keep it moving, and wipe dry immediately. Do not use steam on oiled or waxed wood.

**How often should I clean kitchen cabinets?**
Wipe the cabinets directly above and beside the stovetop weekly. Do a quick wipe of all cabinet fronts monthly. Perform a full degrease with a rinse pass every three to six months, or more often if you cook with oil frequently.

**Do melamine sponges work on cabinet grease?**
Melamine sponges are micro-abrasives. They work on laminate and unpainted surfaces but will dull the sheen on painted and high-gloss cabinets, and the dulling is permanent. Use them only as a last resort and only on surfaces where a slightly duller finish is acceptable.

**Why is the grease worse near the stovetop?**
Cooking vapors rise and spread outward. The cabinets nearest the cooking surface receive the highest concentration of airborne fat particles, so the film builds there first and thickest. This is also why range hood maintenance matters — a working filter captures much of that fat before it reaches the cabinets.
