Ask people what they remember about staying at someone's house and almost nobody mentions the thread count. They remember standing in the dark looking for a light switch, not knowing where to put a wet towel, and being unsure whether it was acceptable to make coffee before the hosts were up.

Guests are not evaluating your decor. They are navigating an unfamiliar space with none of their own routines, and small frictions accumulate into an impression of either ease or vague discomfort. The fixes are specific and mostly cheap, and they have almost nothing to do with the room itself.

## Step 1: Walk the route your guest will walk at 2 a.m.

This is the single most useful exercise, and it takes five minutes. Turn off the lights and walk from the guest bed to the bathroom.

What you will notice immediately:

- **You cannot find the light.** A night light in the hallway and a lamp within reach of the bed solve it. Not a ceiling switch across the room in the dark.
- **You do not know which door is the bathroom.** Especially in a hallway with three doors.
- **The floor is cold**, or there is a rug edge to trip on.
- **The bathroom is dark** until you find the switch, which is usually inside on the wrong side of the door.

Fix those four things and you have solved the majority of guest discomfort before it occurs.

## Step 2: Give the bathroom a clear system for wet things

The most common unspoken guest anxiety is where to put a wet towel. Most hosts never think about it; most guests improvise on the back of a door or over a shower rod, which is exactly where the host's own towels are not.

- **Two hooks, at different heights**, on the back of the bathroom door or the wall beside it. One for the bath towel, one for a hand towel or robe.
- **A distinct color for guest towels.** It removes the ambiguity of which towel is whose, and it makes the laundry obvious after they leave.
- **A small bin with a liner**, visible. Guests will not ask where to put a tissue.
- **Spare toilet paper in plain sight**, not in a cabinet. Nobody wants to open your cabinets looking for paper.
- **A shelf or a clear counter area** for their toiletry bag. This is the detail most guest bathrooms miss  there is nowhere to put their things down.

## Step 3: Stock the five things guests forget

Guests forget the same items every time, and having them costs almost nothing:

- **Toothbrush and toothpaste.** The single most appreciated item.
- **Shampoo and conditioner**, in a size they can use without opening your family bottle.
- **A hairdryer**, even if you think they brought one.
- **Lotion**  hotel rooms include it for a reason, and dry winter air is universal.
- **A phone charger or a USB outlet by the bed.** This is now the most-missed item in any guest room.

Put them on a tray, not scattered in a drawer. A tray reads as intentional; a drawer reads as leftovers.

## Step 4: Make the temperature controllable, not perfect

You cannot guess what temperature a guest wants, so give them the ability to change it.

- **68 to 70�F** is a reasonable target, and older guests generally want it warmer than you do.
- **An extra blanket at the foot of the bed**, folded, always  even in summer.
- **A small space heater in winter** for a guest room over a garage or on a cold corner. Make sure it is rated for indoor use, plugged directly into a wall outlet, and has a tip-over switch.
- **A fan in summer**, and tell them where it is.
- **If the thermostat is locked or in another part of the house**, say so and offer to adjust it. Guests will not ask, and they will be cold all night instead.

## Step 5: Solve sound before it becomes a problem

Guests are polite about noise and will not mention it.

- **Listen from the guest bed with the door closed** while someone walks around and opens cabinets. You will hear things you have stopped noticing.
- **A white noise machine or a fan** masks kitchen and hallway sound and is the cheapest fix.
- **Fix the rattling door** with a felt pad or a strike plate adjustment. A door that clicks all night is memorable in the wrong way.
- **Warn them about the schedule**  the dog that barks at 6 a.m., the school run, the early riser. A warning converts an annoyance into a fact.

## Step 6: Make the kitchen obviously available

The moment a guest is most uncertain is the first morning, when they are awake before you and do not know whether to help themselves.

- **Show them where the coffee, tea, mugs, and glasses are** the night before, in person, not verbally in passing.
- **Set up a self-serve shelf**: coffee, tea, sugar, a kettle or a pod machine they can operate without a tutorial, and a few things for breakfast.
- **Leave a bottle of water in the room**, refreshed daily. Guests drink less than they should because asking feels like an imposition.
- **Say the sentence out loud**: "Help yourself to anything, any time." Guests need explicit permission, and they will not infer it.

## Step 7: Put the practical information in writing

A single printed card in the room removes twenty small questions:

- **Wifi network and password**, in large type.
- **The bathroom**: which door, and whether the shower has a quirk.
- **The kitchen**: where things are, and that they should help themselves.
- **The front door**: how the lock works, and whether they should lock it behind them.
- **Your phone number**, even though they have it  it saves searching.
- **Quiet hours and any house rules**, phrased as information rather than rules.

Write it once, laminate it or put it in a small frame, and reuse it every visit.

## Step 8: Handle pets and allergies in advance

This is the one area where asking beforehand is not optional.

- **Ask about allergies** when you invite them, not when they arrive. Cat and dog dander persists in soft furnishings for weeks.
- **If a guest is allergic**, keep the pet out of the guest room for at least a week before the visit, wash the bedding and curtains, and vacuum with a HEPA filter. A closed door during the visit is not enough on its own.
- **Warn about the dog's behavior**  jumping, barking, nervous around strangers. Guests who know in advance can prepare; guests who do not spend the visit on edge.
- **Ask about food restrictions** at the same time. It is much easier to plan a meal than to improvise one at 7 p.m.

## Step 9: Give them a way out and a way in

Small logistical items that hosts forget:

- **A key or a door code.** Guests who cannot come and go freely feel trapped, and they will not say so.
- **Tell them your schedule**, especially when you will be out, so they are not waiting for permission to leave.
- **Clear closet space with hangers**, and a place to put a suitcase  a luggage rack or a bench, not the floor.
- **A mirror in the room**, full length if possible. People dress in the room they sleep in.

## Step 10: Do a thirty-minute reset after they leave

The reset is what makes the next visit easy:

- **Strip the bed and wash everything**, including the blanket and the pillow protectors. Damp towels in a hamper for a week smell.
- **Restock the toiletry tray** and note what ran out.
- **Check for forgotten items**  chargers are the most common, then glasses, then jewelry. Look under the bed and in the bathroom.
- **Empty the bin and wipe the surfaces.**
- **Note one thing to fix** for next time, in a phone note: the lamp that flickers, the door that sticks, the towel hook that is too high.

## Why This Works

The preparation is organized around the guest's experience of the space rather than around the room's appearance. Guests need to find a light in the dark, know where the bathroom is, have somewhere to put a wet towel, and feel permitted to use the kitchen. None of that shows in a photo, and all of it determines whether they describe the visit as relaxing.

The written card and the self-serve shelf do a specific kind of work: they replace questions that guests are reluctant to ask. Removing the need to ask is the actual definition of hospitality.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Making the bed the whole project | Bedding is the least-noticed element | Light, bathroom, and kitchen access first |
| No night light on the route | Guests navigate in the dark or wake you | Night light plus a lamp by the bed |
| One towel hook, or none | Wet towels go on the floor or the shower rod | Two hooks at different heights |
| Toiletries scattered in a drawer | Reads as leftovers; guests do not look | A tray, visible, restocked |
| Locked thermostat | Guests will not ask and will be cold | Offer control, or adjust on request |
| No bedside outlet | Every guest now travels with a phone | Power strip or USB outlet by the bed |
| Saying "make yourself at home" once | Guests need to be shown, not told | Walk them through the kitchen |
| Assuming no allergies | Dander persists in soft furnishings for weeks | Ask when inviting; prep the room |
| No key or code | Guests feel trapped and do not say so | Spare key or code, plus your schedule |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What do guests most often forget?**

A toothbrush, a phone charger, and a hairdryer, in that order. Keeping a few travel toothbrushes, a USB outlet by the bed, and a spare hairdryer covers almost every visit. A tray of travel-size toiletries handles the rest.

**What should a guest bathroom have?**

Two towel hooks, a bin with a liner, spare toilet paper in plain sight, a shelf or clear counter for their toiletry bag, a night light, and a hairdryer. Guests will not open your cabinets, so anything they need has to be visible.

**How do you make a guest room comfortable?**

Fix the route to the bathroom first  night light, reachable lamp, clear floor. Then an extra blanket, a power outlet by the bed, a fan or white noise machine, and a printed card with the wifi password and house information. The bed matters less than any of these.

**Should you provide toiletries for guests?**

Yes, at minimum a toothbrush, toothpaste, shampoo, conditioner, and lotion. It costs very little and removes the most common source of embarrassment. Keep them on a visible tray so guests know they are for them.

**How do you handle a guest with pet allergies?**

Ask when you invite them, not when they arrive. Keep the pet out of the guest room for at least a week beforehand, wash the bedding and curtains, and vacuum with a HEPA filter. A closed door during the visit is not sufficient on its own.

**How do you make guests feel at home?**

Show rather than tell. Walk them through where the coffee, mugs, and glasses are, say explicitly that they should help themselves at any time, give them a key or door code, and tell them your schedule so they know when they can come and go.

**What should you do after guests leave?**

Strip and wash everything including blankets and pillow protectors, restock the toiletry tray, empty the bin, and check for forgotten chargers, glasses, and jewelry under the bed. Then note one improvement for next time.

**Do you need a dedicated guest room?**

No. A comfortable sofa bed with a night light, a hook for a towel, a shelf for their things, and access to a bathroom works for a short visit. What guests need is privacy at night and clarity about the bathroom  not a separate room with a door.
