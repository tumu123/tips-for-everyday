You have a quote for a repair. You also know roughly what a new one costs. The question is which one costs you less over the next few years — and that is not the same as which one costs less today.

The standard advice is the **50% rule**: if the repair costs more than half the price of a replacement, replace. It is a decent starting point, but applied alone it is wrong often enough to be expensive. A $300 repair on a six-year-old $1,200 washer is a clear fix. A $300 repair on a thirteen-year-old washer that costs $220 a year more to run than a current model is a clear replace — even though the repair is only 25% of the replacement price.

Here is the fuller version, in the order you should actually apply it.

---

## Step 1: Where Is It in Its Expected Life?

This comes first because it sets everything else. A repair that makes sense at year 4 rarely makes sense at year 14.

| Appliance | Typical lifespan | Repair rarely worth it after |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigerator | 12–17 years | 12 years |
| Washing machine | 10–13 years | 10 years |
| Dryer (electric) | 12–15 years | 12 years |
| Dryer (gas) | 12–15 years | 12 years |
| Dishwasher | 9–12 years | 9 years |
| Gas range / oven | 13–17 years | 15 years |
| Electric range | 13–15 years | 13 years |
| Water heater (tank) | 8–12 years | 8 years |
| Water heater (tankless) | 15–20 years | 15 years |
| Microwave | 7–10 years | 7 years |
| Central air conditioner | 12–17 years | 12 years |
| Heat pump | 10–15 years | 12 years |
| Furnace (gas) | 15–20 years | 18 years |
| Garbage disposal | 8–12 years | 10 years |

Find the model and serial number — usually on a sticker inside the door frame, on the back, or behind the kick panel. Most manufacturers encode the production date in the serial number, and you can look up the decoding pattern for your brand.

**Realistic expectations:** the ranges above assume normal use and basic maintenance. A washer running six loads a week in a hard-water area will land at the low end. Skip the maintenance and subtract a few more years.

---

## Step 2: Apply the Repair Cost Ratio — With Adjustments

```
Repair cost ÷ Replacement cost = Ratio
```

Then read the ratio against the appliance's age, not in isolation.

| Machine age | Repair worth it up to | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Under 25% of lifespan | 70–80% of replacement | Plenty of useful life left to amortize |
| 25–50% of lifespan | ~50% of replacement | The classic rule |
| 50–75% of lifespan | 30–40% of replacement | Remaining life is limited |
| Over 75% of lifespan | 20% or less | You're buying a short reprieve |

**Worked example.** Dishwasher, 8 years old, expected life ~11 years — that's roughly 73% through its life, so the threshold is 30–40% of replacement. A new comparable unit is $650. Repair quote is $280, which is 43% — above the threshold. **Replace.**

Change one variable: same dishwasher at 4 years old (36% of life), threshold becomes ~50%. $280 against $650 is 43% — now below the threshold. **Repair.**

Same repair, same cost, opposite decisions. The age is doing the work.

---

## Step 3: Add the Efficiency Gap — The Number Most People Miss

This is the step that changes the most decisions, and it's the one almost nobody calculates.

Appliances sold today use substantially less energy than models from ten to fifteen years ago, because efficiency standards have tightened. The gap is biggest for:

- **Refrigerators** — the largest improvement of any common appliance
- **Clothes washers** — especially front-loaders, which also extract more water so dryer runs get shorter
- **Water heaters** — tankless and heat-pump units cost far less to run
- **Air conditioners and heat pumps** — SEER ratings have moved substantially

**How to estimate your annual savings.** Look at the yellow EnergyGuide label on a candidate replacement — it gives an estimated yearly electricity use in kWh. Then find your current unit's consumption:

1. Check the same EnergyGuide label on your existing machine, if it's still legible
2. Or find the model number's original specification sheet
3. Or measure it directly with a plug-in power meter over a week

Multiply the difference in kWh by your electricity rate. At the US average, each 100 kWh/year of difference is roughly $15/year.

**When this matters most:**

| Situation | Efficiency gap matters? |
|---|---|
| Old fridge (15+ years) running 24/7 | Decisively — can exceed $100/yr |
| Washer used daily in a large household | Strongly |
| Water heater | Strongly — hot water is often the second-largest energy use |
| Microwave, toaster, small appliances used minutes a day | Barely — ignore it |
| Gas oven used a few times a week | Modest |

There is a real ceiling here. On a rarely used appliance, the efficiency difference might be $10–20 a year, which will not justify a $600 replacement under any honest math. **Run the efficiency calculation only on things that run often or run continuously.**

## Step 4: Count the Second Failure Risk

A repair fixes the part that failed. It does nothing about the parts that are the same age.

Once a machine passes roughly 60–70% of its expected life, the probability of another significant failure within two years rises noticeably. This does not show up on the invoice, but it belongs in the decision: if you repair an aging machine, budget for the possibility of paying again.

**Practical framing:** a repair on an old appliance buys expected remaining life, not full remaining life. A 12-year-old refrigerator repaired today might realistically give you two or three more years, not five.

---

## The Fast Decision Table

| Your situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| Under warranty | Repair — always, unless the manufacturer opts to replace |
| Under 3 years old | Repair, essentially regardless of ratio |
| 25–50% of life, repair under 50% of new | Repair |
| 25–50% of life, repair over 50% of new | Compare efficiency gap; lean replace |
| Over 75% of life, any significant repair | Replace |
| Repair is a cheap, non-critical part | Repair |
| Compressor, control board, or heat exchanger failed | Usually replace |
| Safety-related failure (gas, wiring) | Replace |
| Parts discontinued or unavailable | Replace |

## Category-Specific Thresholds

Generic rules hide useful detail. Here is where each type of appliance actually breaks.

**Refrigerators.** The compressor is the dividing line. Compressor replacement commonly runs a large fraction of the cost of a new unit, and on a machine past ten years it is rarely the right call. Door seals, thermostats, defrost heaters, and fans are comparatively cheap and worth fixing at almost any age.

**Washing machines.** Bearing and drum assembly failures effectively total the machine — the labor alone is substantial, and the job can approach replacement cost. Drive belts, pumps, door locks, and inlet valves are routine repairs worth doing mid-life.

**Dryers.** Usually worth repairing. Dryers are mechanically simple, and heating elements, thermostats, belts, and rollers are inexpensive parts. A twelve-year-old dryer with a failed element is a straightforward fix — unless the drum seals are also going.

**Dishwashers.** Pumps, drain valves, and door latches are worth fixing. A failed control board on a machine past eight years usually is not, since the board is one of the most expensive single parts.

**Water heaters.** Tank failure is terminal — you cannot repair a corroded tank, and a leaking tank means replacement. Elements, thermostats, and the anode rod are normal maintenance. If the unit is past eight years and the tank itself is involved, replace. See our [water heater maintenance guide](/fix-it/maintenance/water-heater-maintenance/) for the upkeep that delays this decision.

**Ranges and ovens.** Gas units have long service lives and are usually worth repairing. On electric units, a failed control board on an older range is often the end.

**Air conditioners and heat pumps.** Two factors dominate: the age of the unit and its refrigerant type. Systems using **R-22 (Freon)** have effectively reached end-of-life — the refrigerant has been phased out of production, so recharging is expensive and getting worse. A leaking R-22 coil is a replacement decision, not a repair decision. Compressor failures on units past ten years also generally point to replacement.

**Garbage disposals.** Almost never worth a service call. The labor cost of diagnosis plus repair typically approaches the cost of a new mid-range unit, and replacement is a straightforward job.

## Getting an Accurate Repair Quote

The decision is only as good as the number you're comparing. Repair quotes vary widely for identical work, so:

**Ask for the diagnostic fee to be credited against the repair.** Many companies do this; some do not. It's a fair question and it changes the math on small repairs.

**Get the breakdown written out: parts, labor, and trip charge separately.** This is the only way to tell whether you're being charged $60 for a part that costs $15, and it makes comparison possible.

**Ask directly: "Is this a symptom or the cause?"** A burnt-out pump may be the result of a control problem that will just burn out the next pump. Good technicians will tell you.

**Check parts availability before committing.** On machines over ten years old, parts are sometimes discontinued. A repair that can't be completed in one visit often makes replacement the better option.

**Get a second quote for anything over $300.** The spread on larger repairs is often wide enough to change the decision entirely.

## Before You Replace: Lower the Real Cost

If you land on replace, the sticker price is not the final number.

**Timing.** Holiday weekends and model-year changeovers are when appliance retailers discount most heavily. New models typically arrive in the autumn for major appliances.

**Ask about floor models, scratch-and-dent, and open-box.** Cosmetic damage on a side panel that will face a wall can mean a substantial discount with a full warranty.

**Check utility rebates.** Many utilities offer rebates for high-efficiency appliances, particularly heat-pump water heaters and heat-pump dryers. These can be significant and are frequently unclaimed.

**Haul-away and installation.** Ask whether they're included. Delivery and installation add meaningfully to the total.

**Old unit value.** Some scrap yards pay for units with copper and steel content, and some retailers offer a discount for haul-away. A working unit that simply doesn't fit your needs may sell locally.

If the replacement will be a used or refurbished unit, the warranty is the whole decision — [buying the replacement refurbished](/save-money/shopping/buying-refurbished-appliances/) separates factory recertified from a wiped-down return.

## Why This Works

**The 50% rule is a proxy, not the real variable.** What you're actually buying with a repair is expected years of service, so the only coherent question is: what does a year of service cost under each option? Age-adjusted thresholds approximate that without requiring you to forecast failure dates.

**Efficiency gains are a real, recurring cash flow, not a marketing claim.** Standards have genuinely tightened over the last fifteen years, and for continuously-running appliances the annual difference is large enough to shift the decision. It is also the easiest term to overstate — which is why it should be calculated from your measured or labeled consumption, not from a retailer's savings claim.

**Failure risk rises with age, and it's asymmetric.** Repairing an old machine does not reset the clock on the parts you didn't touch. Once you account for the probability of a second repair, the repair option gets worse the older the machine is.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Deciding on the ratio alone | Replaces machines with years of life left, or repairs machines that fail again in months | Age-adjust the threshold first |
| Assuming new always means cheaper to run | On rarely used appliances the savings are negligible | Only calculate efficiency for high-use items |
| Replacing a unit that has one cheap failed part | Hundreds spent to fix a $40 problem | Get the parts/labor breakdown |
| Repairing past the point of parts availability | Weeks waiting, then a second service call | Confirm parts exist before approving |
| Ignoring the diagnostic fee | A "cheap" repair isn't cheap once fees are added | Ask whether the diagnosis is credited |
| Rushing the replacement because the old one failed | You pay full price under time pressure | If possible, replace before failure, on your schedule |
| Throwing away a unit under warranty | You pay for a covered repair | Check the warranty first — always |
| Comparing a repair quote to a premium replacement | Skews toward "repair" incorrectly | Compare to an equivalent-feature model |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the 50% rule still valid?**
It's a reasonable starting point, but it should be adjusted for age. A young machine justifies a higher ratio; an old machine justifies a much lower one.

**How long should a washing machine last?**
Typically 10–13 years. Heavy use, hard water, and skipped maintenance pull that toward the lower end.

**Is it worth replacing a 15-year-old refrigerator?**
Often yes, even if it still runs. Refrigerators run continuously, so the efficiency difference between a 15-year-old unit and a current one can be the largest of any appliance in the house.

**Should I repair a gas dryer myself?**
Simple parts like belts and rollers are reasonable DIY jobs. Anything involving the gas supply should go to a qualified technician.

**When is a repair never worth it?**
When the failure is in the compressor, the heat exchanger, or a corroded water heater tank — these approach or exceed replacement cost, and on an aging unit the rest of the machine is the same age.

**Does a home warranty make this easier?**
It can cap your cost, but read the terms. Service fees, coverage limits, and exclusions for pre-existing conditions often apply.

**Should I replace before it fails?**
Usually better financially — you can shop for a sale instead of paying full price under time pressure. The tradeoff is you lose the remaining useful life of the old unit.

**How do I find my appliance's age?**
Model and serial number, usually inside the door frame, on the back, or behind a kick panel. Most brands encode the manufacture date in the serial; search for your brand's decoding format.
