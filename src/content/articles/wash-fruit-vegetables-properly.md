Produce washing has accumulated more folklore than almost any other kitchen task — salt soaks, vinegar rinses, commercial washes, baking soda baths — and the research on all of it is remarkably consistent: **running water and friction do most of the work, and everything else adds little.**

That is not a reason to skip washing. It is a reason to stop overcomplicating it and to spend your attention on the parts that actually change the outcome: what you wash, when you wash it, and how you dry it.

---

## Step 1: Know what washing is actually for

There are two contaminants people worry about, and they are not equally dangerous.

**Microbes — soil, manure, and handling bacteria** like *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, and *Listeria*. This is the risk that actually causes illness, and it is the one washing genuinely reduces — by physically removing the film of soil and organisms, not by killing them.

**Pesticide residue** — present, usually at levels far below acute toxicity thresholds, and substantially reduced by washing plus peeling. Studies consistently find washing removes a meaningful fraction of surface residues, and that elaborate methods do not meaningfully outperform water.

**What washing cannot do:** sterilize. Water does not kill bacteria. A contaminated leaf remains contaminated after rinsing — it just carries less of the load. This matters for how you handle produce afterward: clean produce still needs a clean knife, a clean board, and refrigeration.

One more thing washing does: it removes grit, sand, and the occasional insect, which is reason enough on its own.

Washing also removes the eggs that arrive on the skin. For what happens when they stay, and the ten-day cycle that follows, see [how to break the fruit fly cycle](/pest-control/insects/fruit-flies-keep-coming-back/).

## Step 2: Do the thirty-second running-water rub

This is the whole technique. Everything else is a variation.

1. **Turn on cold water** and let it run. A steady stream, not a trickle.
2. **Rub the surface with your hands** for about 30 seconds. For firm produce, use a vegetable brush.
3. **Work the water over every surface**, including the ends and the stem scar.
4. **Shake off excess** and dry — see Step 6.

Why friction matters: microbes and residues sit in a thin biofilm that adheres to the waxy surface. Water alone loosens some of it; rubbing breaks the film and lets the water carry it away. Studies comparing rinsing with and without rubbing show a large difference — the mechanical action is the active ingredient.

For smooth produce like apples, tomatoes, and cucumbers, hands are enough. For potatoes, carrots, and squash, use a brush and accept that you will lose a little skin.

**Do not use dish soap or household detergent.** Produce is porous, and soap residues are not meant to be eaten. The FDA explicitly does not recommend it. Water plus friction, then dry.

## Step 3: Wash the rind even when you don't eat it

This is the most commonly skipped step and the one with the clearest safety case.

A melon, an avocado, an orange, a pineapple — the outside is not eaten, so it feels pointless to wash. But the knife that cuts through the rind carries whatever was on the surface straight into the flesh. Cantaloupe and watermelon have caused documented *Listeria* and *Salmonella* outbreaks exactly this way, because the rough netted rind is nearly impossible to fully clean and the flesh inside is a perfect growth medium.

**Wash and dry before cutting:** melons, pineapple, citrus you will zest or slice, avocado, squash, cucumber, mango, papaya.

If you zest a lemon, the rind goes directly into the food — wash it like you plan to eat it.

## Step 4: Decide what not to wash until the day you eat it

Washing adds water, and water is what spoils most produce. This is the counterintuitive part: **for things you plan to store, washing shortens life.**

**Don't wash before storing:**

- **Berries** — the bloom on a blueberry and the fine skin on a raspberry are the barrier; washing removes it and mold starts within a day. Rinse in a colander right before eating.
- **Mushrooms** — they are sponges. Wipe with a damp cloth or brush off the compost; a quick rinse immediately before cooking is fine, a soak is not.
- **Potatoes, onions, garlic, winter squash** — surface dirt protects them during storage. Brush off dry soil and store unwashed.
- **Leafy greens and herbs** — water in the leaves accelerates rot. Wash, dry very thoroughly, and store with a paper towel, or wash only what you are about to use.
- **Anything you bought pre-washed** — see Step 7.

**Do wash before storing only if** you dry it completely afterward — a salad spinner plus a towel. Any residual moisture undoes the benefit.

A workable rule: **wash at the moment of use, not at the moment of purchase.** The exceptions are things that come in very dirty (leeks, sandy greens) where you want the grit gone before it spreads through the fridge.

## Step 5: Skip most of the internet chemistry

The popular soaks, ranked by what the evidence actually supports:

| Method | Claim | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| Running water + rub | Removes soil and microbes | **Works.** The baseline everything else is measured against |
| Baking soda soak (1 tsp per 2 cups, 12–15 min) | Breaks down pesticide residue | Modestly better than water for some residues; not a substitute for washing, and no help against microbes |
| Vinegar soak (1:3, a few minutes) | Kills bacteria | Some reduction in surface bacteria; inconsistent against pesticides; can soften texture and leave flavor |
| Salt water soak | "Draws out" dirt and bugs | Helps dislodge insects in broccoli and cauliflower; does not kill bacteria at usable concentrations |
| Commercial produce wash | Purpose-built | Performs about the same as water in independent tests, at many times the cost |
| Bleach or soap | "Restaurant-style sanitizing" | Not for home use on food. Residues are a hazard |

If you want one upgrade over plain water, **baking soda** is the only one with a plausible mechanism for pesticide residues, and only on smooth-skinned produce. It will not help with bacteria, and it does nothing for the inside of a strawberry.

## Step 6: Dry it, because wet produce spoils

Drying is not cosmetic. Surface water is the single biggest accelerator of mold and bacterial growth in a fridge.

- **Salad spinner** for leaves and herbs — the most useful piece of equipment in this whole process.
- **Clean towel or paper towels** for everything else. Pat dry, don't rub berries.
- **Air dry** firm produce on a rack for 20 minutes if you have the counter space.
- Then store in a **vented container or a bag with a paper towel**, not sealed wet.

For herbs specifically, the sequence is: wash, spin, lay on a towel, roll loosely, refrigerate. Wet herbs in a sealed bag turn to slime in two days.

## Step 7: Handle the special cases

**Pre-washed salad greens.** Triple-washed bagged greens are washed in chlorinated water in a facility and are labeled "ready to eat." Re-washing them at home in a domestic sink is more likely to introduce contamination than remove it. If you rinse them anyway, use a clean colander and don't soak.

**Berries.** Rinse in a colander under gentle running water for 10–15 seconds, spread on a towel to dry, and eat. Never soak — they take on water and collapse.

**Mushrooms.** Brush or wipe. If they are genuinely dirty, a fast rinse and immediate patting dry is fine; the "never let a mushroom touch water" rule is overstated, but soaking is real damage.

**Leafy greens and leeks.** Separate the layers and rinse where the grit hides — the base of a leek, the stems of kale. Swish in a bowl of water, lift out, repeat until the water is clean.

**Root vegetables.** Brush under running water; peel if you like. Don't scrub so hard you remove the skin unless you plan to peel anyway.

**Organic produce.** Wash it exactly the same way. Organic certification says nothing about manure contact or handling contamination — if anything, the absence of post-harvest washes makes washing more relevant.

## Why This Works

The physics is simple: contamination sits in a thin film on a waxy surface, and removing a film requires both a solvent and shear force. Water is the solvent; rubbing, brushing, and the impact of a running stream provide the shear. Soaking provides solvent but no shear, which is why a long bath underperforms a short scrub.

That also explains why the additives underperform. Vinegar, salt, and baking soda change the chemistry of the water slightly, but they don't increase the mechanical removal that does the work — and where they do help, it is usually by loosening the film (as baking soda does on some pesticide residues) rather than by killing anything.

Drying works because moisture is the limiting factor for microbial growth on a surface. Remove the water and you remove the conditions for the population to expand during storage.

And the rind rule follows from how a knife works. A blade moving through a contaminated surface drags contamination along its path, depositing it in the flesh. Washing the outside protects the inside, which is why the melon outbreaks keep happening to people who washed only what they ate.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Soaking produce in the sink for a long bath | No shear force; contamination redistributes in the water | 30 seconds under running water with your hands |
| Using dish soap or detergent | Residues soak into porous produce | Water and friction only |
| Washing berries before storing them | Removes the protective bloom, invites mold | Rinse in a colander just before eating |
| Skipping the melon because you don't eat the rind | The knife carries surface bacteria into the flesh | Wash and dry all rinds before cutting |
| Re-washing triple-washed salad greens in a home sink | Domestic sinks harbor more bacteria than the washing facility | Use straight from the bag, or rinse briefly in a clean colander |
| Storing leafy greens wet | Surface water accelerates rot | Spin, towel dry, store with a paper towel |
| Trusting a vinegar soak to sanitize | Inconsistent against bacteria, useless against pesticides | Rinse and rub; cook high-risk items thoroughly |
| Assuming organic means clean | Organic produce still carries soil and handling bacteria | Wash identically |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does baking soda remove pesticides from vegetables?**
It helps somewhat with certain surface residues on smooth produce, but it is not a substitute for washing and does nothing against bacteria. Water plus friction remains the effective baseline.

**Should you wash mushrooms?**
Yes, briefly. A quick rinse and immediate drying is fine; the "mushrooms must never touch water" rule is overstated. What actually damages them is soaking, which makes them waterlogged.

**Do you need to wash pre-washed salad greens?**
Generally no. Bagged greens labeled ready-to-eat are washed in a commercial facility, and re-washing in a domestic sink can add contamination rather than remove it.

**Is vinegar effective for washing fruit?**
It reduces surface bacteria slightly, but results are inconsistent and it does not meaningfully address pesticide residue. It can also soften texture and leave flavor in delicate produce.

**How long should you wash vegetables?**
About 30 seconds of running water with your hands rubbing the surface. Firm items can take a brush. Longer soaking does not help and can damage texture.

**Do you have to wash fruit you are going to peel?**
Yes — the knife drags surface contamination through the peel into the flesh, and your hands transfer it afterward. Melons and citrus especially.

**Can you get sick from unwashed produce?**
Yes. Soil and handling bacteria such as *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, and *Listeria* are the main risk, and washing reduces the load substantially. It does not eliminate it, so refrigeration and cooking still matter.

**Should you wash eggs or meat?**
No. Washing raw poultry spreads bacteria around the sink, and washing eggs removes their protective cuticle. Cook meat thoroughly and handle it hygienically instead.
