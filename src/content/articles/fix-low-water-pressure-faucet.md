Before you call anyone about low pressure, run one test that takes thirty seconds and tells you which of three problems you have.

**Open the problem faucet, then open a different faucet elsewhere in the house.** If the second one is strong, your house pressure is fine and the restriction is local to that one fixture. If every faucet in the house is weak, this is a supply or pressure-reducing-valve problem, and it is a different job.

**Pressure and flow are not the same thing.** Pressure is the force in the pipe; flow is how much water actually comes out. A restriction anywhere in the path reduces flow at the outlet while the pressure behind it stays normal. That is why "low pressure" at one faucet is almost always a local blockage rather than a pressure fault.

There are three places the restriction can be, and testing them in this order avoids taking things apart unnecessarily.

## Step 0: Determine Hot, Cold, or Both

Run the faucet hot, then cold, then both.

**Cold weak, hot fine** → the problem is on the cold side.

**Hot weak, cold fine** → the problem is on the hot side, which often points at the water heater's outlet or a partially closed valve there.

**Both weak** → the restriction is somewhere they share: the aerator, the spout, or the faucet body itself.

This single observation eliminates half the possibilities before you touch a tool.

## Step 1: Clean the Aerator (Start Here)

The aerator is the screw-on tip of the spout, and it is the most common cause by a wide margin. It contains a fine screen that catches grit, scale, and debris from pipe work.

Unscrew it by hand if you can. If it will not turn, use pliers with a cloth over the aerator to protect the finish — or an aerator wrench, which is a cheap plastic tool made for this.

**Some aerators are recessed into the spout** and are removed with a coin or a hex key rather than from outside. If you cannot see threads, look up inside the spout.

Disassemble what comes apart — usually a housing, a screen or disc stack, and a rubber washer. Note the order.

Soak the parts in white vinegar for twenty to thirty minutes, then scrub gently with a brush. Rinse and reassemble in the original order.

**Do not poke the screen with a pin.** The mesh is fine and easily torn, and a torn screen stops doing its job even though it looks intact.

If cleaning does not restore flow, replace the aerator. They cost very little and are worth replacing rather than fighting with.

## Step 2: Flush the Spout Without the Aerator

With the aerator removed, run the water into a bucket for a few seconds.

**If flow is now strong, you have found it** — the aerator was the restriction, and you are done.

If flow is still weak with the aerator off, the restriction is further upstream. Continue.

Look inside the spout while the water runs. Some faucets have a second screen or a flow restrictor inside the spout body, and debris can collect there.

## Step 3: Check the Angle Stops

Look under the sink for the two shut-off valves — one hot, one cold — where the supply lines come out of the wall.

**Confirm both are fully open.** A stop that looks open may not be fully open: the handle should be turned fully counterclockwise, and if it is a multi-turn valve, count the turns out and back to be sure it is not partially closed.

A stop that was closed down for a previous repair and never reopened is a genuinely common cause of weak flow at one fixture, and it costs nothing to rule out.

While you are there, look at the supply lines. A kinked flexible line restricts flow dramatically, and a corroded or internally collapsed line does the same without any visible sign.

## Step 4: Flush the Supply Lines

If the stops are open and the lines look sound, flush them.

Close the angle stop. Disconnect the supply line at the faucet end — not at the stop end — and direct the free end into a bucket.

**Open the stop briefly and let it run.** This pushes out any debris sitting in the line or in the stop itself. Catch it in the bucket and look at what comes out — sediment, rust particles, or pieces of degraded rubber from a failing washer inside the stop.

Reconnect the line and test.

This step finds the failures that look like faucet failures but are actually a disintegrating rubber washer inside the angle stop. It is more common than most people expect, especially on older multi-turn stops.

## Step 5: Check Inside the Faucet Body

If flow is still weak at the faucet end with the line disconnected and flowing freely into a bucket, the restriction is inside the faucet.

**That test is the discriminator:** strong flow into the bucket means the supply is fine and the faucet is the problem. Weak flow into the bucket means the problem is upstream of the faucet entirely.

Inside the faucet, the usual culprits are:

- **Debris caught in the cartridge ports** — remove the cartridge and inspect it, as described in fixing a dripping valve
- **A failed or dislodged internal check valve** — more common on pull-out and pull-down spray faucets
- **Scale buildup in the faucet body** — more likely with hard water and an older fixture

For a pull-out or pull-down faucet, also check the spray head and the hose. The hose can kink inside the spout where you cannot see it, and the spray head has its own screen.

## Step 6: Decide Repair or Replace

At this point you know where the restriction is, and the decision is mostly economic.

A [cartridge](/fix-it/plumbing/fix-dripping-shower-valve/) is a cheap, standard repair. A faucet body that is scaled internally on a fixture that is already fifteen years old is usually not worth the time — replacing the whole faucet is often faster and more reliable than rebuilding it.

**If you do replace the faucet, replace the supply lines at the same time.** They are cheap, they are already disconnected, and old lines are a common leak point.

## Why This Works

The method works because it tests from the outlet backward, and each test eliminates a whole section of the path rather than guessing.

Start at the aerator because it is the highest-probability cause and needs no tools. Flush without it because that isolates the aerator from everything upstream in one step. Check the stops because they cost nothing to verify. Flush the lines because that separates the supply from the fixture. And test flow into a bucket because that single measurement tells you whether the money should go into the faucet or into the plumbing behind the wall.

The underlying reason this is usually a local problem is that **restrictions do not reduce pressure, they reduce flow.** Pressure is present throughout the system; a partial blockage means the same pressure is pushing through a smaller opening, so less water comes out. That is why the rest of the house is unaffected, and why the fix is almost always at the fixture rather than at the main.

## What NOT to Do

**Do not start by adjusting the pressure-reducing valve.** If only one faucet is affected, the house pressure is fine. Lowering the PRV will make every fixture worse and fix nothing.

**Do not poke the aerator screen with a pin.** It tears easily and stops filtering.

**Do not assume a weak hot side is the faucet.** It often points to the water heater outlet or a partially closed valve there. Test hot and cold separately first.

**Do not use a wrench on the aerator without padding.** It mars the finish permanently.

**Do not forget to check whether the angle stops are fully open.** It is free to verify and is sometimes the entire problem.

**Do not disconnect the supply line at the wall end to flush it.** Disconnect at the faucet end so the water you release is contained and the stop still controls it.

**Do not rebuild an old faucet without pricing a new one.** On a fixture old enough to have internal scale, replacement is often the better value.

## FAQ

**Why is only one faucet affected?**
Because the restriction is local. House pressure is the same everywhere, so a blockage at one fixture reduces flow there and nowhere else.

**How often should I clean aerators?**
About once a year in average water, more often if you have hard water or have had recent plumbing work. Debris from pipe work is the most common thing an aerator catches.

**Can a water softener or filter cause low flow?**
Yes. A clogged whole-house filter reduces flow everywhere, which distinguishes it from a single-fixture problem. If every faucet weakened at the same time, check the filter first.

**Why did the flow drop suddenly rather than gradually?**
Sudden drops usually mean debris — a chunk of scale or sediment dislodged by work elsewhere and carried to the aerator. Gradual drops point to accumulating scale.

**Is low flow at one faucet dangerous?**
Not usually. But if it is accompanied by discolored water, it can indicate corrosion in the supply line, which is worth having looked at.

**Will a new faucet fix it?**
If you confirmed weak flow with the aerator and supply line ruled out, yes. If the flow into a bucket was weak too, a new faucet will not help — the problem is upstream.

**Can I remove the flow restrictor to get more flow?**
Check local rules first. In any case, on a single weak faucet the restrictor is rarely the cause; a clogged aerator is far more likely.

**What if hot and cold are both weak but the tub nearby is fine?**
That points to something shared by that faucet only — the aerator, the spout, or that fixture's stops. Work outward from the aerator.
