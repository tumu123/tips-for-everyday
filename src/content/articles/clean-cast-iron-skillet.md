Almost everything people believe about cleaning cast iron comes from one outdated fact. The rule "never use soap" came from an era when **soap was made with lye**, which would indeed strip seasoning. Modern dish soap is a surfactant and does not contain lye. **You can wash your skillet with soap.**

Once you clear that away, cast iron care becomes simple, because the actual enemy is not soap — it's **water sitting on iron**. Every step below exists to get the pan dry and keep a thin protective film on it.

---

## Step 1: Know what seasoning actually is

Seasoning is not baked-on grease. It's **polymerized oil** — a plastic-like coating formed when thin oil is heated past its smoke point and chemically bonds into a hard, slick layer bonded to the metal.

This matters because it explains the confusing parts:

- **It survives washing.** A polymerized layer doesn't dissolve in dish soap; it dissolves under abrasion or very high heat with lye.
- **It builds from thin layers.** Thick oil doesn't polymerize properly — it goes sticky and gummy, which is the most common home seasoning failure. **Thin is not a compromise; it's the requirement.**
- **It protects by being hydrophobic.** Seasoning keeps water off the iron. But it's porous at the microscopic level and not a perfect seal, which is why drying still matters.

Your goal isn't to avoid cleaning. It's to maintain this layer while not letting water reach bare iron.

## Step 2: Clean it while it's still warm, right after cooking

The easiest time to clean a pan is before food sets. And for cast iron there's an added wrinkle: **never let it sit with water in it.**

1. Cook, and let the pan cool only to warm — not scalding, but still hot to the touch. Cold plus hot risks thermal shock and cracking on very old or very thin pans, though it's rare with modern ones.
2. Scrape out solids with a metal or wooden spatula or a pan scraper.
3. Rinse with warm water. Add a small amount of dish soap.
4. Scrub with a stiff brush. **For stubborn bits, use coarse kosher salt as an abrasive** with a little oil and a paper towel, or a chainmail scrubber. Both are excellent on seasoning and take off stuck food without taking the seasoning with it.

If food is badly burnt on, put an inch of water in the warm pan and let it simmer for a few minutes to loosen it, then scrape and scrub. Just don't leave that water standing.

## Step 3: Soak only when actually needed — and briefly

Soaking is allowed in small doses; it's not the automatic ruin it's often made out to be. For genuinely carbonized food:

- Soak **15–30 minutes**, not overnight.
- Use warm water, possibly with a little soap.
- Afterward, expect to scrub, dry thoroughly, and re-oil — you may have thinned the seasoning slightly, which is fixable.

**Never** leave it in the sink overnight, never put it through a dishwasher cycle, and never leave leftovers sitting in it for days. Those cause rust, not just seasoning loss.

## Step 4: Dry thoroughly — the most important step

Rust forms when water contacts bare iron. Drying is not a formality; it's the whole point of the process.

1. Towel-dry it completely immediately after washing.
2. Then put it on the stovetop over **low heat for two to five minutes** until every trace of moisture is gone. Watch for the surface to look uniformly dry, with no darker damp patches. Water hides in the texture and around the handle attachment and around any heat ring on the bottom.

Skipping this step explains most rust problems. It takes three minutes and prevents nearly everything.

## Step 5: Oil it thin — thinner than you think

While the pan is still warm from drying:

1. Put a **small** amount of neutral oil in it — about half a teaspoon for a 10-inch pan.
2. Wipe it all over the cooking surface, the rim, the handle, and if practical the exterior.
3. Then — this is the step people skip — **wipe it out again with a clean cloth or paper towel as if you were trying to remove all of it.**

Step 3 is counterintuitive but essential. What remains after wiping off the excess is the thin film that polymerizes correctly. If you leave visible oil, heat will turn it into a **tacky, sticky, uneven layer** that attracts dust and feels like glue.

Common failure: "I seasoned it and now it's sticky." That's too much oil, or too low a temperature. Not insufficient cleaning.

## Step 6: Heat past the smoke point to set it

To polymerize the oil, it has to get hot enough:

**Stovetop method (convenient, fine for maintenance):** Heat the oiled pan over medium-high until you see the first wisps of smoke, hold for a minute or two, then let it cool. Done.

**Oven method (better for seasoning from bare):** Heat the oven to 450–500°F (230–260°C) — above your oil's smoke point. Wipe a very thin coat of oil over the whole pan including outside and handle, place it **upside down** on the middle rack with foil or a tray below to catch drips, bake for one hour, then turn the oven off and let it cool inside. Repeat 2–3 times for a new or stripped pan.

A few practical notes:

- Use an oil with a smoke point you can actually clear: grapeseed, refined sunflower, canola, or crisco all work well. Flaxseed polymerizes hard but can flake; it's popular but fussier.
- Slight smoking is expected. If it's smoking heavily and the kitchen fills with smoke, you used too much oil or too much heat — not a disaster, just wipe it back and continue.
- Modern non-lye dish soap is genuinely fine to use between coats.

## Step 7: Deal with rust when it appears

Surface rust is fixable and not a reason to retire a pan.

**Light surface rust (orange dust):** scrub it off with fine steel wool or a scrubber, then wash, dry, and re-season. Done in five minutes.

**More established rust:** soak the pan in equal parts white vinegar and water for **one to four hours** — longer invites damage to the iron itself. Scrub with steel wool, rinse, dry immediately, then re-season promptly, because bare iron will flash-rust within minutes of being exposed.

**Pitting and deep rust** — small pits are cosmetic and fine to cook on once cleaned and seasoned. Deep flaking is different; if a significant amount of metal has been lost, the pan's structural soundness is worth questioning.

After any rust removal, you're back to bare iron. Season it before cooking anything acidic.

## Step 8: Cook on it, and build seasoning by using it

The best thing for a skillet is use, particularly early on.

- **Start with forgiving fatty foods**: bacon, cornbread, roasted potatoes, seared steak. These build seasoning fast.
- **Hold off on long-simmered acidic dishes** — tomato sauce, braises with wine and vinegar, big pots of chili — for the first several cooks. Once the seasoning is established, occasional acidic cooking is fine; just don't leave acidic food sitting in it.
- **Preheat properly.** Cast iron holds heat because it's dense, but it needs time to get there — three to five minutes over medium before adding food. Most "everything sticks" complaints are preheat problems.
- **Store it dry.** If you stack pans, put a paper towel between them so trapped moisture doesn't create a rust spot.

The pan rewards use and punishes neglect in specific ways, but the choice comes before the care routine. For which material suits which job, and why cast iron holds a sear, see [the cookware material guide](/kitchen-tips/equipment/cookware-material-guide/).

## Why This Works

Cast iron care is entirely about managing one chemical reaction: iron plus oxygen plus water gives you rust. Seasoning is a barrier that reduces both water contact and oxygen contact, and it happens to be non-stick because polymerized oil is smooth.

Every step maps back to that:

- **Cleaning** removes food that would otherwise harbor moisture and harbor the acids that attack both iron and seasoning.
- **Drying** removes the water the reaction requires — hence why it's the highest-stakes step, not soap avoidance.
- **A thin oil film** provides new material to polymerize, filling microscopic gaps. Thick oil can't polymerize evenly because only the surface heats enough; the underlying layer stays semi-liquid and sticky.
- **Heating past smoke point** is what actually drives polymerization; below that temperature you just have warm oil that will go rancid and tacky.
- **Vinegar dissolves iron oxide** for rust removal, which is why it needs to be time-limited — leave it too long and it starts working on the iron itself.

The "no soap" rule survives not because it's correct but because people who follow it also **dry immediately and re-oil**, which is what actually protects the pan. Soap avoidance is harmless; skipping the drying is not.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Leaving it to soak overnight | Rust forms on bare iron within hours | Soak 15–30 min max, then dry immediately |
| Air-drying it | Water sitting on iron is exactly rust | Towel dry plus 2–5 min on low heat |
| Using thick layers of oil to season | Only the surface polymerizes; the rest goes sticky | Apply thin, then wipe it off like you made a mistake |
| Running it through the dishwasher | Detergent plus long hot wet cycle strips seasoning and rusts it | Wash by hand |
| Cooking tomato sauce in a brand-new pan | Acid attacks thin, young seasoning | Build up with fatty foods first |
| Assuming sticky means dirty | It's excess oil polymerized unevenly | Wipe back, heat harder next time |
| Scrubbing aggressively with steel wool every wash | Takes seasoning you worked to build | Brush or salt; steel wool only for rust |
| Storing it wet or stacked without a barrier | Trapped moisture creates rust spots | Dry fully; use a paper towel between stacked pans |

## Frequently Asked Questions
**Can you use soap on cast iron?**
Yes. The no-soap rule dates from when soap contained lye, which does strip seasoning. Modern dish soap doesn't contain lye and won't damage a polymerized surface. Wash it with soap, then dry it thoroughly.

**Why is my cast iron sticky?**
Too much oil, or not enough heat. Thick layers can't fully polymerize and stay tacky. Wipe the pan down as if removing all the oil before heating, and make sure it reaches its smoke point.

**My pan is rusty — is it ruined?**
Almost never. Light rust wipes off with a scrubber. Heavier rust responds to a vinegar-and-water soak of one to four hours, followed by steel wool and immediate re-seasoning. Only deep flaking and structural pitting are real problems.

**How often do I need to re-season?**
For a pan in regular use, rarely — normal cooking maintains it. Re-season when food starts sticking more than usual, the surface looks dull or dry, or you've had rust. The after-each-wash oil-and-dry routine is "maintenance seasoning" and takes seconds.

**Can I cook acidic foods in cast iron?**
Once seasoning is well established, yes, occasionally. Don't simmer tomato sauce or vinegar-heavy braises in it for hours, and never in a newly seasoned pan. Transfer acidic leftovers elsewhere rather than storing them in the skillet.

**What oil is best for seasoning?**
A neutral oil with a high smoke point: grapeseed, refined sunflower, canola, or shortening. Flaxseed polymerizes very hard but is prone to flaking. Avoid low-smoke-point oils like unrefined olive oil.

**Do I need to strip and start over if the seasoning is uneven?**
Usually not. Cook on it regularly, especially fatty foods, and keep up the wash-dry-oil routine — unevenness evens out over weeks. Complete stripping is reasonable only if it's flaking badly or you've inherited a pan in poor shape.

**Can I put a hot pan under cold water?**
Better not to, though modern cast iron is fairly tolerant. Thermal shock can crack old or thin pans. Let it cool to warm first, then wash with warm water.
