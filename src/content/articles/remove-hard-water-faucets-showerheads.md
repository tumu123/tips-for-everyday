Hard water deposits are calcium and magnesium carbonate. They are alkaline mineral solids that precipitate out of water when it evaporates, and they build up in layers — a thin film first, then a crust, then a rock. On a showerhead in a hard water area, that process takes about four to six weeks per visible layer.

Which tells you why scrubbing fails. You are not removing a film; you are trying to abrade a mineral with a cloth. Meanwhile the cloth is pushing the softened outer layer deeper into the aerator screen and the nozzle holes, where it sets harder.

Acid dissolves the mineral. That is the entire method. Everything else — which acid, how strong, how long — is calibration.

## Step 1: Which Acid, and How Strong

| Acid | Strength | Best for | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **White vinegar (5%)** | Weak acetic | Plastic showerheads, aerators, most chrome | Smells; can dull plated finishes with long soaks |
| **Citric acid powder** | Weak, but concentrated when mixed | Chrome, brushed nickel, anything you can soak | Needs dissolving; slightly more expensive |
| **CLR / dedicated descaler** | Formulated blend | Severe crust, whole-house fixtures | Costlier; follow the label exactly |
| **Lemon juice** | Very weak | Light films, spot work | Slow; the sugar leaves a residue |

**Citric acid is the better default for metal fixtures.** One tablespoon per cup of warm water gives a solution that dissolves scale faster than vinegar, has no smell, and is gentler on plated finishes. It is sold as a powder in the canning aisle, and a $10 bag lasts years.

**Vinegar is fine for plastic and for anything you are already going to replace.** It is cheap and always available, and the smell dissipates.

## What Not to Use

- **Hydrochloric-acid toilet bowl cleaner on chrome.** It attacks the plating and leaves a permanently cloudy, pitted surface. This is the most common way people destroy a faucet while trying to clean it.
- **Baking soda.** It is alkaline. Scale is alkaline. They do nothing to each other. Baking soda is for grease and odor, not minerals.
- **Steel wool and abrasive pads.** They scratch chrome and remove the brushed grain from nickel. A scratched finish holds scale faster afterward.
- **Bleach.** No effect on scale, and it degrades rubber washers and O-rings.
- **Overnight soaking of anything plated.** Vinegar left on chrome for 12 hours can dull it. Thirty to sixty minutes is the working window.

## Step 2: Faucets - The Aerator Is the Problem

Almost every "my faucet has low pressure and white crust" complaint is the aerator — the small screw-on tip at the end of the spout. It contains a screen and a flow restrictor, and both collect scale.

**Removing it:**

1. Wrap the aerator in a cloth and turn it counterclockwise by hand, or with pliers over the cloth. Do not grip bare metal with pliers.
2. **Lay the parts out in order as they come out.** Most aerators have three to five pieces: outer housing, screen, a plastic insert, sometimes a rubber washer. Reassembling them in the wrong order causes leaks and changes the flow pattern.
3. Soak all metal and plastic parts in vinegar or citric acid solution for 30 minutes.
4. Scrub the screen with an old toothbrush. Poke the holes clear with a pin if any remain blocked.
5. Rinse, reassemble in the order photographed, and run hot water for 30 seconds.

If the aerator is seized, do not force it — a twisted spout or a cracked aerator thread is a plumbing repair. Soak the whole tip in a bag of vinegar for an hour first, then try again with the cloth and pliers.

**For a faucet that will not come apart**, tie a zip bag of vinegar around the spout with a rubber band so the tip is submerged, and leave it 45 minutes. This is the same bag method used on showerheads, at a smaller scale.

## Step 3: Showerheads - Bag Method or Removal

**If the head unscrews** (most do, hand-tight on a ball joint), take it off and soak it fully in a bowl. That is the better method because it reaches the internal channels, not just the face.

**If it is fixed or you would rather not disturb the joint**, use the bag:

1. Fill a zip bag with enough vinegar to submerge the head.
2. Slip it over the showerhead and secure it with a rubber band around the pipe, above the joint.
3. Leave 30 to 60 minutes. Not overnight — the acid will start to affect rubber seals and any plated ring.
4. Remove the bag, run hot water for a minute, and scrub the face with a toothbrush.

**Clearing the nozzles:** most modern heads have soft rubber nozzles. Rub them with a thumb in a circular motion — the scale cracks off. For hard plastic or metal nozzles, use a pin, but only to loosen; do not ream the hole wider or you will change the spray pattern.

**Do not put a showerhead in the dishwasher.** The heat warps plastic bodies and the detergent is alkaline.

## Step 4: The Pressure Question

Low flow from a showerhead has three possible causes, and scale is only one:

1. **Scale in the nozzles or the internal filter screen.** The method above fixes it.
2. **A flow restrictor.** Many heads have a small plastic insert limiting flow to 2.0 or 1.5 gallons per minute by design. Removing it is legal in the US at the federal level for showerheads (unlike toilets), but it changes the head's rated performance and can void the warranty. Check before you assume the head is clogged.
3. **A clogged inlet filter.** Some heads and most hand-held models have a mesh washer at the threaded connection. It collects grit and scale and is often overlooked because it is inside the joint.

If the whole house has low pressure, the problem is not the fixtures — it is the pressure regulator, a partially closed valve, or a clogged whole-house filter. Cleaning fixtures will not help.

## Step 5: Preventing the Next Layer

Scale is a rate problem: it deposits in proportion to how long water sits on a surface and evaporates.

- **Squeegee or towel-dry the fixture after use.** Thirty seconds, and it roughly triples the interval between soaks. It works on the same principle as [drying glass shower doors](/home-hacks/cleaning/remove-hard-water-stains-shower-door/).
- **Leave the shower door open and run the fan** after showering. Less ambient humidity, less evaporation onto surfaces.
- **Do not let drips run.** A slow drip concentrates minerals at one point and builds a stalactite faster than the whole head does.
- **Install a whole-house softener** if the hardness is above about 10 grains per gallon. This is the only actual solution — everything else is maintenance. A softener also cuts soap use by half and extends water heater life.
- **Frequency:** monthly soak in very hard water (above 14 gpg), quarterly in moderate water.

## Scale Is Not Only a Fixture Problem

The white crust on a showerhead is the visible version of a process happening throughout the plumbing, and the invisible version costs more.

**The water heater.** Scale settles on the heating element and the tank floor. On a gas heater it insulates the burner from the water, so the tank takes longer to recover and the burner runs longer. On an electric heater it coats the elements directly, which is why the lower element is usually the first to fail in a hard water area. An annual flush — attach a hose to the drain valve, run until the water clears — removes the loose sediment but not the bonded scale.

**The washing machine.** Scale on the heating element and in the drum reduces wash performance, and the machine compensates by running longer cycles. Descaling with citric acid on a hot cycle once or twice a year is inexpensive and is covered in the [washing machine cleaning routine](/home-hacks/appliances/how-to-clean-washing-machine/).

**The kettle and coffee maker.** These are the highest-concentration cases because water is boiled and evaporated repeatedly, leaving mineral behind each time. A kettle that takes noticeably longer to boil is scaled. The [descaling method for kettles and coffee makers](/home-hacks/appliances/descale-kettle-coffee-maker/) is the same acid principle as this guide, at a stronger concentration.

**The ice maker and the dishwasher.** Both have small orifices and a heating element. A scaled ice maker produces cloudy, slow ice; a scaled dishwasher leaves film on glassware and eventually blocks the spray arms. Dishwasher scale is usually visible as a white haze on the interior walls.

**The toilet** is the extreme case — standing water plus constant evaporation means scale bonds to porcelain in a hard ring that ordinary bowl cleaner will not touch. That is a different job with a different method, covered in [removing hard water rings from a toilet bowl](/home-hacks/cleaning/remove-toilet-bowl-hard-water-ring/).

The general point: if you are descaling the showerhead every month, the water is the problem, not the fixture. A whole-house softener is the only intervention that changes the rate at which every one of these surfaces scales.

## When the Finish Is Already Gone

Chrome plating is a thin layer over brass or plastic. Once it has pitted — small dark spots, or a surface that feels rough even when clean — the plating is breached. Scale will now bond to the exposed base metal faster, and no cleaning restores the surface.

The signs that a fixture is past saving:

- **Pitting or flaking** on the body, especially at the base of the spout.
- **Green or white corrosion** at the joints, which indicates the plating is gone and the brass is reacting.
- **A crust that returns within a week** after a full soak, which means the surface is rough enough to hold deposit.
- **Cracked rubber nozzles** on a showerhead, which will leak in multiple directions regardless of scale.

At that point, replacing the aerator (a $3 part) or the showerhead (a $25 to $60 part) is cheaper than any descaler, and both are hand-tight replacements that need no plumber.

## FAQ

**Does vinegar really remove hard water stains?**

Yes — scale is an alkaline mineral, and vinegar is a weak acid that dissolves it. Soak for 30 to 60 minutes, then scrub lightly. It works better as a soak than as a spray-and-wipe, because the deposit is thick enough that surface contact alone does not reach the base layer.

**Is citric acid better than vinegar for descaling?**

For metal fixtures, usually yes. It dissolves scale faster at the same concentration, has no smell, and is gentler on plated finishes during longer soaks. It is sold as a powder in the canning aisle and a small bag lasts years.

**How long should I soak a showerhead in vinegar?**

Thirty to sixty minutes. Not overnight — extended acid contact can dull chrome and degrade rubber seals and O-rings. For severe crust, do two shorter soaks with a rinse and scrub in between rather than one long one.

**Why does my showerhead have low pressure after cleaning?**

Check the inlet filter screen at the threaded connection, which most people never look at, and check whether the head has a flow restrictor by design. If the whole house has low pressure, the cause is the regulator or a valve, not the fixture.

**Can I use baking soda on hard water stains?**

No. Baking soda is alkaline and scale is alkaline, so they do nothing to each other. Baking soda is for grease and odor. Use an acid — vinegar, citric acid, or a dedicated descaler.

**Can I put a showerhead in the dishwasher?**

No. The heat warps plastic bodies and the alkaline detergent does nothing to mineral scale. Soak it in acid instead, which is the only thing that dissolves the deposit.

**Why does the scale come back so fast?**

Because the surface has become rough. Once a finish is pitted or etched, deposit bonds to it faster than to a smooth surface. Towel-drying after each use is the single most effective prevention, and a water softener is the only actual fix for hard water.

**When should I replace a fixture instead of cleaning it?**

When the plating is pitting or flaking, when there is green corrosion at the joints, or when a full soak only buys a week. An aerator is a few dollars and a showerhead is under $60 — both screw on by hand, so replacement is cheaper than continued descaling.

---

## Bottom Line

Scale is a mineral, so dissolve it rather than scrub it. Soak aerators and showerheads in vinegar for 30 to 60 minutes, or in citric acid solution for faster results with less risk to the finish. Never use acid toilet cleaner on chrome and never soak plated parts overnight. Then dry the fixture after each use, because prevention is thirty seconds and descaling is an afternoon.
