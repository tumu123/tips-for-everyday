Nothing about leaving a house is dangerous. The danger is entirely in the **duration**  a dripping supply line that would be a ten-minute mop-up on a normal Tuesday becomes a $20,000 insurance claim by day six, because nobody is there to notice.

That framing changes the checklist. You are not preparing for departure; you are preparing for ten days of an empty house with nobody watching. Four systems fail in that window, and each has a fix that takes minutes: **water, power, air, and access.**

## Step 1: Deal with water first, because it is the expensive one

Water damage is the most common cause of loss in an unattended house, and it is almost always a supply line, not a flood. A failed braided hose under a sink runs at 4 to 8 gallons a minute. In a week, that is not a repair  it is a rebuild.

Two options, chosen by how long you are gone and what is in the house:

**Shut the main off.** For any trip over about four days, this is the right answer. Then open the lowest faucet in the house to relieve pressure, and flush a toilet once to confirm the line is empty. Two exceptions to plan around: an ice maker or a fridge water dispenser will not refill, and an irrigation system needs its own valve. Both are acceptable losses.

**Leave it on with detection.** If a shutoff is impractical  a shared building, a sprinkler system, a house with a boiler  install a leak detector with an automatic shutoff valve. A $150 device with sensors under the water heater and the washing machine closes the main when it senses water. It is the only way to get the benefit of a shutoff without turning anything off.

Either way: **inspect the supply lines before you go.** Braided hoses under sinks and behind the washing machine have a service life of about five years, and a bulge or a rust-colored stain at the fitting means replace it now, not after the trip.

## Step 2: Set the temperature, and do not turn it off

The instinct to shut off heating and cooling for a week is expensive in both directions.

**In winter:** never below 55�F (13�C). Pipes in exterior walls freeze well before the indoor air reaches 32�F, because the wall cavity runs colder than the room. A 55�F setpoint costs a few dollars a day and prevents a burst line. Open the cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls before you leave.

**In summer:** never fully off. Above roughly 60% relative humidity, mold grows on drywall, leather, and fabric  and a closed house in a humid climate reaches that within days. Set the AC to 80 to 85�F (27 to 29�C) and leave the fan on auto. That keeps humidity controlled at a fraction of the cost of cooling to 72�F.

**Either season:** set the water heater to its "vacation" or lowest setting if it has one. It is the second-largest energy user in most homes, and nobody is using hot water.

## Step 3: Empty the fridge of anything that will not survive

A week is long enough for produce to liquefy and for the smell to become a problem that outlasts the trip.

- **Clear out perishables:** milk, meat, cut produce, leftovers, anything opened more than a few days ago.
- **Check the crisper drawers**  this is where things are forgotten and where the smell usually originates.
- **Take the trash out**, including the bathroom bins and anything in the garage. A week of kitchen scraps in a closed bin is a pest invitation.
- **Run the dishwasher** before you leave, and leave it empty and cracked open.
- **If you will be gone more than three weeks:** empty the fridge entirely, unplug it, clean it, and prop the door open. A closed, unplugged fridge grows mold in a week.

## Step 4: Kill the smell sources before they start

Most "empty house smell" is not dust. It is three specific things:

- **The garbage disposal.** Run it with a handful of ice and half a lemon, then flush with hot water. Week-old disposal residue is the strongest odor in most homes.
- **Damp laundry.** A load left in the washer for a week requires a hot wash with bleach or vinegar to recover, and sometimes does not recover at all. Run a load the morning you leave, or leave the drum empty and the door open.
- **Dry drain traps.** Floor drains in basements and laundry rooms evaporate over a week or two, and the trap stops blocking sewer gas. Pour a tablespoon of cooking oil into each floor drain  it floats on the water and slows evaporation for weeks.

Also empty the kitchen compost, wipe the sink, and take out recycling. Paper and glass do not smell; the residue on them does.

## Step 5: Set up light and access so the house looks occupied

A dark house for a week is the clearest possible signal. The goal is not a fortress; it is the appearance of ordinary life.

- **Two or three plug-in timers**, on different schedules: one living room lamp, one bedroom lamp, one porch light. Random or slightly varied times beat a single on-at-6-off-at-11 timer.
- **Stop the mail and the newspaper.** A full mailbox is the second-clear signal. A hold request takes two minutes online.
- **Ask one neighbor** to take in packages and to know you are away  not to check the house daily, just to notice.
- **Leave a car in the driveway** if you have two, or ask the neighbor to park there once.
- **Do not post the trip on social media** until you are back. This is the single most common self-inflicted burglary risk, and it is entirely avoidable.
- **Lock every window**, including the ones you think are painted shut, and confirm the garage door closes and stays down.

## Step 6: Reduce the electrical surface area

You are not trying to save standby power; you are trying to reduce what a surge can destroy and what a fault can start.

- **Unplug** the TV, computers, modem, printer, and anything with a wall wart. A nearby lightning strike travels through the panel and takes out whatever is connected.
- **Leave the fridge, freezer, furnace, and security system plugged in.**
- **Turn off the water heater** at the breaker if it is electric and you have shut the water off. A heating element with no water in the tank burns out in seconds.
- **Check that nothing is left on**  a curling iron, a space heater, a soldering iron. This is the fire risk that actually happens.

## Step 7: Handle the outdoor items

- **Move patio furniture and grills** against the house or into the garage if wind is forecast. A week of wind on a loose cover damages more than the cover is worth.
- **Disconnect hoses** from spigots in freezing weather. A connected hose traps water in the faucet body and splits it.
- **Set the irrigation** to a schedule appropriate to the season, or off if rain is forecast  but do not shut off the main valve that feeds it unless you have drained the system.
- **Check the sump pump** if you have one, and confirm the discharge line is clear and pointing away from the foundation.

## Step 8: Know what your insurance requires

This is the step almost nobody checks, and it can void a claim.

- **Vacancy clauses.** Most homeowner policies define "vacant" as unoccupied for 30 or 60 consecutive days, and coverage drops sharply after that. A one-week trip is fine; a two-month absence may not be.
- **Some policies require weekly checks** by someone for extended absences, or specify that the water must be shut off. Read the clause before you leave, not after a loss.
- **Renters' policies** have similar language and usually require the same water precautions.
- **If you rent out short-term**, the policy may not cover a rental period at all without an endorsement.

## Step 9: Do the ten-minute final walk-through

Do this last, in this order, with the door open:

1. Water off (or confirmed on with detectors armed).
2. Every faucet closed, no drips.
3. Stove and oven off  check, do not assume.
4. All windows latched, doors locked.
5. Trash and compost out.
6. Thermostat set (55�F winter, 80 to 85�F summer).
7. Lights on timers, all others off.
8. Garage door down and confirmed by looking at it.
9. Nothing unplugged that should be on  fridge, freezer, sump pump.
10. Take a photo of the thermostat and the water valve. It settles arguments later.

## Step 10: On return, check in the right order

Coming home to a problem is much better if you check the right things first:

- **Smell at the door before opening it.** A musty or chemical smell means do not go in  call someone.
- **Look at the floor near the water heater, washing machine, and under sinks** before turning anything on.
- **Turn the water main on slowly**, then listen. A running sound with no faucet open means a leak.
- **Check the fridge temperature** before assuming the food is fine.
- **Run the hot water** at each fixture to clear any sediment from a sitting water heater.

## Why This Works

Every item on this list maps to one of four failure modes that only appear with time: water escaping unnoticed, temperature drifting out of range, organic material sitting, or a house that visibly stops being lived in. None of them are problems during a day out; all of them become problems by day seven.

The checklist also separates what should be turned off from what must stay on. That distinction is where most vacation damage originates  people shut off the heat to save money and freeze a pipe, or shut off the water and leave an electric water heater powered.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Should you turn off the water when leaving for a week?**

Yes, for any trip longer than about four days. Shut the main, open the lowest faucet to relieve pressure, and flush a toilet once to confirm. If a shutoff is impractical, install a leak detector with an automatic shutoff valve instead. Water damage is the most common and most expensive loss in an unattended house.

**What temperature should you leave the house at in winter?**

55�F (13�C) minimum, and no lower regardless of how well insulated the house is. Pipes in exterior wall cavities run colder than the indoor air. Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, and never shut the heat off completely.

**Should you turn off the AC when on vacation?**

Turn it up, not off. Set 80 to 85�F (27 to 29�C) with the fan on auto. A closed house in a humid climate passes 60% relative humidity quickly, which is where mold starts on drywall and fabric. Cooling to 85�F controls humidity at a fraction of the cost of 72�F.

**How do you keep a house from smelling musty while away?**

Remove the sources: empty the fridge of perishables, take out all trash including bathroom bins, run the disposal with ice and lemon, leave the washer empty and open, and pour a tablespoon of oil into floor drains so the trap does not evaporate. Set the AC to control humidity rather than shutting it off.

**How long can you leave a house before insurance stops covering it?**

Most policies define vacancy at 30 or 60 consecutive days, after which coverage for water damage, vandalism, and sometimes theft is reduced or excluded. Some require weekly checks or a shutoff valve for extended absences. Read your specific clause before a long trip.

**What should you unplug before a vacation?**

Televisions, computers, routers, printers, and anything with a small power adapter. Leave the refrigerator, freezer, furnace, sump pump, and security system connected. The goal is to reduce what a surge can destroy, not to chase standby watts.

**How do you make a house look occupied while away?**

Two or three plug-in timers on different schedules, a mail and package hold, one neighbor who knows you are away, and no social media posts until you return. A dark house with a full mailbox is the most reliable signal that nobody is home.

**What should you check first when you get home?**

Smell at the door before entering, then look for water near the water heater, washing machine, and under sinks. Turn the water main on slowly and listen for running water with no faucet open. Check the fridge temperature before trusting the food, and run hot water at each fixture to clear sediment.
