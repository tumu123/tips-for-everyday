Cooking temperatures are the one part of cooking where "close enough" is a real risk rather than a matter of taste. The numbers below come from the USDA and FDA food code, and they are not arbitrary: each one corresponds to a specific reduction in a specific organism, at a specific time.

Two things trip people up. First, there are three different standards — poultry, ground meat, and whole muscle — and they are not interchangeable. Second, most people's thermometers are wrong by enough to matter, which makes the numbers academic. And temperature is only the last step - [how long food spent below it](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/temperature-danger-zone/) is the other half of the safety question.

## The Table

| Food | Internal temperature | Rest time |
|---|---|---|
| Poultry — whole bird, parts, ground turkey or chicken | 165°F / 74°C | None required |
| Ground meat — beef, pork, lamb, veal | 160°F / 71°C | None required |
| Whole cuts — beef, pork, lamb, veal (steaks, chops, roasts) | 145°F / 63°C | 3 minutes |
| Fish and shellfish | 145°F / 63°C | None required |
| Egg dishes | 160°F / 71°C | None required |
| Leftovers and casseroles (reheated) | 165°F / 74°C | None required |
| Fresh ham, raw | 145°F / 63°C | 3 minutes |
| Fully cooked ham (reheated) | 165°F / 74°C | None required |

A few notes on that table before the reasoning.

The rest time is part of the standard, not a cooking tip. For whole cuts, 145°F held for three minutes achieves the same lethality as a higher temperature, and the meat continues to rise five to ten degrees off the heat, so pulling at 145°F gives a final 150 to 152°F after resting.

Ground poultry is 165°F, not 160°F, even though ground beef is 160°F. This is the single most common error in the table.

## Why the Three Standards Differ

The difference is about where the bacteria are, not how dangerous the meat is.

On a whole cut of beef or pork, contamination sits on the surface. Searing the outside to 145°F kills what is there, and the interior was sterile to begin with — which is why a steak can be served medium-rare safely and a burger cannot. Grinding moves the surface into the middle, distributing it through the meat, so ground beef has to reach 160°F throughout.

Poultry carries *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter* on the surface and, in the case of whole birds, potentially in the muscle tissue and inside the cavity. There is no "safe" rare chicken, and 165°F is the temperature at which the reduction happens essentially instantly.

This is also why 165°F is conservative rather than magical. Lethality is a function of temperature *and* time — poultry held at 150°F for three minutes reaches the same reduction as 165°F in under a second. Commercial sous vide charts work exactly this way. For home cooking, 165°F is simply the number that requires no timing, which is why the food code uses it.

## The Time-Temperature Table Behind the Numbers

Lethality is not a threshold, it is a curve, and the curve is the reason restaurants can serve chicken at 150°F while a home cook is told 165°F.

For poultry, the time needed to achieve the standard seven-log reduction of *Salmonella* looks like this:

| Holding temperature | Time required |
|---|---|
| 145°F / 63°C | 9.8 minutes |
| 150°F / 66°C | 3.7 minutes |
| 155°F / 68°C | 1.2 minutes |
| 160°F / 71°C | 30 seconds |
| 165°F / 74°C | Instantaneous |

Notice that 165°F is not twenty degrees safer than 145°F. It is the temperature at which the required reduction happens faster than the time it takes to read the thermometer, which is why it is the only number that works without a stopwatch. Any temperature above 145°F is safe if held long enough — the sous vide method exists entirely on this principle.

For home cooking, two practical consequences. First, a chicken breast that reads 150°F and rests for five minutes off the heat is both safe and considerably juicier than one pushed to 165°F in the pan. Second, a chicken that reads 165°F and is carved immediately is safe but dry, because the fibres have contracted past the point of holding moisture.

The rest time is doing two jobs at once: it lets the temperature continue to rise through carryover, and it lets the muscle fibres relax so the juices stay in the meat rather than on the board.

## Stuffing, and Why It Is a Special Case

Stuffing cooked inside a bird is the most reliable way to make a household ill, and the reason is geometry. The stuffing is in the centre of the coldest, densest part of the cavity, and heat has to pass through several inches of meat to reach it. By the time the stuffing reaches 165°F, the breast is at 180°F and dry.

Two rules:

**If you stuff, the stuffing must read 165°F in the centre** — not the bird. Measuring the thigh tells you nothing about the cavity.

**Better, do not stuff.** Cook the stuffing in a separate dish to 165°F, and put only aromatics — onion, lemon, herbs — inside the bird. It takes twenty minutes off the roasting time as well, because an empty cavity heats faster.

The same logic applies to anything stuffed: pork chops with a bread filling, chicken breasts rolled around cheese, a turkey breast roulade. The filling is the last thing to reach temperature, so it is the thing to measure.

## The Numbers People Get Wrong

**Pork is not 160°F.** It was, until 2011, when the USDA lowered the recommendation to 145°F with a three-minute rest. Trichinosis in commercial pork is now vanishingly rare, and 145°F produces a pork chop that is pink in the middle and actually edible. Anyone still cooking pork to 160°F is producing a dry chop for no safety benefit.

**Fish is 145°F, but most fish is better at 125–135°F.** The food code number is a safety floor. Salmon at 145°F is opaque and flaky and, to most people, overcooked. If the fish is fresh and from a reputable source, 125°F for tuna and 130°F for salmon is a quality decision made knowingly.

**Egg dishes are 160°F, and that includes quiche.** A quiche pulled at 160°F in the centre will set as it cools. Pulling it when the centre is still loose means the custard was never at temperature.

**Reheated leftovers are 165°F, and this is about more than the original cook.** It is a kill step for anything that grew during storage, not a re-verification of the first cooking. The storage windows themselves are in [the shelf-life guide](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/how-long-food-lasts-shelf-life-guide/).

**Whole birds need the thigh, not the breast.** The breast reaches 165°F twenty to thirty minutes before the thigh does, because they are different muscles at different depths. Measure the thigh at its thickest point, away from the bone. If you measure the breast and pull the bird, the thighs are still at 150°F.

## How to Measure It Properly

The thermometer matters more than the recipe. Three things go wrong.

**Placement.** The probe goes into the thickest part, and it must not touch bone, fat, or the pan. Bone conducts heat differently and reads high; fat renders and reads low. In a whole chicken, that means the thigh, angled in from the side, avoiding the bone. In a burger, the centre from the side, not from the top.

**Wrong thermometer.** A dial thermometer takes fifteen to thirty seconds to settle and is typically accurate to ±5°F. An instant-read digital is accurate to ±1 to 2°F and settles in two to three seconds. The difference between 160°F and 165°F is exactly the error band of a cheap dial thermometer, which means it cannot tell you whether chicken is done.

**Calibration.** Ice water should read 32°F. If it reads 38°F, everything you cook is coming out six degrees under. Most digital thermometers have a calibration screw or a reset procedure; check it twice a year, and any time the thermometer has been dropped.

One more: measure before the food comes off the heat and again after resting. The second reading is the one that matters for doneness, and the difference tells you how fast your cooking environment is transferring heat.

## Why "Until the Juices Run Clear" Fails

The old test — pierce the meat and look for clear juices — is not wrong so much as early. Chicken juices run clear at around 150°F, which is fifteen degrees below the number the food code wants without a timed hold. Pork juices run clear at 140°F, five below its target.

The test also fails in the other direction: dark meat can release pinkish juices at 175°F because of myoglobin, not blood, which sends people back to the oven to overcook an already-done piece. Both errors come from the same place — the test is measuring colour, and colour is a function of myoglobin chemistry, pH, and age, not temperature.

The same applies to the "palm test" for steak doneness, which correlates loosely with firmness and badly with actual temperature, and to time-per-pound charts, which assume a shape and a starting temperature that your roast may not have. A 4-pound roast at room temperature and the same roast from the fridge differ by twenty minutes.

A $12 instant-read thermometer is the entire solution. There is no technique that substitutes for it.

## Temperatures Beyond Meat

The same logic applies to anything where "done" is a physical state rather than a time.

| Item | Target internal | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Yeast bread | 190–210°F | Gluten set, structure fixed |
| Custard, crème brûlée | 170–180°F | Proteins set, no curdling |
| Cheesecake | 150°F at the edge, 145°F centre | Set but still creamy |
| Custard-based ice cream base | 170°F | Egg yolks pasteurised and thickened |
| Roasted vegetables | 190–205°F | Starches gelatinised |
| Reheated rice | 165°F | *Bacillus cereus* kill step |

Bread at 190°F versus 210°F is the difference between a gummy crumb and a dry one, and it is far more reliable than tapping the bottom for a hollow sound. If the loaf is still gummy at temperature, [the baking diagnosis chart](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/why-baking-failed/) covers the other variables.

## What Temperature Does Not Tell You

A thermometer verifies the kill step. It does not fix anything that happened before.

It cannot detect *Staphylococcus* toxin, which is heat stable and survives reheating — the reason food left out for six hours is not made safe by cooking it again. It cannot tell you whether the meat was handled with clean hands, and [thawing](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/thaw-food-safely/) is where most of that handling actually happens. And it will not help if the food sat in the danger zone long enough for bacteria to multiply to a level where the toxins, not the organisms, are the problem.

Temperature is the last line, not the only one.

## FAQ

**What temperature is chicken done?**

165°F in the thickest part of the thigh, away from the bone. Ground chicken and turkey are also 165°F. There is no safe rare chicken.

**Is 145°F pork safe?**

Yes, with a three-minute rest. The USDA lowered the recommendation from 160°F in 2011. A pork chop at 145°F is slightly pink and safe; at 160°F it is dry.

**Why is ground beef 160°F but steak 145°F?**

Grinding distributes surface bacteria throughout the meat. A steak's interior was sterile; a burger's interior is not.

**Do I really need to let meat rest?**

For whole cuts, yes — the three-minute rest is part of the safety standard, and it also lets juices redistribute. Poultry and ground meat have no rest requirement in the food code, though resting poultry ten minutes improves the texture.

**My thermometer reads 32°F in ice water but 5 degrees off in boiling water. Which do I trust?**

Ice water. Boiling point varies with altitude and atmospheric pressure, so 212°F is not a reliable reference unless you correct for elevation. Ice water at 32°F is stable everywhere.

**Can I cook chicken to 150°F and hold it?**

Yes, in principle — 150°F held for about three minutes reaches the same lethality as 165°F instantaneously. It requires a thermometer you trust and a timer, which is why the food code uses 165°F for home cooks.

**What temperature should leftovers reach when reheated?**

165°F throughout, measured in the centre of the thickest part. This applies to casseroles, rice, and anything that was cooked once and stored.

---

## Bottom Line

Poultry to 165°F, ground meat to 160°F, whole cuts to 145°F with a three-minute rest, fish to 145°F if you want the code number and less if you know what you are doing. Measure the thickest part, avoid bone, use a digital instant-read, and check it against ice water twice a year. The rest of the skill is not memorising numbers — it is having a thermometer that tells the truth.
