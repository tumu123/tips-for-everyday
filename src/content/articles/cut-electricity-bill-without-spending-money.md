Most electricity-saving guides open with "install solar panels" or "buy a smart thermostat." That is backwards. The largest and cheapest savings usually come from **changing how you use what you already own** — no purchase required.

**The highest-impact actions, ranked by typical dollar savings:**

1. Adjust your water heater temperature (5 minutes, saves $30–60/year)
2. Address air leaks around doors and windows (free if you have a towel, saves $50–120/year)
3. Shift heavy appliance use to off-peak hours (if your utility offers time-of-use rates)
4. Reduce dryer use by air-drying (saves $60–140/year in a typical home)

The rest of this guide covers all 15, with the reasoning behind each so you can judge which apply to your home.

**Important caveat:** Actual savings vary substantially by climate, home size, utility rates, and household habits. The figures below are typical U.S. ranges and should be treated as directional, not guaranteed.

## 1. Lower Your Water Heater to 120°F

**Typical savings: $30–60/year | Cost: $0 | Effort: 5 minutes**

Water heating is the second-largest energy expense in most U.S. homes, after space heating and cooling. Most water heaters ship set to 140°F (60°C), which is hotter than necessary for household use.

1. Find the thermostat dial on the water heater tank (gas) or behind the access panel (electric).
2. Set it to **120°F (49°C)**.
3. For electric water heaters, turn off the breaker first — the upper element is live.
4. Mark the original setting so you can restore it if needed.

**Safety note:** If anyone in the household has a compromised immune system, consult a doctor before lowering below 140°F. Also note that 120°F reduces but does not eliminate scald risk for young children.

## 2. Seal Air Leaks Around Doors and Windows

**Typical savings: $50–120/year | Cost: $0–20**

Air leaks force your heating and cooling system to work harder. The largest leak points are usually invisible:

| Location | Free fix |
|---|---|
| Gap under exterior doors | Roll a towel into a snake; test with a dollar bill — if it slides out easily, air is escaping |
| Window frames | Press plastic wrap or a damp towel against the gap as a temporary seal |
| Electrical outlets on exterior walls | Insert child-safety plugs |
| Mail slots, pet doors, fireplace dampers | Close when not in use |

**Free test:** On a windy day, hold a lit incense stick or a piece of tissue near suspected gaps. A flickering flame reveals airflow. This takes three minutes and confirms exactly where to focus.

## 3. Shift Laundry and Dishwashing to Off-Peak Hours

**Typical savings: $20–80/year (only on time-of-use rate plans)**

Many utilities charge different rates by time of day. Peak hours (typically 4 p.m.–9 p.m. on weekdays) can cost 2–3 times the off-peak rate.

1. Check your utility bill or website for a time-of-use (TOU) rate schedule.
2. Identify peak and off-peak windows.
3. Run the dishwasher, washing machine, and dryer outside peak hours — morning or late evening.
4. Use the delay-start function if your appliances have one (costs nothing to use).

If your utility charges a flat rate, this tip does not apply and you can skip it.

## 4. Air-Dry Laundry Instead of Using the Dryer

**Typical savings: $60–140/year | Cost: $0**

The dryer is one of the most energy-intensive appliances in the home. Air-drying even half your loads produces meaningful savings.

1. Hang clothes on a drying rack, shower rod, or outdoor line.
2. Best candidates for air-drying: shirts, dresses, delicates, and anything that benefits from not shrinking.
3. Keep using the dryer for towels and bedding if you prefer, but shorten the cycle using the moisture sensor instead of a fixed timer.

**Bonus:** Air-drying extends the life of fabrics. The dryer's lint trap is mostly fibers worn off your clothes — every load you air-dry is fabric you keep.

## 5. Clean Your Refrigerator Coils and Seals

**Typical savings: $10–30/year | Cost: $0**

A refrigerator with dusty coils and a leaky door seal runs longer to maintain temperature.

1. Unplug the refrigerator or turn off its breaker.
2. Pull it away from the wall.
3. Vacuum the coils at the back or bottom (a coil brush helps but is optional).
4. Check the door seal: close the door on a dollar bill. If you can pull it out with no resistance, the seal is loose.
5. Clean the seal with warm soapy water — dirt prevents a tight seal.
6. If the seal is visibly cracked or permanently deformed, it needs replacement (a low-cost part).

## 6. Use Cold Water for Washing Clothes

**Typical savings: $30–60/year | Cost: $0**

About 75–90% of a washing machine's energy use goes to heating water. Modern detergents are formulated to work in cold water.

1. Set the default wash temperature to cold.
2. Reserve warm or hot water for heavily soiled items, bedding during illness, and cloth diapers.
3. If your machine has an "eco" or "cold wash" setting, use it.

If you have not tried cold washing because of concerns about cleaning power: modern enzyme-based detergents are specifically designed for cold water and perform comparably for normal soil levels.

## 7. Turn Off Lights You Are Not Using

**Typical savings: $10–40/year | Cost: $0**

The savings from turning off lights are often overstated because modern LEDs use very little power. Still, the habit is free and it adds up over a year — especially with multiple rooms, outdoor fixtures, and garage or basement lights left on habitually.

The practical version: **focus on the lights that stay on for hours** (garage, basement, porch, home office during the day), not on bathroom lights you use for two minutes.

## 8. Unplug Devices That Draw Standby Power

**Typical savings: $20–50/year | Cost: $0**

Some electronics continue drawing power when "off" — game consoles, cable boxes, desktop computers, and TV streaming devices are the biggest offenders. Chargers left plugged in with no device attached draw negligible power in modern designs, so prioritize the big items.

**How to find them:** Touch the power adapter. If it is warm when the device is off, it is drawing power.

**Practical approach:** Put entertainment centers and computer setups on a power strip you switch off at night. Do not put DVRs or anything with a scheduled recording or update cycle on a switched strip.

## 9. Close Doors and Vents in Unused Rooms

**Typical savings: Variable | Cost: $0**

Shutting registers in an unused room reduces the space your HVAC system must condition. This is most effective in rooms that are both unused and closed off.

**Important nuance:** Closing too many vents in a forced-air system can increase pressure in the ductwork and reduce overall system efficiency, potentially causing damage. Limit this to one or two rooms, and do not close more than about 20% of your total vents.

## 10. Use Ceiling Fans Correctly

**Typical savings: Up to 10% on cooling | Cost: $0**

Ceiling fans do not lower air temperature — they move air across your skin, which makes you feel cooler. This means **fans only save energy if you raise the thermostat as a result.**

- **Summer:** Fan blades should spin counterclockwise (viewed from below), pushing air down.
- **Winter:** Reverse to clockwise at low speed to push warm air down from the ceiling.
- **Always turn fans off when you leave a room.** A fan running in an empty room is pure waste.

The real saving comes from setting the thermostat 3–4°F higher and using fans to maintain comfort.

## 11. Run the Dishwasher Only When Full

**Typical savings: $20–40/year | Cost: $0**

A dishwasher uses roughly the same amount of water and energy regardless of how full it is. Running full loads cuts both the number of cycles and total consumption.

- Scrape dishes instead of pre-rinsing. Pre-rinsing under hot water wastes more energy than the dishwasher saves.
- Use the air-dry or eco setting instead of heated drying.
- Skip the "rinse hold" feature unless dishes will sit for more than a day.

Counterintuitively, a full dishwasher uses less water than hand-washing the same dishes.

## 12. Set Back Your Thermostat While Away or Asleep

**Typical savings: 5–15% on heating/cooling | Cost: $0**

The savings from lowering your thermostat for 8 hours a day are well documented — the U.S. Department of Energy estimates roughly 1% savings on heating bills for each degree of setback over 8 hours.

**Without a programmable thermostat:**
- Set it manually before bed and before leaving for work.
- 68°F (20°C) when awake, lower at night and when away (heating season).
- 78°F (26°C) when home, higher when away (cooling season).

**Myth to discard:** Many people believe it takes more energy to reheat a house than to maintain temperature. This is false for typical homes — the heat lost while away is always greater than the energy used to recover, so setback always saves.

## 13. Keep Your HVAC Filter Clean

**Typical savings: 5–15% on system efficiency | Cost: $0 if washable, low if disposable**

A dirty filter restricts airflow, forcing the blower to work harder and reducing system efficiency.

1. Check the filter monthly during heavy-use seasons.
2. Replace disposable filters every 1–3 months (more often with pets or during pollen season).
3. If you have a washable filter, rinse and dry it monthly.

This is arguably the highest-value free maintenance task in a home — it reduces energy use and extends equipment life.

## 14. Cook More Efficiently

**Typical savings: $20–50/year | Cost: $0**

- **Use the microwave** for small portions — it uses roughly a third to half the energy of a stovetop for heating small amounts.
- **Match the pan to the burner** — an 8-inch pan on a 6-inch burner wastes heat.
- **Keep lids on pots** — covered pots boil faster and use less energy.
- **Turn off the oven a few minutes early** and use residual heat to finish cooking.
- **Skip preheating** for anything that cooks more than an hour, and for most frozen foods.
- **Use the toaster oven or air fryer** instead of a full-size oven for small items — a large oven heats a lot of empty air.

## 15. Monitor Your Usage (The Meta-Habit)

**Typical savings: 5–10% from awareness alone | Cost: $0**

Studies consistently show that simply showing people their energy use reduces consumption. You do not need a smart meter to do this.

1. Read your electricity meter on the same day each week at the same time.
2. Write down the reading.
3. Compare week to week and note what changed (weather, guests, new appliance).

This takes two minutes weekly and makes the impact of every other tip visible, which is what sustains the habits.

## Why This Approach Works

The logic behind ranking by cost-effectiveness rather than by novelty:

| Action type | Average payback | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Behavior change | Immediate | Zero investment, compounding |
| Free physical fixes (seals, filters) | Immediate | Low effort, recurring benefit |
| Small purchases (LED bulbs, low-flow heads) | 3–12 months | Low cost, long life |
| Equipment upgrade (appliances) | 5–15 years | High cost, slow payback |
| Major systems (solar, HVAC replacement) | 10–25 years | Very high cost, financing often required |

The common mistake is starting at the bottom of this table. Starting at the top typically yields the first $200–400 of annual savings at no cost, which then funds the later investments.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Closing most vents in the house | Over-pressurizes ductwork, can damage the system, usually increases energy use overall. |
| Believing a running ceiling fan cools the room | Fans cool people, not air. A fan in an empty room wastes energy. |
| Using "space heater for one room" as a general strategy | Space heaters are efficient for heating one occupied room but very expensive if used to heat multiple rooms. |
| Relying on the "vampire power" savings from unplugging phone chargers | Modern chargers draw near-zero standby power. Focus on cable boxes and consoles. |
| Setting the thermostat to a very high temperature to "cool faster" | AC systems cool at a constant rate; this only overheats the house and wastes energy. |
| Assuming a smart thermostat pays for itself automatically | Savings come from correct scheduling and setbacks, not from the device itself. |
| Ignoring the water heater | It is the second-largest energy expense in most homes and the easiest to adjust. |

## FAQ

**What is the single fastest way to lower my electric bill?**
Lower your water heater setting to 120°F and seal the gap under your exterior doors. Both take minutes, cost nothing, and typically save $80–180/year combined.

**Do LED bulbs actually save money?**
Yes — an LED uses roughly 75–85% less energy than an equivalent incandescent and lasts far longer, so the bulb pays for itself. But since lighting is a small share of most bills, the savings are modest compared with water heating and air sealing.

**Is it cheaper to leave the AC on all day or turn it off?**
Turning it off while away is cheaper in nearly all cases. The idea that restarting costs more is false for typical residential systems.

**How much does a clothes dryer cost to run?**
A typical electric dryer costs roughly $0.30–0.60 per load depending on local rates, which works out to $60–140 per year for average use. Air-drying even half your loads captures a significant portion of that.

**Does unplugging things really matter?**
For most chargers and small electronics, no. For cable boxes, game consoles, and desktop computers, yes — these can draw 10–30 watts continuously, which adds up to $20–50 per year.

**Will lowering the thermostat at night really save money?**
Yes. The Department of Energy estimates about 1% savings on heating per degree of setback over an 8-hour period. A 5-degree night setback is roughly 5% savings.

**Should I switch to a time-of-use rate plan to save money?**
Only if your household can genuinely shift heavy usage (laundry, dishwashing, EV charging) outside peak hours. If you cannot, a TOU plan can cost more than a flat rate.

**Are energy-saving devices like "power savers" worth buying?**
No. Plug-in power-saving devices marketed to consumers do not meaningfully reduce consumption in a normal home. The savings come from behavior and maintenance, not gadgets.
