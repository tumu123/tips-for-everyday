The date on the package tells you what the manufacturer estimated. Your senses tell you what is actually in front of you. For most foods, the senses are more reliable.

**The core principle:** spoilage bacteria — the ones that make food smell and taste wrong — are usually **not** the ones that make you sick. This is both good and bad news:

- **Good:** if food smells or tastes clearly off, spoilage bacteria have already flagged it. You will not eat it by mistake.
- **Bad:** the pathogens that cause most food poisoning (Salmonella, E. coli, Listeria) often produce **no smell, no taste, and no visible change.** You cannot detect them by inspection.

That means inspection tells you when food is **spoiled**, but never proves food is **safe**. Time and temperature rules cover that second job, and they are not negotiable.

---

## Step 1: Run the four checks in order

Look first, then smell, then touch, then — only if the first three pass — taste. Never taste something that fails an earlier check.

### Check 1: Look

| What you see | What it means |
|---|---|
| Green, blue, white fuzzy patches | Mold — see the mold rule below |
| Slimy or tacky surface film | Bacterial growth, discard |
| Cloudy liquid where it should be clear | Bacterial growth |
| Swollen, bulging, or leaking can | Bacterial gas production, discard immediately without tasting |
| Discoloration beyond normal | Oxidation or spoilage |
| Liquid pooling or separating | Breakdown, judge by smell next |
| Crystals on cheese or cured meat | Usually harmless — salt or amino acid crystals, taste is fine |

### Check 2: Smell

This is the most useful single test. Spoilage bacteria produce volatile compounds that your nose detects at very low concentrations.

- **Sour, sharp, or tangy** where it should not be — dairy, meat, cooked grains
- **Rotten, sulfurous, or like garbage** — protein breakdown
- **Yeasty, alcoholic, or fermented** — sugars being consumed
- **Rancid, like old crayons or paint** — fat oxidation; common in nuts, oils, whole wheat flour, and old crackers
- **Ammonia** — spoiled fish and old meat

**Important:** a food that smells fine is not proven safe. Absence of smell only clears the spoilage-bacteria check.

### Check 3: Touch

- **Slimy or sticky** on meat, fish, or cooked vegetables — discard
- **Mushy** beyond the food's normal texture — breakdown, use immediately or discard
- **Fuzzy or hairy** — mold

### Check 4: Taste (only if the first three passed)

Take a tiny amount, and only for foods where the smell test is inconclusive. If it tastes off, spit it out and discard the rest. Note that tasting is a confirmation step, never a discovery step — if you are unsure enough to be tasting, the answer is usually no.

## Step 2: Apply the mold rule

Mold is the most common judgment call, and there is a clear rule.

**Mold has roots (hyphae) that extend invisibly beyond the visible growth.** Whether you can cut it off depends on the food's density and moisture.

### Mold: cut it off (hard, firm foods)

- Hard cheese (Cheddar, Parmesan, Gouda)
- Firm vegetables (carrots, bell peppers, cabbage)
- Salami and dry-cured sausage
- Hard-crust bread (judge carefully — see below)

**How to cut:** remove at least **1 inch (2.5 cm) around and below** the visible mold, and keep the knife out of the mold itself so you do not spread spores.

### Mold: discard the whole thing (soft or liquid foods)

- Soft cheese (cottage, cream, Brie, blue-adjacent soft styles)
- Yogurt, sour cream, milk
- Jam, jelly, preserves
- Bread (the whole loaf — soft and porous)
- Cooked leftovers
- Juices and sauces
- Soft fruit and vegetables

**Why bread is always discarded:** bread is porous, so mold roots spread through the interior long before you see them. Cutting off the visible patch does not remove the mold.

**The one exception to "discard" that people get wrong:** blue cheese, Brie, Camembert, and Gorgonzola are made with specific mold cultures. That mold is intentional. If you see a **different** mold — green, black, or fuzzy white — on a blue cheese, that is contamination and the cheese should be discarded.

## Step 3: Know the foods where inspection is not enough

These foods can carry pathogens with no warning signs. For them, time and temperature rules replace inspection entirely.

| Food | Risk | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Cooked rice** | Bacillus cereus | Refrigerate within 1 hour, eat within 1 day |
| **Reheated leftovers** | General | Reheat to 165°F (74°C), once only |
| **Raw sprouts** | Salmonella, E. coli | High risk, cook before eating |
| **Unpasteurized dairy and juice** | Listeria, E. coli, Salmonella | Avoid if pregnant, elderly, or immunocompromised |
| **Raw or undercooked ground meat** | E. coli | Cook to 160°F (71°C) |
| **Raw eggs in uncooked dishes** | Salmonella | Use pasteurized eggs |
| **Cold deli meat, unheated** | Listeria | Heat to steaming if in a risk group |
| **Pre-cut fruit left out** | Bacterial growth on cut surfaces | Refrigerate cut fruit promptly |
| **Leftover food left out >2 hours** | Broad | Discard — one hour if above 90°F (32°C) |

**The two-hour rule** is the single most useful safety rule in a home kitchen, and it exists precisely because you cannot smell these pathogens. Food in the temperature danger zone — **40°F to 140°F (4°C to 60°C)** — for more than two hours should be discarded. That is cumulative time, not a single unbroken stretch.

## Step 4: Learn the specific spoilage signs by food

| Food | Clear discard signs |
|---|---|
| **Milk** | Sour smell, lumps, chunky texture, cloudy separation |
| **Eggs** | Float in water, sulfurous smell when cracked, pink or iridescent white |
| **Raw meat** | Slimy or tacky surface, sour or ammonia smell, gray-green color, sticky feel |
| **Raw chicken** | Gray or greenish tint, slimy, sour smell |
| **Fish** | Ammonia smell, sunken dull eyes, gills brown or gray, flesh dents on press |
| **Bread** | Any mold at all (discard whole loaf), sour smell, stringy interior |
| **Rice (cooked)** | Sour smell, slimy texture, any off odor — never risk it |
| **Potatoes** | Green skin or sprouts (solanine), soft black spots, rotten smell |
| **Nuts and nut butters** | Rancid paint-like smell, bitter taste — discard |
| **Oils** | Rancid smell, sticky texture, off-bottle flavor |
| **Flour** | Musty or sour smell, discoloration, clumping in whole wheat |
| **Honey** | Never spoils; fermentation (foaming, alcohol smell) means contamination |
| **Canned goods** | Bulging can, spurting on open, off smell, cloudy liquid |
| **Fresh berries** | Fuzzy mold, mushy collapse, fermented smell |
| **Leftovers** | Any off smell, slime, or visible growth |

## Step 5: Make the decision in five seconds

When you are standing in front of the fridge holding something uncertain, run this order:

1. **Does it smell wrong?** If yes, discard. Done.
2. **Is it slimy, fuzzy, or discolored beyond normal?** If yes, discard.
3. **Has it been out of the fridge more than two hours, or past a use-by date?** If yes, discard.
4. **Is it a soft food with any mold?** If yes, discard the whole thing.
5. **Is it a hard food with mold?** Cut 1 inch around and below.
6. **Has it been in the fridge longer than the shelf life window?** Check condition; if it passes all above checks, it is likely fine.
7. **Still unsure?** Discard. The cost of a lost portion is smaller than the cost of a foodborne illness.

**When in doubt, throw it out is not a cliché — it is the correct rule of thumb.** Your nose is a genuinely good detector of spoilage, and the intuition that something is wrong is usually based on a real signal.

---

## Why This Works

Two different problems exist, and they require two different tools.

**Spoilage** is visible and smellable. Bacteria that break down food produce volatile organic compounds and physical changes. Your nose detects these at concentrations far below the level that would make you sick, which is why spoilage is largely self-policing.

**Contamination** by pathogens is silent. Salmonella, E. coli, and Listeria do not produce off odors or visible changes in most foods, because they do not need to break down the food to survive. This is the entire reason that the two-hour rule, the temperature danger zone, and cooking temperatures exist — they substitute for senses that cannot detect the threat.

The mold rule follows from biology rather than caution: mold is a fungus with a mycelial network of hyphae. In a firm, dry substrate, the network stays local. In a soft, wet substrate, it spreads throughout the food's interior long before the surface growth is visible.

Understanding which problem you are solving tells you which test to trust.

## What NOT to Do

| Common mistake | Why it's wrong | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Relying on the date alone | Quality dates are estimates, not safety thresholds | Run the look-smell-touch checks |
| Trusting that no smell means safe | Key pathogens produce no odor | Enforce the two-hour and temperature rules |
| Cutting mold off bread | Roots spread through the porous interior | Discard the whole loaf |
| Cutting mold off soft cheese or jam | Hyphae extend invisibly through soft foods | Discard the entire container |
| Tasting food you are unsure about | Adds risk without resolving the question | If unsure after looking and smelling, discard |
| Leaving food out to cool for hours | Spends time in the danger zone | Refrigerate within two hours |
| Refreezing counter-thawed food | Each thaw cycle multiplies risk | Cook it, or freeze only fridge-thawed food |
| Eating leftovers more than 4 days old | Pathogens do not announce themselves | Limit most leftovers to 3–4 days |
| Trusting a bulging can "if it smells fine" | Gas production means bacterial activity | Discard without opening or tasting |
| Keeping cooked rice more than a day | Bacillus cereus survives reheating | Eat within 1 day, reheat only once |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I eat food that smells fine but is past its date?**
Usually yes, if it is a "best by" or "sell by" date and the food passes the look, smell, and touch checks. Past a "use by" date, discard it. The date is information, not the whole answer.

**Is it safe to eat around mold on hard cheese?**
Yes, if you cut at least one inch around and below the mold, and keep the knife away from the mold itself. Hard cheese is dense enough that the mold's roots stay local. This does not apply to soft cheese, bread, jam, or leftovers.

**How can I tell if meat has gone bad?**
Three signals: a slimy or tacky surface, a sour or ammonia-like smell, and a gray-green or dull discoloration. Fresh raw meat should feel slightly moist, not sticky, and should smell like very little at all.

**Does reheating food kill all bacteria?**
It kills most bacteria, but not all toxins they have already produced. Bacillus cereus toxin and Staphylococcus toxin are heat-stable, which is why reheating old rice or a sauce left out too long does not make it safe. Reheating to 165°F (74°C) and reheating only once is the practical rule.

**What is the temperature danger zone?**
40°F to 140°F (4°C to 60°C) — the range where bacteria multiply fastest. Food should spend less than two hours cumulative in this range, or one hour if the room is above 90°F (32°C).

**Why does rice have such a short window?**
Cooked rice can carry Bacillus cereus spores that survive cooking. If rice is left at room temperature, the spores germinate and produce a heat-stable toxin. Cool rice quickly and refrigerate it, then eat within one day.

**Is green potato skin dangerous?**
Yes. Green skin indicates solanine, a toxin produced when potatoes are exposed to light. Cut away all green areas with a wide margin, or discard the potato entirely if the greening is extensive. Solanine is not destroyed by cooking.

**How do I know if eggs have gone bad?**
Crack one into a separate bowl. A rotten egg has an unmistakable sulfurous smell. For whole uncracked eggs, the float test works: fresh eggs sink, older eggs tilt or stand, and a floating egg should be discarded.
