The honest answer: **line drying saves real money, but less than most estimates claim** — and the biggest financial benefit is not the electricity. It's that your clothes wear out more slowly.

That second point is where the actual money is, and it's almost always missing from the comparison.

---

## The Direct Energy Cost

A typical electric dryer draws roughly **3,000 watts** per load. Over a 45-minute cycle that's:

```
3.0 kW × 0.75 hours = 2.25 kWh per load
```

At the US average residential rate, that works out to roughly **$0.30–0.40 per load**, though this varies widely by region — some areas run half that, others double it.

For a household doing **five loads a week** (about 260 loads a year):

| Scenario | Annual energy cost |
|---|---|
| Dryer for every load | ~$80–105 |
| Line dry half the loads | ~$40–55 |
| Line dry everything | ~$0–5 |

**Realistic annual saving: roughly $40–100** for most households. Meaningful, but it's not going to change your finances.

**Gas dryers change the math.** Natural gas costs considerably less per unit of heat delivered than electricity, so a gas dryer might cost $0.15–0.20 per load — halving the savings from line drying.

---

## The Bigger Number: Clothes Last Longer

This is where line drying wins decisively, and it's rarely counted.

Machine drying damages clothing through three mechanisms:

1. **Heat** degrades elastic fibers and sets stains permanently
2. **Tumbling** abrades fabric surface — this is what causes pilling and thinning
3. **Over-drying** makes fibers brittle

Removing or reducing machine drying meaningfully extends garment life. If it extends the life of a wardrobe by even 10–20%, the annualized replacement cost saved typically **exceeds** the electricity saved — and for households with quality clothing, often by several times.

**Where the biggest gains are:**

| Item | Why line drying matters most |
|---|---|
| Elastic (underwear, sportswear) | Heat destroys elasticity fastest |
| Knitwear and sweaters | Tumbling causes pilling and distortion |
| Anything with prints or logos | Heat and abrasion crack and peel them |
| Towels | Tumbling breaks the loop fibers that make them absorbent |

Line drying towels is counterintuitive — they come out stiffer, but the loops survive much longer, so they stay absorbent for years instead of months.

## What Line Drying Actually Costs You

**Time.** Expect roughly 10–15 minutes per load to hang, more if you're dealing with many small items. Over 260 loads a year that's somewhere between 40 and 65 hours. Whether that matters depends entirely on your circumstances.

**Space.** A drying rack occupies real floor area. Hanging lines need anchor points.

**Weather dependence.** Outdoor drying requires dry weather and adds a rescheduling burden.

**Texture.** Air-dried towels and some cottons feel stiffer than machine-dried. A brief tumble on low at the end fixes this.

**A realistic approach:** line dry most items, machine dry towels and anything where texture matters, and use the dryer for the last few minutes when you want softness.

## Making the Dryer Cheaper When You Do Use It

Most comparisons stop at "line dry or don't." But if you're going to run the dryer — and most households will, at least sometimes — these reduce the cost per load substantially.

**Extract more water first.** This is the single biggest lever. Every bit of water you remove in the washer is water the dryer doesn't have to boil off.

| Action | Why it works |
|---|---|
| Use the highest spin speed your fabrics tolerate | Higher spin removes markedly more water |
| Don't overload the washer | Clothes can't move freely, so water isn't extracted |
| Run an extra spin cycle on heavy items | Cheap in electricity, saves a lot of drying time |

High spin speeds remove far more water than low ones, and spinning costs a fraction of what evaporating the same water with heat costs.

**Clean the lint filter every load.** Not every few loads — every load. A partially blocked filter restricts airflow, extends drying time, and wastes energy on every cycle. It's also a genuine fire hazard.

**Clean the vent duct periodically.** Lint accumulates in the ductwork where you can't see it. If drying times have been creeping up, this is usually why. Symptoms of a blocked vent: clothes take much longer than they used to, the top of the dryer gets very hot, or the laundry room feels humid.

**Dry similar fabrics together.** Mixing heavy towels with light shirts means the light items sit in heat long after they're dry. Separate loads by weight and they finish together.

**Don't over-dry.** Modern moisture sensors stop the cycle when clothes are dry. Fixed-time cycles keep heating after the water is gone, wasting energy and making fibers brittle.

**Consider dryer balls.** Wool dryer balls improve air circulation and reduce drying time modestly. They're a one-time purchase that replaces disposable sheets.

**Use the dryer's heat setting deliberately.** High heat is faster but harder on fabric. Medium heat is the usual sensible default — the time penalty is small and the wear reduction is real.

## A Strategy That Fits Your Situation

There is no universal right answer. The right mix depends on your space, your time, and your wardrobe.

| Your situation | Sensible approach |
|---|---|
| Yard, mild dry climate | Line dry outdoors most of the year |
| Apartment, no outdoor space | Folding rack near a window or fan |
| Small home, limited floor space | Wall-mounted retractable line, or over-door rack |
| Humid climate | Indoor drying with air circulation; use the dryer for heavy items |
| Large household, many loads | Line dry most, machine dry towels and bedding |
| Mainly synthetic workwear | Line dry — synthetics dry fast and are heat-sensitive |
| Mainly cotton and denim | Machine dry, or line dry with a short tumble to finish |
| Very limited time | Line dry only what's easy; don't add stress |
| Allergy or pollen concerns | Dry indoors, or use the dryer during high-pollen days |

**A workable default for most households:** line dry shirts, trousers, knitwear, synthetics, and delicates; machine dry towels, bedding, and anything where stiffness matters; and if you want softness, add a five-minute low-heat tumble at the end.

**What matters consistency-wise:** partial line drying captures most of the garment-life benefit. You do not have to be all-or-nothing. Line drying half your loads gets you roughly half the benefit — which is still worth having.

## If You Have No Space for a Rack

This is the most common practical objection, and it's solvable.

**Over-the-door racks** hang on a door frame and fold flat. Good for shirts and delicates, limited weight capacity.

**Wall-mounted retractable lines** extend across a room or bathroom and retract when not in use. Mount over a bathtub or shower for drip containment.

**Ceiling-mounted pulley racks** use vertical space in a laundry area or stairwell. Common in Europe for good reason — warm air rises, which is exactly where you want the laundry.

**Heated drying racks** use a fraction of a dryer's energy and dry reliably in humid weather. Worth considering if you line dry year-round in a damp climate.

**Shower curtain rod** works for hanging items on hangers in the short term.

**The drip question:** indoors, place racks over a tub, or put a towel beneath. In winter, indoor line drying adds useful humidity to dry heated air — a rare case where a side effect is genuinely beneficial.

## Why This Works

**Heat is the primary aging mechanism for textiles.** Elastic fibers in particular lose recovery permanently when exposed to repeated high heat. This is why dryer sheets make clothes feel softer without making them last longer — the softness is a surface effect, not a structural repair.

**Mechanical abrasion compounds heat damage.** Tumbling rubs fabric against itself and the drum. The fuzz that collects in your lint trap is your clothing, coming off. That material is gone permanently.

**Energy cost per load is genuinely small.** Dryers are efficient at what they do. The reason line drying is financially attractive is the garment-life effect, not the kilowatt-hours.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Line drying wool or silk flat-hung wet | Weight stretches the fiber permanently | Roll in a towel first, then dry flat |
| Leaving line-dried items in direct sun too long | Fades dyes, especially darks | Dry darks in shade |
| Over-drying in the machine to save time | Brittle fibers, more wrinkles, wasted energy | Use a moisture sensor or shorter cycle |
| Ignoring the lint filter | Longer dry times and a fire hazard | Clean it every single load |
| Hanging heavy wet items on a weak line | Stretched shoulders and line sag | Support the weight or dry flat |
| Assuming line drying is always free | Racks wear out; time has value | Count both honestly |

## How Much This Saves

| Approach | Energy | Wear on clothes | Time per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dryer only | ~$80–105/yr | Highest | ~5 hours |
| Half and half | ~$40–55/yr | Moderate | ~25 hours |
| Line dry | ~$0–5/yr | Lowest | ~45–65 hours |

Exact figures depend on local energy prices, dryer efficiency, load size, and how much you value your time. The pattern that holds up: the money saved on electricity is modest, and the money saved on clothing is larger.

**The honest framing:** line dry if it fits your routine and you own a rack that suits your space. Don't do it expecting major utility savings — do it because your clothes will last noticeably longer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does one dryer load cost?**
Typically $0.30–0.40 for an electric dryer at average US rates. It varies substantially by region and by whether the dryer is gas or electric.

**Does line drying really save money on clothes?**
Yes, and usually more than it saves on energy. Reduced heat and tumbling noticeably slows pilling, fading, and elastic failure.

**Why do air-dried towels feel stiff?**
Without tumbling, the loop fibers dry in place and bond together. A short low-heat tumble at the end restores softness.

**Can I line dry everything?**
Most things, yes. Wool and silk should not be hung wet because the weight stretches them — dry those flat.

**Is a dryer more expensive than a washer to run?**
Yes, typically several times more, because it applies heat rather than just moving water and drum.

**Does the moisture sensor actually save money?**
Yes. It stops the cycle when clothes are dry instead of running a fixed time, which both saves energy and reduces over-drying damage.

**What saves more: efficient dryer or line drying?**
Line drying, because it eliminates the energy cost entirely and reduces wear. An efficient dryer only improves the energy term.

**Does using a higher spin speed actually save energy?**
Yes, meaningfully. Spinning water out mechanically costs a small fraction of what evaporating it with heat does, so a faster spin shortens drying time and cuts dryer energy.

**Is it OK to dry laundry indoors in winter?**
Yes, and it's often beneficial — indoor drying adds moisture to air that winter heating has made dry. Just ensure some air circulation to prevent dampness building up in one spot.
