The instinct when bugs appear indoors is to spray. Spraying kills the insects currently visible and does essentially nothing about the ones that will arrive next week, because the insects are coming in from outside through openings you haven't closed.

Perimeter exclusion beats spraying on cost, on effort, and on results. It also has the advantage of working on everything at once — ants, spiders, beetles, crickets, earwigs, and the occasional rodent — rather than requiring a different product per pest.

**What actually drives summer insect invasions:** warmth, moisture, light at night, and food odor. You can't change the season, but you can change all four of the latter.

---

## Step 1: Find the entry points with a flashlight

Do this at dusk with interior lights on. From outside, any gap that shows light is a gap insects can use.

**Check these locations — they account for the large majority of entries:**

1. **The bottom of exterior doors.** The single most common entry, especially for ants, beetles, and crickets. Check the entire width, including the corners.
2. **Door and window frames.** Look at the seams where the frame meets the wall. Old caulk cracks and pulls away.
3. **Utility penetrations.** Where pipes, cables, and AC lines enter the house. These are almost always under-sealed.
4. **Foundation and sill plate.** The joint between foundation and framing is a classic ant highway.
5. **Crawlspace and basement vents.** Vents often have no screening or torn screening. These are the biggest openings in most houses.
6. **Soffit and roof vents.** Easy to miss, and wasps and beetles use them.
7. **Dryer vent and exhaust fans.** The exterior flap should seal when off. If it hangs open, insects walk in.
8. **Garage door seals.** The side and bottom seals harden and shrink.
9. **Window screens.** Check for holes, torn spline, and gaps at the frame edges — not just the mesh.
10. **Chimney.** An uncapped chimney is an open door.

**Also do a moisture and food audit indoors** while you're at it. Insects need water and food. Leaky faucets, damp basements, and unsealed food create the conditions that turn a minor entry problem into an infestation. For food specifically, [How to Organize a Fridge to Waste Less Food](/kitchen-tips/food-storage/organize-fridge-less-food-waste/) covers keeping things sealed and rotated so nothing ferments out of sight.

## Step 2: Seal every gap, from smallest to largest

**Small gaps (up to 1/4 inch) — the majority of entries**

1. **Caulk around window and door frames**, both inside and out. Use silicone for exterior and paintable acrylic for interior.
2. **Caulk utility penetrations** where pipes and cables enter. This is often overlooked entirely.
3. **Replace hardened door sweeps** with new brush or rubber sweeps that actually contact the threshold.
4. **Add weatherstripping to door frames** so the door seals on all four sides, not just the bottom.

**Medium gaps (1/4 to 1 inch)**

1. **Use copper mesh or steel wool packed into the gap**, then cover with caulk or expanding foam. Note: use **copper** mesh, not steel wool, for exterior gaps — steel wool rusts and disintegrates.
2. **Backer rod plus caulk** for wider cracks in masonry or siding.
3. **Screen vents with 1/16-inch mesh.** This blocks most flying and crawling insects while maintaining airflow; smaller mesh clogs with dust rapidly.

**Large openings**

1. **Screen crawlspace and foundation vents.** This is the highest-value single fix if you have unvented crawlspace openings.
2. **Cap the chimney** with a screened cap.
3. **Screen the attic gable vents.**
4. **Fix or replace torn window screens** rather than patching with tape; the patch peels and looks terrible.
5. **Seal the garage-to-house door** as carefully as an exterior door — this is a frequent entry point for mice and large insects.

## Step 3: Change the lighting that attracts them

Many flying insects navigate by moonlight and are disoriented by artificial light, with UV-rich light being the strongest attractant.

1. **Switch exterior lights to warm-color LEDs** (2700K or lower, or specifically "bug light" yellow). Cool white and daylight bulbs are the worst offenders.
2. **Turn off unnecessary exterior lights**, or put them on a motion sensor so they're dark most of the night.
3. **Move lights away from doors.** If the light is beside the door, insects gather there; if it's on a post away from the house, they gather there instead.
4. **Direct light downward** with shielded fixtures. Light spilling upward and sideways pulls far more insects in.
5. **Keep interior light from leaking out** around door frames and through uncovered windows without screens.

## Step 4: Remove the water and food sources outside

Exclusion stops entry; source reduction reduces the population trying to get in.

1. **Eliminate standing water.** Clogged gutters, plant saucers, tarps, buckets, and wheelbarrows. Mosquitoes need roughly a week of standing water to complete a generation.
2. **Fix leaking exterior faucets and AC condensate drips.** Constant moisture attracts ants, cockroaches, and earwigs.
3. **Move firewood and lumber away from the foundation** and elevate it. Woodpiles against the house are prime termite, spider, and beetle habitat.
4. **Keep mulch from touching the siding.** A mulch bed against the foundation bridges the gap insects use.
5. **Trim vegetation back from the house.** Shrubs touching siding act as a ladder.
6. **Manage trash:** sealed bins, emptied frequently, rinsed. Fermenting waste is a powerful attractant. Odor issues transfer indoors too — see [How to Remove Kitchen Odors](/kitchen-tips/cooking-hacks/remove-kitchen-odors/).
7. **Clean up fallen fruit immediately** if you have fruit trees. This is a major attractant most people don't connect to their indoor bug problem.

## Step 5: Use barriers where exclusion isn't possible

Some entry points can't be fully sealed.

1. **Diatomaceous earth (food grade)** along sills, thresholds, and baseboards. It damages the waxy outer layer of insects that walk through it. Keep it dry — it's ineffective once wet.
2. **Copper tape or mesh** at known ant trails. Ants avoid copper.
3. **Sticky traps in garages, basements, and along sills.** These are monitoring tools as much as control: they show you which direction insects are entering from.
4. **Perimeter spray** outside, focused on the foundation line, door thresholds, and window frames. Apply to the exterior border only — indoor baseboard spraying is largely ineffective and unnecessary.
5. **Peppermint or citrus sprays** do deter some insects as a short-term barrier, but the effect fades in hours. Useful, not a solution.

## Step 6: Handle the pest-specific cases

**Ants**

- Follow the trail to find the entry. Ant trails are almost always along a physical edge — foundation, pipe, or cable.
- Don't spray the trail indoors; it splits the colony and scatters them. Use bait stations instead so foragers carry it back.
- A line of diatomaceous earth across the entry works and doesn't split the colony.

**Fruit flies**

- Find the source. It's almost never "just the kitchen" — it's a forgotten potato, a recycling bin, a drain.
- Cover and refrigerate produce; empty recycling daily; clean the drain with a brush, not just boiling water.

**House flies**

- Entry via open doors and windows, mostly. Screening plus a door-closing habit is the fix; sprays don't help.
- Manage outdoor sources: sealed trash, cleaned bins, removed pet waste.

**Mosquitoes**

- Indoors, they come through open doors and unscreened windows. Fix the openings.
- Outdoors, remove standing water — that's the entire strategy, since they breed in water volumes as small as a bottle cap.

**Spiders**

- Spiders follow their prey. Remove the other insects and spiders decline on their own.
- Reduce clutter and cardboard in garages and basements, which is where they establish.

**Wasps and hornets**

- Find and treat the nest at dusk, when they're least active. Never approach a nest in daylight.
- Seal soffit and vent gaps so they can't nest in the structure.
- For ground nests or large nests near doors, use a professional — the risk isn't worth it.

## Step 7: Verify and maintain

Exclusion is a repair, and repairs need re-checking.

- **Re-check your sealing in late spring and again in midsummer.** Caulk shrinks; weatherstripping compresses.
- **Check sweeps and threshold seals annually.** They're the highest-wear exclusion materials.
- **Keep a caulk gun and a tube of caulk on hand.** Half of exclusion work is just doing it promptly when you spot a gap.
- **If you have repeated ant or roach activity despite sealing,** look for a moisture problem. Persistent damp attracts and supports them regardless of exclusion.
- **For termites or bed bugs, call a professional.** Neither responds to DIY methods, and both get worse with delay.

## Why This Works

Insect entry is a size problem, not a behavior problem. Most household insects need only a gap roughly 1/16 to 1/8 inch to pass, and ants need considerably less. Caulking and weatherstripping work because they reduce the entry cross-section below what the insects can use, and they do it permanently rather than for a few hours as a spray does.

Sealing also addresses the reason sprays underperform. A residual insecticide applied indoors kills insects that cross the treated surface. That reduces visible activity but does nothing to the flow of new insects arriving, so the population indoors is continuously replenished. Exclusion breaks the flow.

The lighting piece is a navigation mechanism rather than a preference. Many night-flying insects orient using light sources, and artificial light disrupts that. Warm, low-UV, downward-shielded light attracts far fewer insects because it mimics less of the spectrum they evolved to navigate by. Moving the light source away from the door simply relocates the gathering point.

Water sources matter because most insects need water far more urgently than food, and many species complete their larval stage in standing water. Removing a single clogged gutter can eliminate an entire mosquito population cycle. This is why source reduction outperforms control attempts.

Diatomaceous earth works mechanically rather than chemically. Its microscopic abrasive structure damages the waxy cuticle that prevents insects from losing water. Insects don't develop resistance to it the way they do to chemical insecticides, and it has no toxicity to mammals — which is why it remains useful where sprays have stopped working.

The reason baiting beats spraying for ants is colony structure. Indoor contact sprays kill the foragers you see, and the colony responds by splitting into multiple satellite colonies — a well-documented outcome that makes the problem worse. Bait works because foragers carry it back and distribute it through the colony's food-sharing behavior.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Spraying insects indoors as the primary fix | Kills visible ones; new ones keep arriving | Seal entry points first |
| Spraying ant trails | Splits the colony and spreads it | Bait stations or diatomaceous earth |
| Using steel wool in exterior gaps | Rusts and disintegrates, reopening the gap | Copper mesh |
| Leaving cool-white exterior lights on all night | Cool/UV-heavy light attracts the most insects | Warm LEDs, shielded, on motion sensors |
| Screening vents with very fine mesh | Clogs with dust and blocks airflow | 1/16-inch mesh, checked periodically |
| Leaving woodpiles against the house | Prime habitat for termites, spiders, beetles | Move 20+ feet away and elevate |
| Mulch piled against the siding | Bridges the gap insects use to enter | Keep 6 inches of clearance |
| Ignoring standing water | Breeds mosquitoes in a week | Dump anything that holds water weekly |
| Using outdoor-only solutions for indoor pests | Doesn't close the actual entry | Find and seal the entry point |
| DIY for termites or bed bugs | Both need professional treatment and worsen with delay | Call a licensed professional |
| Treating a persistent problem as seasonal | Usually indicates a moisture or food source | Find and fix the underlying source |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the most effective way to keep bugs out of the house?**
Sealing entry points. Caulk window and door frames, replace door sweeps, seal utility penetrations, and screen vents. Exclusion is more effective and longer-lasting than any spray.

**Why do I have so many bugs in my house this summer?**
Usually unsealed entry points combined with a nearby attractant — damp woodpiles, mulch against the foundation, standing water, or exterior lights left on. Growing vegetation close to the house is also a common factor.

**Does spraying indoors actually work?**
It reduces visible activity temporarily but doesn't stop new insects entering. Outdoor perimeter treatment focused on the foundation line and thresholds is more effective, combined with sealing.

**What can I use to keep bugs out naturally?**
Diatomaceous earth along sills and thresholds, copper mesh in gaps, and peppermint or citrus spray as a short-lived barrier. Caulking and screening are the genuinely effective natural options.

**How do I stop ants coming in without poison?**
Find the entry point and seal it. Place bait stations near the trail so foragers carry it back to the colony, and use diatomaceous earth as a barrier. Avoid sprays, which split the colony.

**Why are bugs attracted to my porch light?**
Many night-flying insects navigate by light, and cool-white or UV-rich bulbs disrupt that most strongly. Switch to warm LEDs, shield the fixture so light points down, and use a motion sensor.

**Do bug zappers help?**
They kill large numbers of harmless and beneficial insects while catching relatively few mosquitoes. They're more likely to attract insects to the area than to reduce the population.

**How do I keep mosquitoes from breeding near my house?**
Eliminate standing water weekly — gutters, plant saucers, buckets, tarps, and low spots. Mosquitoes complete a generation in about a week in small water volumes, so weekly emptying is the key.
