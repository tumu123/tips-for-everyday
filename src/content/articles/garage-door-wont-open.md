**A garage door is the largest moving object in most houses, and it is the only one that routinely injures the person repairing it.** That fact should shape the whole approach: diagnose freely, but stop at a specific line.

The line is the torsion spring. Everything else — sensors, remotes, wall switches, locks, tracks, rollers — is ordinary household repair. A torsion spring under tension stores enough energy to break bones and has killed people who tried to adjust it. It is not a matter of being careful; it is a matter of not doing it.

With that established, the diagnosis itself is straightforward, and most failures resolve without tools.

---

## Step 1: Does the Opener Run?

Listen. This one question splits the diagnosis in half.

| What you hear | Meaning | Go to |
|---|---|---|
| **Motor runs, door doesn't move** | Mechanical disconnect or broken spring | Step 3 |
| **Motor runs, door moves a few inches and stops** | Safety sensor or force limit | Step 2 |
| **Motor runs, door moves a little then reverses** | Safety sensor obstruction | Step 2 |
| **No sound at all** | Power, remote, wall switch, or logic board | Step 4 |
| **Click, then nothing** | Logic board or capacitor | Step 4 |
| **Grinding or rattling from the opener** | Gear or chain/screw drive failure | Step 5 |
| **Loud bang from above the door, then nothing** | Broken torsion spring | **Stop — Step 6** |

**The loud bang is the important one.** A torsion spring failing sounds like a gunshot from inside the garage. If you heard that, and the door is now extremely heavy or won't move with the opener, do not attempt to operate it. The spring is the counterweight; without it the door's full weight — often 150–250 pounds — is unsupported, and the opener is not designed to lift it.

## Step 2: The Safety Sensors — Half of All "Won't Close" Calls

Two small sensors sit at the bottom of the tracks, about six inches above the floor, one on each side. One emits an infrared beam, the other receives it. If the beam is broken, the door will not close — and on many openers, a persistent fault will also prevent opening.

**What to check, in order:**

1. **The indicator lights.** Most sensors have an LED. A steady light on both means the beam is aligned. A flashing or dark LED on the receiving unit means misalignment or a fault.
2. **Obstruction.** A broom, a bike, a bag of mulch, a spider web, or a cobweb across the lens. Clean the lenses with a dry cloth.
3. **Alignment.** The two sensors should point at each other. A sensor bumped by a car or a lawnmower goes out of alignment easily. Loosen the wing nut, aim, and tighten. A laser level or a taut string helps.
4. **Sunlight.** Direct low sun into the receiving sensor confuses it. This is a real cause of doors that work all day and fail at sunset. A small shield or a different sensor brand fixes it.
5. **Wiring.** The low-voltage wires run along the track and get damaged by ladders, stored items, and rodents. Look for a nick or a chewed section.
6. **The sensor itself.** A failed sensor is $20–$40 and replaceable as a pair.

**The sensor LED is diagnostic gold.** If the receiving light is off, the door will not close no matter what else you do. Fix that before anything else.

## Step 3: Motor Runs, Door Doesn't Move

If the opener is working but the door isn't moving, the connection between them has failed.

**Check the emergency release cord.** Every opener has a red handle hanging from the trolley. It disconnects the door from the opener. If someone pulled it — during a power outage, or by accident — the motor runs and the door stays put. Pull it again (or move the trolley back into engagement) and the door reconnects.

**On a screw-drive or belt-drive opener,** pull the cord and then run the opener until the trolley re-engages, or manually move the door until the trolley clicks in.

**If the release is engaged and the door still doesn't move with the opener,** disconnect the door by hand and try lifting it manually:

- **Door lifts easily by hand** → the door and spring are fine, and the opener's drive is the problem.
- **Door is extremely heavy, or lifts a few inches then falls** → broken spring, or a door that's come off its track. Stop and go to Step 6.

**The manual lift test is the single most useful check on a garage door** and it requires no tools. It tells you whether you have a door problem or an opener problem, which is the divide that determines everything downstream.

## Step 4: No Sound At All

Dead silence from the opener is an electrical problem, and the checks are ordinary.

**The wall switch.** This is the most common and most overlooked cause. The wall console is wired to the opener, and it also supplies the lock/vacation function on many models. If the wall switch doesn't work either, the fault is upstream of the radio.

**The lock/vacation button.** Many wall consoles have a lock mode that disables the remotes while still allowing the wall button to work. If the remotes are dead but the wall button works, check this first — it's a button, not a fault.

**The breaker.** The opener is usually on a dedicated 15-amp circuit in the garage, often shared with the garage outlets. [Resetting a tripped breaker](/fix-it/home-repair/reset-tripped-circuit-breaker/) is the check, and a GFCI outlet upstream of the opener is a frequent culprit — one tripped GFCI kills the whole circuit.

**The remote's battery.** Obvious, frequently skipped. Also re-check the remote after a battery change: some remotes need to be re-paired, and the pairing procedure is model-specific.

**The logic board.** If power reaches the opener and the wall switch does nothing, the board or its transformer has failed. Boards are $80–$200 depending on the model, and on an opener past 15 years old, replacement is usually the better answer — modern openers are quieter, and they have battery backup and better safety features.

| No-sound cause | Test | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Wall console lock mode | Try the wall button | Press lock again |
| Tripped breaker or GFCI | Check the panel and the outlet | Reset |
| Dead remote battery | Try the wall switch | New battery, re-pair |
| Remote unpaired | Wall switch works, remote doesn't | Re-pair per manual |
| Failed logic board | Power in, wall switch dead | Replace board or opener |
| Failed capacitor (older units) | Motor hums but doesn't start | Replace capacitor |

## Step 5: Grinding, Rattling, or a Door That Stops Partway

**Grinding from the opener head** usually means the drive gear has stripped. On Chamberlain/LiftMaster-style openers, the nylon gear wears out after years of use — it's a designed wear item. A gear and sprocket kit is $25–$40 and a satisfying DIY repair, though it requires removing the opener from the ceiling.

**Rattling and binding** point at the door's mechanicals rather than the opener:

- **Rollers** — worn or seized rollers make the door drag and jump. $5 each, and replacing all of them transforms a noisy door.
- **Hinges** — bent or loose hinges shift the door's panels out of alignment.
- **Tracks** — a track that's been hit (by a car, a ladder, a bike) pinches the rollers. Tracks can sometimes be straightened; a badly bent one should be replaced.
- **Cables** — frayed cables are a **stop-and-call** item. Like springs, they're under tension. A snapped cable whips and can cause serious injury.
- **The door out of balance** — a door that lifts halfway and stops, or that drifts down when released halfway, is out of balance. That's a spring tension issue, which means professional.

**The balance test:** disconnect the opener, lift the door halfway by hand, and let go. A properly balanced door stays where you put it. One that falls is under-tensioned; one that rises is over-tensioned. Both adjustments are spring adjustments — see Step 6.

## Step 6: The Torsion Spring — Where You Stop

**Do not adjust, remove, or replace a torsion spring.** This is not a caution about carelessness. A torsion spring on a standard seven-foot door holds several hundred inch-pounds of torque, and the winding bars used to adjust it are held by hand. If the bar slips or the spring lets go, the energy releases into whatever is in its path.

The same applies to **extension springs** — the older style that runs alongside the tracks. They're slightly less dangerous to adjust but store comparable energy, and a snapped extension spring can travel across the garage. Extension springs should have a safety cable running through them; if yours don't, that's a cheap professional addition worth making.

**What you can safely do:**

- **Look at the spring.** A visible gap in the coil means it's broken.
- **Look for a gap of about two inches** between the coils at rest — that's normal.
- **Note the colour code** on the spring (painted on one end) if you need to order a replacement — but let a technician install it.
- **Keep the door closed and stop using the opener.** Running the opener against a broken spring burns out the motor and can drop the door.

**What a spring replacement costs:** typically $150–$350 for a pair, including labour, depending on the market. A technician will also inspect cables, rollers, and the balance. That price is the cheapest part of this entire article if it prevents one injury.

## Step 7: Power Outages and the Manual Release

If the door won't open because there's no power, the release cord is the answer — but there's a right way to use it.

**Pull the release cord, then lift the door by hand.** It will be heavy. Lift from the bottom centre with both hands, and once it's up, the door should stay up on its own if it's balanced.

**Re-engage by running the opener** once power returns. The trolley will reconnect on the next cycle.

**Two things that catch people out:**

- **A door with a broken spring cannot be opened by hand.** If you pull the release and the door is immovable, stop — this is a spring failure, not a power failure.
- **A door left disengaged can be opened from outside** by anyone who can reach the release. If you're going away, re-engage it.

[Preparing for a power outage](/seasonal/seasons/prepare-for-power-outage-home/) covers the wider checklist, including the garage door's specific role as a security point.

## Why This Works

**The opener lifts with a fraction of the door's weight because the spring carries the rest.** A torsion spring is wound so that its stored energy roughly equals the door's weight at every point in travel. The opener only supplies the difference — typically 10–15 pounds of force. That's why a broken spring makes the door feel impossibly heavy: the counterbalance is gone, and the opener's gear train is not built for 200 pounds.

**The safety sensors are a photocell interlock, and an interlock is binary.** The beam is either made or broken. There is no partial state, which is why a spider web across the lens produces total failure rather than degraded operation. This also makes the LEDs unusually informative: they report the interlock state directly.

**Force limits are what stop the door on obstruction.** Openers measure current draw or use an encoder to detect resistance. When the door meets something — or when it binds in a misaligned track — the force exceeds the limit and the opener reverses. A door that reverses for no visible reason is usually binding, not detecting a phantom obstruction.

**Spring energy is stored, not consumed.** Unlike a motor, a wound spring doesn't discharge gradually — it holds everything until it fails. That is precisely what makes it dangerous to work on and why the professional cost is worth paying.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | What happens | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Adjusting a torsion spring yourself | Serious injury or death | Call a garage door technician |
| Running the opener with a broken spring | Burns out the motor; door may fall | Leave it closed |
| Cutting or removing a cable | Cable whips at high speed | Professional only |
| Bypassing the safety sensors | Door closes on people or pets | Fix or replace the sensors |
| Standing under a moving door | Crush hazard if it fails | Stand clear |
| Putting fingers between door sections | Amputation risk at the hinges | Keep hands on the bottom edge |
| Forcing a binding door with the opener | Strips the gear; damages panels | Find the bind first |
| Replacing a remote without re-pairing | Still doesn't work | Follow the pairing procedure |
| Ignoring frayed cables | Snap without warning | Replace immediately, professionally |
| Using the emergency release while the door is up | Door drops | Pull the release only when the door is down |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**My opener runs but the door doesn't move. What's most likely?**
The emergency release has been pulled, disconnecting the door from the trolley. Check the red handle first.

**How do I know if my garage door spring is broken?**
A gap in the coil, and often a loud bang when it failed. The door becomes extremely heavy and the opener can't lift it. Stop using it.

**Can I replace a garage door spring myself?**
No. This is the one repair in this guide that genuinely should never be DIY — the stored energy can kill.

**Why does my door close partway and go back up?**
The safety sensors are obstructed, misaligned, or faulty. Check the LED on each sensor.

**Why does my door only fail at sunset?**
Sunlight shining into the receiving sensor. Shield it or reposition it slightly.

**What does it cost to fix a garage door?**
Springs $150–$350 installed; sensors $100–$200 installed or $20–$40 for the parts; a stripped gear $40 for the kit if you do it yourself. A full opener replacement runs $400–$800 installed.

**Do I need to lubricate the door?**
Yes — the rollers, hinges, and tracks, twice a year, with a silicone or lithium grease. Not WD-40, which attracts dust and evaporates.

**How long does a garage door opener last?**
10–15 years. Springs last roughly 10,000 cycles — about 7–10 years for a typical household.

## Bottom Line

Diagnose in this order: does the motor run, does the door move freely by hand, are the sensors aligned. Those three questions cover most failures and none of them needs a tool beyond a flashlight.

Then stop at the spring. A broken spring, a frayed cable, or a door that's badly out of balance is professional work — not because it's difficult, but because the stored energy has no margin for error. Everything else on the door is ordinary hardware you can replace on a Saturday.
