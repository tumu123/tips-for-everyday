Water bills rise for three reasons: **a leak you cannot see**, **fixtures that use more water than necessary**, and **habits**. The order matters — a single silent toilet leak can waste more water in a month than replacing every fixture in the house.

**The highest-value actions, ranked:**

1. **Find and fix leaks** (a running toilet can waste 200 gallons per day)
2. **Check your meter with everything off** (five minutes, reveals all leaks)
3. **Fix the toilet flapper** (often a $5 part, saves $50–100/year)
4. **Install low-flow showerheads and aerators** (low cost, permanent savings)
5. **Change watering and cleaning habits**

**Important caveat on savings figures:** Water rates vary dramatically by region — from roughly $2 to $10 per 1,000 gallons. The same fix produces very different dollar savings depending on your location. Gallons-saved figures are more transferable than dollar figures.

## Step 1: Read Your Meter and Detect Leaks (Do This First)

This is a five-minute test that reveals whether you have a leak at all. Fixing leaks is almost always more valuable than any fixture upgrade.

1. **Turn off every water-using appliance and fixture** — faucets, dishwasher, washing machine, ice maker, irrigation system.
2. **Find your water meter** (usually in the basement, utility closet, or an outdoor box).
3. **Read and record the number on the meter**, including the small dial that shows fine flow.
4. **Wait 30–60 minutes without using any water.**
5. **Read the meter again.**

**Interpretation:**

| Result | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Meter unchanged | No leaks | Move to fixture upgrades |
| Meter moved slightly | Small leak (often a toilet flapper) | Check toilets first |
| Meter moved significantly | Major leak | Check toilets, then irrigation, then supply lines |
| Low-flow indicator spinning | Active leak somewhere | Systematic search needed |

**Likely culprits, in order of probability:**

1. **Toilet flapper** — the most common household leak by far
2. **Irrigation system** — broken sprinkler heads, leaking valves
3. **Outdoor spigots** — worn washers
4. **Water heater relief valve** — dripping discharge
5. **Supply lines under sinks** — slow drips
6. **Pool or spa** — autofill running constantly

## Room 1: The Bathroom (Highest Impact)

Bathrooms account for roughly 60% of indoor water use in a typical home.

### Toilet

The toilet is the single largest water user in most homes, and the most common source of leaks.

**Fix a running toilet (highest priority):**

A running toilet — one where water continues to flow after the flush — can waste 200 gallons per day. That is roughly 6,000 gallons per month.

1. **Identify the type of run.** Remove the tank lid and look.
   - **Water flowing into the bowl** → flapper is not sealing
   - **Water flowing into the overflow tube** → fill valve is set too high
2. **For a flapper problem:** Turn off the water supply, flush to empty the tank, unhook the flapper chain, and inspect the flapper. If it is warped, stiff, or coated in mineral deposits, replace it. Flappers cost a few dollars.
3. **For a fill valve problem:** Adjust the float so the water level sits about an inch below the overflow tube top. If adjustment does not hold, replace the fill valve.
4. **Check the chain length.** A chain that is too long catches under the flapper; too short holds it open.
5. **Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank**, wait 15 minutes without flushing, and check the bowl. Color in the bowl confirms the flapper is leaking.

**Reduce water per flush:**

- **Adjust the fill level down** slightly if your toilet allows it.
- **Place a filled water bottle in the tank** to displace volume — but only for older, high-volume toilets. Do not do this in modern low-flow toilets, where it can reduce flush effectiveness and cause double flushing, which wastes more water than it saves.
- **Do not use bricks** — they degrade and clog the tank.

### Shower

Showers account for roughly 17% of indoor water use.

**Low-flow showerhead:**

1. Standard showerheads use 2.5 gallons per minute (GPM). Low-flow models use 1.5–2.0 GPM.
2. **A family of four showering 10 minutes daily** uses roughly 36,500 gallons per year at 2.5 GPM. At 1.8 GPM, that drops to about 26,000 gallons — a 29% reduction.
3. Modern low-flow showerheads use pressure-compensating designs, so the difference is far less noticeable than older models.
4. Cost: typically $20–50. Pays back within months at most water rates.

**Shower habits:**

- **Shorten showers by 2 minutes** — saves roughly 5 gallons per person per shower
- **Turn water off while lathering** — saves 3–5 gallons per shower
- **Fix dripping shower valves** — a dripping shower can waste 10+ gallons daily

### Sink and Faucet

**Aerator:**

1. Screw an aerator onto the faucet (usually included with newer faucets, or available for a few dollars).
2. Aerators mix air with water, reducing flow from ~2.2 GPM to 1.0–1.5 GPM without a noticeable difference in function.
3. Standard aerators cost $3–8.

**Habits:**
- **Turn off the tap while brushing teeth** — saves 2–4 gallons per brushing
- **Fill the sink to wash hands or shave** rather than running the tap
- **Fix dripping faucets promptly** — a slow drip wastes roughly 3,000 gallons per year

Most tanks ship at 140°F, and 120°F is safe for an adult household — [setting the water heater temperature](/save-money/bills/water-heater-temperature-setting-savings/) covers the trade-offs.

## Room 2: The Kitchen

### Dishwasher

**The counterintuitive fact:** A full dishwasher load uses **less water than hand-washing the same dishes**. Hand-washing with the tap running can use 15–20 gallons; a dishwasher uses 3–5 gallons per cycle on modern models.

1. **Run only full loads.**
2. **Skip pre-rinsing** — scrape dishes instead. Pre-rinsing under a running tap can waste more water than the entire dishwasher cycle.
3. **Use the eco or light cycle** when dishes are not heavily soiled.
4. **Fix leaks around the door gasket.**

**If hand-washing:** Fill one basin with soapy water and one with rinse water rather than running the tap. This alone cuts hand-washing water use by half or more.

### Refrigerator Ice Maker and Water Dispenser

1. **Check for leaks** in the supply line — a common and invisible source of waste.
2. **Do not run the dispenser longer than needed.**
3. A leaking ice maker supply line is a common cause of unexpectedly high water bills.

The answer depends on how you heat water and how you run the tap — [whether the dishwasher or hand washing costs less](/save-money/bills/dishwasher-vs-hand-washing-cost/) does the arithmetic.

## Room 3: The Laundry

1. **Run full loads.** A washing machine uses roughly the same water per cycle regardless of load size.
2. **Adjust the water level setting** to match the load. Many machines default to the highest level.
3. **Front-loaders use roughly 40–50% less water than top-loaders.** If your machine is due for replacement, this is a significant factor.
4. **Wash in cold water** — saves energy (not water directly) and is gentler on fabrics.
5. **Reuse towels** — washing towels after every use is unnecessary for most people and adds several loads weekly.
6. **Fix supply hose leaks.** Check the hoses behind the machine for drips.

## Room 4: Outdoors (The Largest Potential Savings)

Outdoor water use can exceed all indoor use combined in warm climates. It is also where waste is most common and often invisible.

### Irrigation

1. **Water in the early morning** (before 9 a.m.). Midday watering loses a large fraction to evaporation; evening watering promotes fungal disease.
2. **Check for broken sprinkler heads** monthly. A broken head can waste hundreds of gallons per day. Look for soggy spots, unusually green patches, or water running down the driveway.
3. **Adjust the controller seasonally.** Most systems are set once and never changed, so they overwater in cool months.
4. **Install a rain sensor** — inexpensive and prevents watering during or after rain.
5. **Add a smart controller** if your system supports it. These adjust based on weather and typically reduce outdoor water use 15–30%.
6. **Water deeply and less often** rather than shallow and frequent. Deep watering encourages deeper roots and reduces total water needed.
7. **Mulch garden beds** to reduce evaporation from soil.

### Outdoor Spigots and Hose

1. **Check for leaks at the spigot** — a worn washer causes a constant drip.
2. **Use a shutoff nozzle** on the hose rather than letting water run.
3. **Fix or replace a leaking hose** — a leaking hose left running can waste a substantial amount before anyone notices.
4. **Drain and store hoses in winter** to prevent freeze damage that causes spring leaks.

### Pool / Spa (If Applicable)

1. **Use a pool cover** — reduces evaporation significantly, which is the largest water loss for most pools.
2. **Check for leaks** in the pump and plumbing.
3. **Fix an autofill that runs constantly** — this is a common hidden cause of high water bills in homes with pools.

### Car Washing

1. **Use a bucket and sponge** rather than a running hose. A running hose uses roughly 10 gallons per minute.
2. **Use a commercial car wash** if water efficiency matters — many recycle water. Counterintuitive but often correct.

## Step: Compare Against Your Baseline

To know what is normal in your household, calculate your usage:

1. **Find your water bill** and locate the usage figure (usually in gallons or cubic feet per billing period).
2. **Divide by the number of days** in the billing period and the number of people in the household.
3. **Typical indoor usage is 40–60 gallons per person per day.** Below that is efficient; above 80 usually indicates a leak or inefficient fixtures.

**If your usage is significantly above 80 gallons per person per day**, prioritize the leak search in Step 1 before anything else.

## What NOT to Do

| Mistake | Why it's a problem |
|---|---|
| Putting a brick in the toilet tank | Degrades and clogs the tank. Use a sealed water bottle if anything. |
| Displacing water in a modern low-flow toilet | Reduces flush effectiveness, causing double flushing that wastes more |
| Pre-rinsing dishes before the dishwasher | Can waste more water than the whole cycle |
| Watering in midday | Loses a large fraction to evaporation |
| Setting irrigation once and never adjusting | Overwaters in cool months |
| Running the hose while washing a car | Uses roughly 10 gallons per minute |
| Ignoring a small drip | Small drips add up to thousands of gallons annually |
| Assuming a higher water bill is just rate increases | Rates do rise, but a sudden increase usually means a leak |

## FAQ

**What uses the most water in a home?**
Toilets and showers, followed by outdoor irrigation. In warm climates, outdoor watering can exceed all indoor use combined. Leaks anywhere in the system can exceed all normal usage if unaddressed.

**How do I know if I have a water leak?**
Read your water meter, wait 30–60 minutes without using any water, and read it again. If the meter moved, you have a leak. Check the toilet flapper first — it is the most common source by a wide margin.

**How much water does a running toilet waste?**
A continuously running toilet can waste up to 200 gallons per day, or roughly 6,000 gallons per month. This is typically the single largest water waste in a home and the cheapest to fix — a replacement flapper costs a few dollars.

**Do low-flow showerheads really save water?**
Yes. Standard showerheads use 2.5 GPM; low-flow models use 1.5–2.0 GPM. A family of four showering 10 minutes daily can save roughly 10,000 gallons per year by switching. Modern pressure-compensating models feel far less weak than older low-flow designs.

**Is it cheaper to wash dishes by hand or use a dishwasher?**
A full dishwasher load uses less water than hand-washing the same dishes — typically 3–5 gallons versus 15–20. But only if you run full loads and do not pre-rinse. Hand-washing with two basins of water can also be efficient.

**Why did my water bill suddenly increase?**
The most common causes are a running toilet, a leaking irrigation head, or a leaking pool autofill. Rate increases are also possible but usually produce gradual rather than sudden changes. Run the meter test to confirm a leak before assuming it is a rate change.

**How often should I water my lawn?**
Deeply and infrequently — typically once or twice a week in most climates, in the early morning. This encourages deeper roots and uses less total water than frequent shallow watering. Adjust seasonally; most irrigation controllers are set once and then overwater for the rest of the year.

**Do water-saving devices actually pay back?**
Yes for the inexpensive ones. Faucet aerators and low-flow showerheads typically pay back in one to three months at most water rates. Toilet flapper repairs pay back in days. The larger investments, like smart irrigation controllers, pay back in one to three years depending on outdoor water use.
