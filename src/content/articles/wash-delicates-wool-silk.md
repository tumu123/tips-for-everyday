Delicates are damaged by four things: **heat, agitation, alkalinity, and hanging wet.** Avoid all four and wool, silk, cashmere, and lace can be washed at home safely.

Most "dry clean only" labels are conservative rather than absolute. Silk and wool are protein fibers — the same family as human hair — and they respond well to the same treatment: cool water, a gentle pH-neutral detergent, minimal agitation, and flat drying.

**What damages protein fibers:**

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Heat** | Causes shrinkage and felting in wool; dulls silk's sheen |
| **Agitation** | Wool fibers interlock and felt permanently |
| **Alkaline detergent** | Degrades protein fibers; strips silk's natural finish |
| **Chlorine bleach** | Destroys wool and silk outright |
| **Hanging wet** | Stretches garments out of shape under their own weight |
| **Tumble drying** | Shrinks wool and damages silk |

## Step 0: Read the label, then test

**Labels to heed:**

- **"Dry clean only"** — often conservative; hand-washing is frequently possible for silk and wool, but there is risk. If the garment is valuable, err toward professional cleaning.
- **"Dry clean"** — a stronger signal. Some garments have linings, structured shoulders, or delicate trim that will not survive washing.
- **"Hand wash"** — wash by hand, not in a machine.
- **"Machine wash cold, gentle"** — machine-washable with care.

**Always do a colorfastness test:**

1. Find a hidden area — an inner seam, a hem, or the inside of a cuff.
2. Dab with a damp white cloth.
3. Check whether any dye transfers to the cloth.
4. If dye transfers, do not soak. Spot-clean or use professional cleaning.

**For embellished or structured garments** — beading, sequins, shoulder pads, linings, embroidery — hand-washing risks damage to the structure rather than the fabric. These are reasonable candidates for professional cleaning.

## Step 1: Hand-washing (the safest method)

Hand-washing is the default for wool, silk, cashmere, and lace. It takes about fifteen minutes of active time.

### Setup

1. **Fill a basin or clean sink with cool water.** Around 85°F (30°C) or less. Cool, not cold, because very cold water makes detergent less effective. Never warm or hot.
2. **Add a gentle detergent.** Use a wool-specific or pH-neutral formula. A small amount is enough — about a teaspoon for a basin.
3. **Mix the detergent in before adding the garment.** Undissolved detergent can spot wool and silk.

### Washing

4. **Submerge the garment** and gently press it down so it absorbs water. Do not wring, twist, or scrub.
5. **Soak for 10–15 minutes.** Wool and silk release soil easily. Longer soaking is not better, and prolonged soaking can damage some dyes.
6. **Gently squeeze the water through the fabric** with flat hands — a pressing motion, not kneading. This is the only agitation protein fibers tolerate.
7. **For stained areas**, dab with a fingertip and a little detergent. Do not rub.

### Rinsing

8. **Drain the basin and refill with cool water.**
9. **Press the garment gently** to work the rinse water through. Repeat until the water runs clear.
10. **For silk**, add a tablespoon of white vinegar to the final rinse water. Silk's natural finish responds to a slightly acidic rinse, and the vinegar also helps remove detergent residue.
11. **Never wring.** Wringing permanently distorts protein fibers.

### Removing water

12. **Lay the garment flat on a clean, dry white towel.**
13. **Roll the towel** with the garment inside, starting from one end.
14. **Press down on the rolled towel** to squeeze out water. The towel absorbs most of it.
15. **Unroll and reshape the garment** to its original dimensions.

This towel-roll method is how you remove water without the mechanical stress of wringing.

Thread count is the least useful number on the label — [buying bedding that lasts](/save-money/shopping/save-money-on-bedding-and-linens/) covers what to check instead.

## Step 2: Machine-washing delicates (if the label allows)

Some delicates are genuinely machine washable. The rules matter more than the machine.

1. **Use a mesh laundry bag.** One bag per garment for wool and silk, so nothing rubs.
2. **Turn the garment inside out.** Protects the surface finish.
3. **Use the delicate or wool cycle.** This cycle reduces agitation speed and uses lower water movement.
4. **Set the temperature to cold or cool.** Never warm or hot.
5. **Use a gentle detergent.** No bleach, no fabric softener, no enzymes for wool and silk — enzymes are designed to break down protein and will attack the fiber itself.
6. **Reduce the spin speed.** High spin distorts shapes. Use the lowest available setting.
7. **Wash delicates alone or with similar items.** Zippers, hooks, and heavy fabrics abrade delicate fibers.
8. **Never crowd the machine.** Delicates need room to move gently.

**Never put these in a machine:**

- Structured garments with shoulder pads or linings
- Beaded or sequined items
- Crocheted or heavily textured lace
- Anything with fragile trim
- Hand-wash-only wool

## Step 3: Dry correctly (this is where most damage happens)

**Never tumble dry wool, silk, or cashmere.** The heat and tumbling cause shrinkage and felting, and the damage is irreversible.

| Fabric | Drying method |
|---|---|
| **Wool sweater** | Lay flat on a towel or drying rack, reshape to original dimensions |
| **Cashmere** | Lay flat, reshape, dry away from direct heat and sunlight |
| **Silk** | Lay flat, or hang on a padded hanger to minimize weight on the shoulders |
| **Lace** | Lay flat on a towel, pin into shape if needed |
| **Knitwear generally** | Lay flat, never hanging |
| **Delicate but structured** | Lay flat, dry in a shape that supports the structure |

**Why lifting and hanging matters:** wet wool is heavy, and hanging it puts the entire weight of the garment on the shoulders, which stretches it out of shape permanently. This is the single most common way a hand-washed sweater gets ruined.

**Reshaping is part of drying.** After washing, lay the garment flat and gently shape it back to its original dimensions — the shoulders, the length, the sleeve width. Sweaters often relax during washing and need to be reshaped while damp.

**Dry away from heat and sun.** Direct sunlight fades dye and weakens silk. Radiators and direct heat cause wool to felt.

**A note on towels and drying racks:** use a mesh drying rack or a clean towel so air circulates under the garment. A garment drying flat against a solid surface stays damp underneath and can develop an odor.

## Step 4: Special situations

### Cashmere

Cashmere is a fine wool and needs more care than ordinary wool.

- **Wash less often.** Cashmere is naturally odor-resistant; air it between wears instead of washing.
- **Use cashmere or wool-specific detergent** — the pH matters more for fine fibers.
- **Optionally, use a fine-gauge mesh bag** and the wool cycle on a machine for small items.
- **Never wring or hang.**
- **Comb out pills** with a cashmere comb rather than pulling them off.
- **Store folded**, not on a hanger, to prevent shoulder stretch.

### Silk

- **Rinse in cool water with a tablespoon of vinegar** to restore the finish.
- **Do not wring or twist.**
- **Iron while slightly damp** on the lowest setting, or use a press cloth. Never iron dry silk directly at high heat.
- **Dry away from sunlight**, which fades silk dye.

### Lace and embroidery

- **Wash by hand only**, in a basin with gentle detergent.
- **Do not scrub**, and do not let anything abrade the lace.
- **Lay flat on a towel** and reshape. Pinning into shape is fine for items that hold a form.
- **Iron with a press cloth** or through a towel, never directly.

### Wool sweaters with pilling

- **Pills are normal**, caused by fiber friction, not by damage.
- **Remove with a fabric shaver or cashmere comb**, not by picking or pulling, which pulls fibers loose.
- **Reduce pilling** by turning the sweater inside out before washing.

## Step 5: Store correctly

- **Fold** sweaters and knits. Never hang them.
- **Store clean.** Body oils attract pests — see our off-season storage guide.
- **Use cedar or lavender**, not mothballs, for pest protection.
- **Avoid plastic bags.** Use breathable cotton bags for natural fibers.
- **Let wool air** between wears rather than washing it every time, which extends both the garment's life and the fiber's.

## Why This Works

Wool and silk are **protein fibers** — chemically similar to human hair. This one fact explains most of the rules.

**Heat and agitation cause felting in wool.** Wool fibers have a scaly surface. Heat, moisture, and agitation make the scales interlock irreversibly, which is why a wool sweater can shrink several sizes and become dense and stiff. All three factors must be avoided together, which is why cool water, no agitation, and flat drying are a package rather than separate tips.

**Alkaline detergent degrades protein.** Protein fibers are sensitive to pH, which is why wool-specific detergents are pH neutral. An enzyme detergent is worse: enzymes are designed to break down protein, so they attack the fiber itself along with any stain.

**Hanging distorts because water is heavy.** Wet wool can weigh several times its dry weight, and hanging concentrates that load at the shoulders. Flat drying supports the garment from below.

**Silk's sheen comes from the fiber's smooth surface.** Heat and alkaline detergent abrade and dull it. An acidic vinegar rinse helps restore the finish because silk's natural condition is slightly acidic.

**Chlorine bleach is fundamentally incompatible.** It oxidizes the protein structure along with any stain, which is why it dissolves or destroys wool and silk. Oxygen-based bleach is gentler, but even that is best avoided on fine protein fibers.

## What NOT to Do

| Common mistake | Why it fails | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Using hot water on wool or silk | Heat causes felting and shrinkage | Cool water, around 85°F or less |
| Using an enzyme detergent on wool | Enzymes break down protein, including the fiber | pH-neutral wool detergent |
| Wringing or twisting out water | Permanently distorts protein fibers | Towel-roll and press |
| Hanging a wet sweater | Its own weight stretches the shoulders | Lay flat and reshape |
| Tumble drying wool or cashmere | Irreversible shrinkage and felting | Lay flat away from heat |
| Using chlorine bleach | Destroys wool and silk | Oxygen bleach if needed, or skip |
| Using fabric softener | Coats fibers, reduces breathability | Skip it; use vinegar in the rinse |
| Rubbing a stain on silk | Abrades the fiber and spreads the stain | Dab gently with a fingertip |
| Ironing silk at high heat | Scorches and dulls the sheen | Low heat, press cloth, slightly damp |
| Machine-washing structured garments | Linings and padding distort | Professional cleaning |
| Storing sweaters on hangers | Stretches shoulders permanently | Fold |
| Washing cashmere as often as cotton | Accelerates wear on fine fibers | Air between wears; wash rarely |
| Picking at pills | Pulls fibers loose and creates holes | Fabric shaver or cashmere comb |
| Drying in direct sunlight | Fades dye and weakens silk | Dry in shade |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I wash "dry clean only" silk and wool at home?**
Often yes, especially for silk and wool garments without structure or lining. Use cool water and a pH-neutral detergent by hand. However, garments with linings, shoulder pads, beading, or fragile trim are genuinely better professionally cleaned. If the item is valuable, the risk of a home wash may not be worth it.

**What detergent should I use for wool and silk?**
A pH-neutral, wool-specific or "gentle" detergent. Avoid enzyme detergents, because enzymes break down protein and will attack the fiber. Avoid alkaline detergents and any product containing chlorine bleach.

**Why does wool shrink in the washing machine?**
Heat, moisture, and agitation cause the wool fiber's scales to interlock irreversibly — a process called felting. It cannot be reversed. Cool water, minimal agitation, and flat drying are all required to prevent it.

**How do I dry a wool sweater without stretching it?**
Lay it flat on a towel or mesh drying rack and reshape it to its original dimensions while damp. Never hang it, because wet wool is heavy and hanging stretches the shoulders permanently.

**How often should I wash cashmere?**
Less often than you think. Cashmere is naturally odor-resistant, so airing between wears is often enough. Wash when it is genuinely soiled, by hand or on a wool cycle in a mesh bag, and always lay flat to dry.

**Can I use vinegar on silk?**
Yes, as a final rinse. About a tablespoon of white vinegar in the rinse water helps remove detergent residue and restores silk's slightly acidic natural finish. Do not combine it with chlorine bleach.

**What do I do about pilling on wool?**
Remove pills with a fabric shaver or cashmere comb. Do not pull or pick them, which loosens fibers and creates holes. Turning the garment inside out before washing reduces new pilling.

**Is it safe to hand-wash a beaded garment?**
Not generally. The beads, thread, and any lining are usually the weak point rather than the fabric. Hand-washing risks loosening beading or distorting the structure. These are reasonable to have professionally cleaned.
